Abstract
Therapeutic successes in mouse models of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) largely fail to translate into clinical trials, with 
experimental drugs rarely validated in human models before being administered to humans. To address this, 
we developed an accessible method to culture commercially available primary human neurons and astrocytes, 
along with an amyloid-beta 1–42 (Aβ)-based in vitro AD model. This system enables to reliably culture primary 
neurons to mature stages of development, essential to model the adult brain and neurodegenerative diseases 
such as AD. The absence of a blood–brain barrier (BBB) in this model permits evaluation of drug mechanisms 
of action independently of BBB permeability, thereby informing the feasibility of developing fully BBB-penetrant 
therapeutics for CNS interventions based on validated senolytic pathways. Using this platform, we evaluated two 
senolytic regimens previously shown to be effective in AD mouse models: Navitoclax (NAV), which targets the 
Bcl-2 family of anti-apoptotic proteins, and the dasatinib–quercetin (DQ) cocktail, which inhibits tyrosine kinases 
and AKT signaling, among other pathways. We also assess the natural killer cell line NK92 to model emerging 
immune-mediated senescent cell ablation therapies. In synaptically mature cultures, we show that NK92 cells 
preferentially—but not exclusively—targeted Aβ-treated neurons and astrocytes with senescent-like phenotypes. 
DQ demonstrated a safe profile for human neurons, but Navitoclax exhibited non-selective neurotoxicity. These 
findings highlight potential risks associated with developing BBB-permeable therapies based on the mechanisms 
of NAV and NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity. Our work underscores the critical need for human-relevant models in the 
AD drug-development pipeline to improve safety and clinical translatability.

Introduction
The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved 
three Aβ-clearing antibodies that, while effective at 
reducing biological markers of pathology, are yet to 
robustly stop clinicopathological AD progression [ 1–
4]. Historically, a paradoxical disconnect has existed 
between positive results in preclinical research and the 
extraordinarily high failure rate in AD clinical trials [ 5–
7]. This highlights two critical bottlenecks: the need for 
new therapies and the lack of translation from research in 
animal models to humans.
In this context, cellular senescence has emerged as a 
novel druggable target in AD [ 8]. Senescent cells, which 
accumulate with age, adopt a harmful senescence-asso -
ciated secretory phenotype (SASP) that drives inflam -
mation and contributes to tissue damage [ 9, 10]. Both 
tau and Aβ have been associated to deleterious cellular 
senescence in transgenic mouse models of AD neuropa -
thology [ 11– 13]. The selective elimination of senescent 
cells has demonstrated beneficial effects in preclinical 
studies, initially in mouse models of Parkinson’s disease 
[14]. Subsequently senolytics, a class of drugs used to 
eliminate senescent cells [15]have shown promise in mit-
igating AD and AD-like pathology in rodent models [ 11–
[13, 16, 17] and have progressed to clinical trials for AD 
[8]. Importantly, different senolytics act through distinct 
mechanisms and can target different subsets of senescent 
cells [ 15]. In a transgenic mouse model of AD-like tau 
pathology, the senolytic cocktail Dasatinib plus Querce -
tin (DQ) reduced neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) burden, 
neuronal loss, and ventricular enlargement [ 11]. In an 
Aβ-based AD mouse model, DQ selectively eliminated 
senescent cells localized near Aβ plaques, decreased Aβ 
burden, suppressed inflammation, and improved cog -
nitive performance [ 13]. In another tauopathy model, 
treatment with Navitoclax (NAV)—a Bcl-2 family inhibi -
tor—prevented the upregulation of senescence-associ -
ated genes and reduced tau phosphorylation [12].
Senolytics are broadly categorized under a unifying 
umbrella and, as noted, demonstrate diverse mechanisms 
of action [18]. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the use of different 
senolytics in different models of AD-like pathology has 
resulted in conflicting evidence regarding which central 
nervous system (CNS) cell types—neurons, astrocytes, 
microglia, or oligodendrocyte progenitor cells (OPCs)—
undergo deleterious cellular senescence which can be 
targeted by senolytics [ 11– 13, 16– 18]. Indeed, different 
senolytics have been reported to eliminate distinct, non-
overlapping senescent non-CNS cell populations [18].
Laboratory-generated human neurons, such as those 
derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), 
are powerful tools commonly used to address species-
specific differences inherent in the use of rodent models 
[19, 20]. However, the developmental origins and cellular 
characteristics of laboratory-generated neurons dif -
fer fundamentally from those of bona fide human CNS 
neurons [ 21]. Specifically, iPSC-derived neurons origi -
nate from reprogrammed somatic cells and are directed 
through artificial differentiation protocols. In contrast, 
primary human neurons are derived directly from human 
CNS tissue and undergo their natural, lineage-specific 
developmental trajectory toward terminal differentia -
tion. As such, primary neurons are considered an in vitro 
gold standard representing unaltered human CNS cells. 
As mentioned above, senolytics exhibit cell-type-specific 
toxicity [ 18]. Whether artificial reprogramming alters 
molecular pathways in laboratory-generated neurons in 
ways that affect senolytic selectivity and off-target toxic -
ity remains unknown. To the best of our knowledge, such 
validation has not yet been performed.
Human primary neuron cultures have contributed to 
landmark insights into AD pathobiology [ 22– 27]and 
have been extensively characterized for maturation and 
cell composition. Neurons begin to polarize and extend 
processes within the first week, with up to 90% of cells 
expressing class III β-tubulin by day 7 in cultures derived 
from 8–12-week tissue [ 28]. The microtubule associated 
protein 2 (MAP2) and synaptic markers such as synapto -
physin increase by 28 days in vitro (DIV), accompanied 
by extensive neurite networks and electron microscopy 
evidence of mature synapses [ 29]. Synaptosomal-asso -
ciated protein of 25  kDa (SNAP-25) and (postsynaptic 
density protein 95) (PSD-95) levels raise between 20 and 
28 DIV, reflecting both presynaptic and postsynaptic 
maturation [ 30]. Kerkovich and colleagues demonstrate 
increasing outward delayed rectifying currents from 4 to 
21 DIV, and the appearance of inward currents by 7 DIV 
[31]. Calcium imaging shows KCl-evoked Ca² ⁺ influx by 
4 DIV and sustained responses through at least 28 DIV 
[30]. The glutamatergic system also undergoes matura -
tional changes: 1 mM glutamate has minimal toxicity up 
to 30 DIV but becomes neurotoxic by 35 DIV [32].
Cellular diversity in primary human CNS cultures 
evolves dynamically over time. In early post-replating 
stages, neurons constitute the majority cell type, com -
prising ~ 72.5% at 3 days, ~ 79.9% at 6 days, and ~ 73.7% 
at 20 days in vitro, as identified by class III β-tubulin and 
MAP2 immunostaining [ 31]. During the first two weeks 
in vitro, many cells simultaneously express Nestin, a neu-
ral stem/progenitor marker, glial fibrillary acidic protein 
(GFAP), an astrocytic marker, and pan-neuronal antigens 
labeling soma and neurites [ 30, 33]. This co-expression 
profile indicates the presence of bipotential or undiffer -
entiated neural progenitors actively undergoing lineage 
specification. By approximately 24 DIV, this transitional 
state resolves into more defined cell identities, with seg -
regation between GFAP-positive astrocytes and pan-neu-
ronal marker-positive neurons, signaling commitment

Human primary neuron cultures are a well character -
ized alternative which offer a biologically relevant model 
for studying age-related diseases including AD [ 21]. 
However, protocols that support neuronal maturation—
an essential requirement for modeling the adult human 
brain—involve initiating cultures after tissue harvest, 
necessitating access to specialized medical facilities. This 
logistical barrier has limited the accessibility of mature 
human primary neuron cultures for modeling AD and 
related interventions in most laboratories. Cryopreserva -
tion offers a practical alternative by enabling the storage 
and shipment of human primary neurons. This approach 
was demonstrated to be feasible decades ago [ 34– 36]
and today, cryopreserved primary human neurons are 
commercially available. However, cryopreserved neu -
rons typically exhibit reduced viability [ 35]which limits 
their capacity to mature and, by extension, their utility 
as models of the adult human brain. This limitation may 
explain why most AD models using mature human pri -
mary neurons, including models based on Aβ treatments 
as described here, are sparse and have remained con -
fined to a few laboratories over several decades [ 22–[ 28, 
33]37–[ 47].
By applying standard primary mouse neuron culture 
techniques and widely available modern reagents, we 
demonstrate that mixed primary human neuron and 
astrocyte cultures—derived from commercially available 
cryopreserved preparations—can be reliably maintained 
and matured in vitro. Using this system, we evaluated 
the two senolytic drug regimens previously preclinically 
explored as AD interventions—NAV and the senolytic 
cocktail DQ—in an in vitro model of Aβ-induced AD 
pathology [ 8]. NAV inhibits the Bcl-2 family of anti-
apoptotic proteins [ 18, 48– 50]. While Dasatinib inhibits 
tyrosine kinases, including Src family kinases and Bcr-
Abl [51, 52]Quercetin targets survival pathways including 
Bcl-2 and AKT signaling [ 53, 54]. To benchmark NAV 
and DQ, we compare these senolytics to a model of phys-
iological senescent cell ablation using the natural killer 
(NK) cell line NK92, modeling immune system-medi -
ated clearance [ 55, 56]. The results for NK92 co-culture 
experiments are consistent with the preferential abla -
tion of senescent-like populations amidst non-selective 
elimination of healthy-control cells. DQ did not show 
neurotoxicity and only one of several metrics showed a 
non-selective impact on astrocyte viability. In contrast, 
NAV exhibited dose-dependent toxicity in both healthy 
and Aβ-treated neurons. These findings underscore the 
need to refine senolytic strategies in human-relevant 
models of AD.
Results
Characterization of cells types and maturation of 
cryoconserved human neuronal-astrocytic cultures
Only minor modifications to the vendor-provided culture 
protocol and commonly used mouse primary neuron 
culture techniques were required to successfully main -
tain these cells for over 100 DIV. Briefly, the vendor rec -
ommends a media wash within the first 24 h to remove 
dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) from the cryopreservation 
solution; however, we found this step to be detrimental 
to long-term cell viability (data not shown). Instead, we 
allowed the cells to remain undisturbed in the culture 
medium for 48 h, after which the DMSO was further and 
gradually diluted through scheduled media refreshment. 
Critically, we avoided complete media changes, instead 
replacing approximately one-third of the medium every 
two days to retain a portion of the conditioned medium, 
which contains cell-derived trophic factors. It is impor -
tant not to remove a fixed volume (e.g., 100 µL from a 
300 µL well), as media evaporation over time reduces the 
total volume, and fixed-volume aspiration may eventu -
ally expose cells to air and cause desiccation. Instead, it 
is essential that the media be aspirated only to a visually 
determined two-thirds minimum volume, followed by 
the addition of a one-third fresh medium. Evaporation 
must also be carefully monitored, as this approach inher -
ently leads to a gradual irreversible increase in solute 
concentration over time. We recommend seeding cells at 
a density of 1–1.5 × 10⁴ cells/cm², a range in which cyto -
sine arabinoside (Ara-C) is not needed to curb astrocyte 
proliferation. This was of particular importance for our 
studies, as Ara-C is a DNA-damaging agent that could 
induce cellular senescence. However, we did test Ara-C 
and it was tolerated (data not shown). While we did not 
attempt this approach, we think it is possible to maintain 
very low-density primary neuron cultures supported by 
an astrocytic feeder layer, as previously described [57]. By 
following the straightforward steps outlined in the Meth -
ods section, users should be able to maintain human pri -
mary neuron and astrocyte cultures beyond 100 DIV.

The expression of several neuronal maturation mark -

can vary depending on factors such as neuronal plating 
density, we selected this timeframe to represent a consis -

ments, marked by the exogenous administration of Aβ. 
Regarding the experimental duration, the development 
of senescent phenotypes has been reported to occur over 
approximately 5–14 days, depending on the cell type and 
the method used to induce cellular senescence [9, 63, 64]. 
Accordingly, we allowed a 13-day period following Aβ 
treatment before applying senolytic regimens for 24 h.

Numerous previous studies have modeled AD in 
vitro following exogenous administration of Aβ [ 22, 26, 
27, 37, 39, 40, 42, 46, 47]. These experiments are typi -
cally conducted in two-dimensional (2D) cultures rather 
than three-dimensional (3D) systems in which cells are 
embedded in hydrogel scaffolds (e.g., PuraMatrix or 
Matrigel). In 2D cultures, monomeric Aβ secreted by 
cells engineered to overexpressing familial AD (FAD)-
linked genes can diffuse into the media and may be lost 
during routine media changes [67]. For these in vitro FAD 
models, 3D scaffolds may enhance Aβ retention and, con-
sequently, promote aggregation. Conversely, 3D scaffolds 
may hinder the accessibility of exogenously administered, 
pre-aggregated Aβ species, particularly larger oligomers 
and fibrils. Therefore, we chose to employ traditional 2D 
cultures for our experiments.
To determine whether exogenous Aβ persists in 2D cul-
tures following extended media replacement, we treated 
human primary neurons at 28 DIV with Aβ(1–42) and 
assessed whether aggregates were still present at 61 DIV. 
Aβ(1–42) preparations enriched in oligomers (0.5 µM) 
could be readily detected 33 days post-treatment (DPT), 
as demonstrated by immunostaining with the 4G8 anti -
body (Sup. Figure 2a), which targets residues 17–24 of 
Aβ. This was confirmed by the conformation-specific OC 
antibody (Sup. Figure 2 b), which recognizes fibrillar Aβ 
oligomers and fibrils [68]. Preparations enriched in fibrils 
(0.5 µM) were similarly positive 33 DPT (Sup. Figure 2b, 
bottom panel).
To confirm marker specificity, we assessed the widely 
used Aβ/APP-targeting antibody 6E10 together with 4G8 
and OC in cell-free conditions (Sup. Figure 2c). DAPI was 
also included, based on observations that DAPI stained 
cell nuclei but also some Aβ(1–42) aggregates in primary 
neuron cultures. As expected, all antibodies co-localized 
on Aβ(1–42) aggregates (Sup. Figure 2c). Notably, some 
larger plaques exhibited a shell-like pattern with periph -
eral antibody staining and a DAPI-positive core. This may 
indicate that antibody penetration is limited in denser 
aggregates, whereas DAPI remains able to access the 
plaque interior.
Having confirmed the persistence of Aβ aggregates 
following a single administration and characterizing the 
maturation profile of the neurons in culture, we imple -
mented a protocol in which Aβ was applied at 28 DIV, 
followed by a 13-day incubation period prior to the intro-
duction of NK92 cells, NAV, or DQ. The effects of these 
interventions were then assessed at 43 DIV. These treat -
ments were chosen to target overlapping and distinct 
senescence-associated pathways, with the aim of gaining 
mechanistic insight that could inform the rational design 
of future, more selective senolytic strategies (Sup. Figure 
2d).

and macrophages [ 55]. In vitro studies have utilized 
NK92 cells, a human NK cell line, to model the immune-
mediated clearance of senescent cells [ 56]. To investi -

a therapeutic for AD as well as to benchmark NAV and

oligomers, and a low concentration of 0.5 µM was chosen 
from ranges reported in the literature to allow evaluation 
of senolytic activity at 43 DIV while minimizing short-
term toxicity unrelated to cellular senescence [27, 37].

HMGB1 have been shown to be reduced [ 10]69–[ 71, 
75, 83, 84]. Lamin B1 is a structural component of the 
nuclear lamina and essential for brain development [ 85]. 
However, its decrease during senescence destabilizes 
nuclear architecture, contributing to chromatin remodel-
ing, activation of inflammatory signaling, and the main -
tenance of the senescent state [ 70, 71, 83, 84]. HMGB1, a 
highly conserved, ubiquitously expressed nuclear protein 
[86]. In senescent cells, HMGB1 is actively translocated

lar pattern or DAMP [ 69]. Taken together, the reduction 
of LaminB1 and nuclear HMGB1 can be used to identify 
senescent cells. Conversely, the selective elimination of a

and/or nuclear HMGB1—would be expected to increase 
the overall population mean for these markers, as the 
remaining non-senescent cells would drive a shift toward 
their higher LaminB1 and nuclear HMGB1 populations 
means.

NAV inhibits the Bcl-2 family of anti-apoptotic proteins

astrocytes and spare mouse neurons to achieve therapeu-

To gain further insights into the effect of NAV on human 
neurons and astrocytes, cultures were treated with the 
vehicle for Aβ or Aβ 0.5 µM at 28 DIV as performed pre -

added at 14 days post-treatment (DPT) for 24 h and cells 
were fixed for ICC. For our initial experiments we chose 
NAV treatments at 4 and 8 µM. The concentrations of 
NAV used in our study were selected based on our previ -

other senolytics across a range of cell types, experimental 
conditions, and dose levels [ 18]. We intentionally chose 
concentrations from the higher end of the in vitro-tested 
range, as some cell types are known to be non-respon -

aimed to determine whether NAV exerted any effect on 
neurons or astrocytes, or whether it was innocuous in 
our system.

remnant may not have been neurons or the effects of 
NAV 0.5 µM were mild enough to avoid detection by our 
metrics.
Surprisingly, NAV treatment significantly reduced 
the population mean of LaminB1 in neurons overall 
(p = 0.047; Fig. 5g; Table  4e), opposite to what would be 
expected from the elimination of a senescent-like neu -
ron population characterized by low LaminB1 expres -
sion. Condition-specific analysis did not reveal additional 
insights (Fig.  5h; Table 4e), indicating reduction in Lam -
inB1 was taking place irrespective of Aβ treatment. 
HMGB1 analysis did not yield significant effects or trends 
(Fig. 5i; Table 4f). As with NK92 cells, a Bonferroni cor -
rection for the repeated use of HMGB1 and LaminB1 
required a p < 0.0167 to achieve significance. The assess -
ment of HMGB1Lo/LaminB1Lo neurons showed no signif-
icant differences between VEH◊NAV and Aβ◊Aβ + NAV 
(p = 0.058; Fig. 5j; Table  4g; Sup. Figure 5  k). Thus, any 
undetected preferential killing associated with NAV 
treatment would not involve the selective elimination of 
HMGB1Lo/LaminB1Lo neurons.
In terms of the effects on astrocytes, there were no 
observable significant results. The analysis of live astro -
cytes (Fig.  6a; Table  4h), aCasp3 signal within astro -
cyte nuclei (Fig.  6b; Table  4i), and aCasp3 within GFAP 
positive processes (Fig.  6c; Table  4j) did not reveal sig -
nificant effects. Similarly, the analysis of astrocytic Lam -
inB1 (Fig.  6d; Table 4k), HMGB1 (Fig.  6e; Table 4l), and 
HMGB1Lo/LaminB1Lo astrocytes (significance declared 
at p < 0.0167; Fig. 6f; Sup. Figure 6  ; Table  4m) did not 
provide statistical support for selective elimination of 
cells by NAV. 
Overall, NAV treatment at high concentrations (4 and 
8 µM) resulted in non-selective elimination of both neu -
rons and astrocytes. The analysis of apoptotic cell death 
following lower-concentration NAV treatment (0.5 µM) 
further supported the lack of selectivity. Cell type-specific 
viability and apoptosis metrics, however, did not clearly 
indicate whether neurons or astrocytes were preferen -
tially affected at this lower dose. As a result, we remain 
cautious about whether toxicity occurs in neurons at 0.5 
µM. Nonetheless, even if selective cell elimination was 
occurring undetected, NAV 0.5 µM did not target cells 
in a way that is consistent with the elimination of cells 
expressing low LaminB1 or low nuclear HMGB1.

markers establishes a well-defined timeline for modeling 
CNS diseases with adult-onset characteristics.

NAV, DQ, and NK92 cells: insights into senolytic activity 
and selectivity
We utilized these cultures to model AD pathology and 
investigate senolytic selectivity as a proof of principle for 
their potential application in AD therapeutic testing.
To the best of our knowledge, the BBB permeability 
of NAV in humans has not been established. In con -
trast, recent Phase I clinical trial data in AD indicate 
that Dasatinib, but not Quercetin, is detectable in the 
cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [ 94]suggesting limited BBB 
permeability for the DQ cocktail. The brain is also an 
immune-privileged site, with restricted access of periph -
eral immune cells to the parenchyma [ 95]. Nonetheless, 
the mechanisms of action of NAV, DQ, and NK cells are 
well characterized [ 18, 55] making them valuable tools 
for probing senolytic strategies and for guiding the devel-
opment of novel compounds with improved BBB per -
meability for CNS applications. The cell culture system 
described here lacks a BBB, thereby enabling direct expo-
sure of primary human neurons and astrocytes to NAV, 
DQ, and NK92 cells and facilitating such mechanistic 
assessment.
NK92 co-culture was the only regimen in which the 
metrics for viability and the analysis of putative senes -
cence markers LaminB1 and HMGB1 collectively sup -
ported the targeted elimination of senescent-like neurons 
and astrocytes treated with Aβ. However, elimination 
was partially selective at best, based on ample evidence of 
toxicity to neurons and astrocytes in the absence of Aβ. 
This aligns with previous findings on primary NK cyto -
toxicity on astrocytes [ 96]. The off-target cytotoxicity 
observed in this study raises concerns that CNS cells may 
exhibit cross-reactivity with emerging immune-based 
senolytic therapies designed to leverage NK signaling 
for senescent cell clearance [ 97, 98]. Further studies are 
required to assess this potential risk.
Previous studies in mice and non-human primates 
have not reported NAV as neurotoxic [12, 16, 99] but our 
results demonstrate that NAV can kill human primary 
neurons in a concentration-dependent manner in vitro. 
As noted, to the best of our knowledge, the BBB perme -
ability of NAV has not been characterized in humans. 
Consequently, it remains unknown whether NAV, when 
administered for non-CNS indications, can reach the 
concentrations we tested in vitro within the human brain 
parenchyma and induce toxic side effects. Nonetheless, 
our findings highlight a potential neurotoxicity risk that 
may not have been evidenced in vivo in murine models.
Noteworthy, off-target toxicity of NAV in non-senes -
cent cell types is well documented across multiple in 
vitro studies [ 18]. From a mechanistic standpoint, such 
toxicity is not inherently problematic in vitro—provided 
NAV selectively eliminates a substantially greater propor-
tion of senescent cells compared to non-senescent cells. 
In that case, a theoretical dose should exist at which only 
senescent cells are targeted. In contrast, a non-selective 
compound would exhibit similar toxicity to senescent 
and non-senescent cells regardless of concentration, 
with reductions in dose yielding proportionally reduced 
effects but no increase in selectivity. This is precisely 
what we observe with NAV, despite testing concentra -
tions that probe the range in which NAV is reported 
selective in other cells [18].
Thus, even if NAV is ultimately safe for peripheral 
applications due to limited BBB permeability, our find -
ings raise concerns about targeting CNS cells with BBB-
permeable agents that rely on the same mechanism of 
action. NAV acts by inhibiting the Bcl-2 family of anti-
apoptotic proteins [ 18, 48– 50]and our results therefore 
suggest that indiscriminate targeting of this family may 
not be viable for CNS interventions. Future research 
should focus on identifying specific Bcl-2 family mem -
bers that can be targeted more selectively to preserve 
senolytic efficacy while minimizing off-target effects. 
This type of refinement is not unprecedented. NAV’s 
original application in oncology revealed dose-limiting 
thrombocytopenia caused by inhibition of Bcl-XL, a 
Bcl-2 family member essential for platelet survival [ 100–
102]. This led to the development of Venetoclax, a more 
selective Bcl-2 inhibitor designed to avoid Bcl-XL–medi -
ated toxicity [103]. Based on our results, similar precision 
may be required for NAV-inspired CNS-directed seno -
lytic therapies.
In contrast, DQ did not exhibit overt neurotoxicity, 
even at higher concentrations commonly used in vitro 
[18]. While non-selective astrocyte toxicity was sug -
gested by total cell counts, aCasp3-based viability metrics 
were inconsistent, indicating that any observed effect was 
likely moderate. No clear senolytic activity was detected 
based on LaminB1 or HMGB1, diverging from previous 

Page 23 of 31
Walton et al. Alzheimer's Research & Therapy           (2025) 17:176 
reports in transgenic mouse models where DQ selectively 
eliminated senescent-like neurons or OPCs [ 11, 13]. 
However, the emergence of deleterious senescent-like 
neurons in previous mouse studies was attributed to tau 
pathology [11]which may not be present in our cultures. 
Although previous studies on mouse OPCs used mouse 
models of Aβ pathology comparable to our in vitro exper-
iments [13]we neither studied nor assessed the presence 
of OPCs in our cultures. Consequently, our results with 
DQ are consistent with prior findings in mice. Impor -
tantly, a Phase I clinical trial in AD reported detectable 
levels of dasatinib, but not quercetin, in the CSF, and 
deemed the intervention safe for human use [ 94]. Given 
quercetin’s CSF levels, the high concentrations tested in 
our study are unlikely to be clinically achievable, which 
in our view supports the safety profile of the aforemen -
tioned phase I clinical trials.
While experiments with NK92 co-culture were consis -
tent with the elimination of senescent cell populations 
and NAV experiments were not, neither regimen is argu -
ably safe to target senescent cell populations that repre -
sent a small percentage of the total cell population. For 
example, a realistic scenario based on published in vitro 
data involves 76% on-target killing, derived from experi -
ments in which the senolytic compound was tested on 
a pure population of senescent cells in culture, and 25% 
off-target toxicity, measured in a separate culture com -
posed exclusively of non-senescent cells [ 18]. This yields 
an apparent selectivity of 51% (76% – 25%), which may 
initially seem promising. However, such a level of selec -
tivity would have catastrophic implications in mixed cell 
populations, as would be encountered in vivo. To illus -
trate, if senescent cells comprise only 1% of the total pop-
ulation, the same 76% on-target and 25% off-target killing 
would result in 76 senescent cells eliminated and 2,475 
non-senescent (healthy) cells killed in a population of 
10,000 cells. This equates to over 3,250 healthy cells lost 
for every 100 senescent cells eliminated, a tradeoff that 
would be unacceptable in clinical settings, especially in 
non-regenerative tissues such as the brain. Even if NK92 
cells exhibit preferential cytotoxicity, our findings suggest 
their risk profile is comparable to the lack of selectivity 
seen with NAV at high concentrations. Future studies 
incorporating tau pathology in our model should deter -
mine whether DQ selectively eliminates senescent cells 
as reported in mice [11].
Limitations
Our work is not intended to be the final word on the 
mechanisms of action of NK92 cells, NAV, or DQ, but 
rather to underscore that the translatability of findings 
from mouse models to human systems cannot be taken 
for granted. Each senolytic mechanism examined in this 
study warrants further detailed investigation to validate 
our findings and to fully elucidate the nature and specific-
ity of their effects. For instance, incorporating additional 
markers of apoptotic and non-apoptotic cell death (e.g., 
Annexin V, propidium iodide) could offer deeper insight 
into both the mode and extent of cytotoxicity. Moreover, 
the use of small interfering RNA (siRNA) to selectively 
knock down proteins targeted by these senolytics could 
help distinguish which survival pathways are essential 
for healthy neurons and astrocytes—and thus may need 
to be preserved in human interventions [ 50]. While our 
findings provide valuable insights into the effects of 
NAV, DQ, and NK92 cells, they must be interpreted cau -
tiously due to several limitations. First, mixed primary 
neuron and astrocyte cultures do not fully recapitulate 
the complexity of the brain, which includes additional 
cell types and a highly organized structural architecture. 
The mixed human primary neuron and astrocyte cultures 
described here are not intended to replace other models 
but rather to expand the available toolkit by offering a 
human-relevant, reductionist system amenable to mech -
anistic investigation. Regarding cellular senescence, we 
did not detect a distinct senescent-like population when 
comparing control and Aβ-treated cells. Aβ induces a 
wide range of cellular and molecular toxicities unrelated 
to cellular senescence [104]which may have obscured 
small senescent-like populations that were revealed only 
under NK92 co-culture. Future studies should incorpo -
rate additional senescence markers and techniques such 
as single cell RNA sequencing while acknowledging the 
known challenges of identifying senescent neurons [ 78, 
79]. Another limitation is that NK cells eliminate both 
“stressed” and senescent cells through NKG2D receptor 
activation [55, 105]. Since Aβ is a known cellular stressor 
[104]NK92 cells may have targeted stressed cells rather 
than or in addition to senescent cells. Additionally, NK92 
cells possess a simplified receptor repertoire [ 106] and 
our cultures lack additional immune cells required to 
regulate NK cytotoxicity in vivo. Future studies should 
assess NK cytotoxicity in a more physiologically rele -
vant context. Finally, astrocyte assessments may be con -
founded by the use of GFAP , a marker linked to astrocyte 
reactivity rather than astrocyte identity [ 107]emphasiz-
ing the need for additional markers to facilitate a more 
refined analysis.
Conclusions
We developed an accessible method for culturing pri -
mary human neurons and astrocytes to maturity using 
commercially available cryopreserved cell preparations. 
This system is straightforward to establish, requiring only 
basic cell culture skills, and can be readily adapted for 
studying a range of CNS models and therapeutic inter -
ventions. The robustness of our human in vitro AD model 
is demonstrated by its ability to differentiate the effects

mechanisms of action [18]. These findings emphasize the 
importance of human-specific models in refining thera -

their advancement to clinical applications.

Mixed human primary neuron and astrocyte cultures
To prepare Ibidi “ibiTreat” 8-well chamber slides (Ibidi, 
80826) for neuronal cultures, each well was coated with 
200 µL of a 1:1 solution of 1x Dulbecco’s phosphate-
buffered saline (VWR, VWRL0119-0500) and Poly-D-
Lysine (PDL; ThermoFisher, A3890401). The slides were 
placed in Petri dishes, serving as secondary containers to 
minimize contamination risk, and incubated overnight at 
37  °C in a sterile humidified atmosphere. The following 
day, the wells were washed by aspirating the DPBS/PDL 
solution and immediately adding 300 µL of 1x DPBS to 
prevent drying. This washing process was repeated twice, 
waiting at least 2  h between washes. The culture media 
was prepared before cell plating and consisted of Neu -
robasal Plus Medium (ThermoFisher, A3582901), 50x 
B-27 Plus Supplement (ThermoFisher, A3582801), 100x 
GlutaMAX Supplement (ThermoFisher, 35050061), and 
100x Penicillin-Streptomycin solution (ThermoFisher, 
15070063). Filter sterilization of the solution with a 
0.22 μm syringe filter (VWR, 76479-024) prior to adding 
the B-27 Plus Supplement had no observable effects on 
cell viability. Before plating, the wells of the slides were 
washed with pre-warmed culture media and 240 µL of 
media was added to each well to accommodate approxi -
mately a 60 µL cell suspension, ensuring a final volume 
around 300 µL. The prepared slides were equilibrated in a 
humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 3–5% O₂ 
for at least 30 min before cell plating. O₂ levels at 3–5% 
were used to mimic physiological oxygen levels for cellu -
lar senescence experiments, but the cells may be cultured 
at the more conventional 20%. Vials with human fetal 
brain tissue between 17 and 19 gestational weeks were 
provided by Sciencell (Sciencell, 1520). Each cryovial 
(~ 1 × 10⁶ cells) was briefly opened/closed to release pres-
sure and thawed rapidly in a 37 °C water bath for approx-
imately 2 min until a small ice crystal remained. The vial 
was then cleaned with ethanol and the cell suspension 
was gently tapped to dislodge cells adhering to the cap. 
A 50 mL centrifuge tube pre-washed with plating media 
was prepared to prevent cell adhesion to the tube walls. 
Similarly, pipette tips were primed with media before 
aspirating cell suspensions to minimize cell loss dur -
ing handling. The cells were carefully transferred to the 
pre-washed tube using primed tips, with the vial cap also 
aspirated to recover any remaining cells. One milliliter of 
pre-warmed media was added dropwise to the suspen -
sion to avoid osmotic shock, followed by the gentle addi -
tion of 2 mL of media to achieve a final concentration 
of ~ 2.5 × 10⁵ cells/mL. The cell suspension was gently 
pipetted to dissociate clumps and cell density confirmed 
using a hemocytometer. Cells were plated onto the equil -
ibrated Ibidi slides at a density of ~ 1–1.5 × 10⁴ cells/cm², 
resulting in a total well volume of 300 µL and placed into 
humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ and 3–5% O₂. 
Completely replacing the media after plating to remove 
residual cryopreservants significantly reduced cell viabil -
ity. Omitting the media replacement step and leaving the 
cells completely undisturbed for 48  h post-plating sub -
stantially improved culture health. Subsequent media 
changes were performed every 2 days whenever feasible, 
replacing only one-third of the media with fresh cul -
ture media lacking Penicillin-Streptomycin. Retaining 
sufficient conditioned media during these changes was 
essential to support the cells. Adjustments to the media 
volume were made to account for evaporation, ensuring 
the total well volume remained consistent with the ini -
tial plating volume. At the specified plating density, anti -
mitotics were not required as astrocyte growth did not 
adversely affect neuronal viability or the overall health of 
the cultures. While some slight variations in cell density 
and volume can occur, both the overall cell-to-volume 
and cell-to-area ratios described herein were found to be 
important for the long-term viability of the cultures.
NK92 culture and co-culture
NK92 cells (ATCC, CRL-2407) were cultured following 
the vendor’s instructions. Complete growth media was 
prepared using alpha MEM media (Gibco, 12561072) 
supplemented with 12.5% non-heat-inactivated fetal 
bovine serum (VWR, 45000-73), 12.5% heat-inactivated 
horse serum (ThermoFisher, 26050088), 0.02 mM folic 
acid (Sigma, F8758-5G), 0.2 mM myo-Inositol (Sigma, 
I7508-50G), 0.1 mM beta-mercaptoethanol (Thermo -
Fisher, 21985023), 100 U/mL Penicillin-Streptomycin 
solution (ThermoFisher, 15070063), and 100 U/mL 
human recombinant IL-2 (R&D Systems, 202-IL). The 
prepared media was equilibrated in T25 flasks (Corn -
ing) in humidified incubator at 37  °C with 5% CO₂ and 
20% O₂. Thawed NK92 cells were centrifuged at 800 x g 
for 5 min, resuspended in pre-warmed complete growth 
media, and transferred to pre-equilibrated T25 flasks at 
an initial density of 2–4 × 10⁵ cells/mL. Cultures were 
maintained in a humidified incubator and split every 2–3 
days, with the culture medium refreshed to sustain opti -
mal cell density and viability. For co-culture experiments, 
NK92 cells were harvested, pelleted at 340 x g for 7 min, 
and resuspended in neurobasal medium. The cells were 
subsequently added to 42 DIV mixed primary human 
neuron and astrocyte cultures at an effector-to-target (E: 
T) ratio of 10:1 and co-cultured for 24 h (Figs. 3 and 4) or 
20 h (Videos 10– 13).

Aβ & senolytic treatments
Aβ peptide preparations enriched in oligomers were 
generated using a Beta Amyloid (1–42) aggregation kit 
(rPeptide, A-1170-2). Lyophilized Aβ peptides were 
reconstituted in either 5 mM Tris or 10 mM NaOH at 
a concentration of 1  mg/mL, then diluted with HPLC-
grade water and Tris-buffered saline (TBS) to create 
a 100 µM stock solution. To enrich for oligomers, the 
stock solution was incubated at 37 °C for 3 h, aliquoted, 
and stored at -20 °C until use. Primary human neuronal-
astrocytic cultures were treated with Aβ preparations or 
their vehicle at 28 DIV at a final concentration of 0.5 µM. 
The senolytics Navitoclax (Selleckchem, S1001), Dasat -
inib (Selleckchem, S1021), and Quercetin (Selleckchem,

tions. NAV (0.5, 4, or 8 µM), Dasatinib (4 µM) plus Quer-

DIV. After 24 h of treatment, the cultures were collected 
for ICC.
Immunocytochemistry (ICC) and image acquisition
ICC and confocal imaging were performed on the 
microscopy-compatible Ibidi slides to ensure minimal 
disruption to cellular morphology. Cell cultures were 
fixed by progressively replacing the culture media with 
4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Santa Cruz, 3052589-4) 
and incubated with PFA at room temperature (RT) for 
15  min, followed by three washes with ice-cold PBS for 
10 min each. Permeabilization was performed using PBS 
containing 0.05% Triton x-100 (Northwest Scientific, 
JTB/x198-07) (PBTx) for 30  min. After permeabiliza -
tion, cultures were incubated for 30 min at RT in PBTx 
supplemented with 10% normal goat serum (NGS; Jack -
son ImmunoResearch, 005-000-121) to block nonspecific 
antibody binding. Primary antibodies were diluted in 
PBS containing 1% NGS and applied to the cultures for 
1  h at RT. This was followed by four 10-minute washes 
with PBS to remove unbound primary antibodies. Sec -
ondary antibodies, diluted in PBS with 1% NGS, were 
applied for 1 h at RT, followed by four additional 10-min-
ute washes with PBS. For ICC involving repeated use of 
isotype-specific antibodies, a sequential staining protocol 
was employed. The first round of primary and second -
ary antibody incubations was performed on the first day. 
Following an overnight wash to remove residual antibod -
ies, a second round of primary and secondary antibody 
incubations was carried out on the next day. DNA label -
ing was performed by incubating the cultures in PBS con-
taining 1  µg/mL 4 ′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; 
Invitrogen, D1306) for 3  min. After three final washes 
with PBS, the PBS was entirely replaced with pure glyc -
erol (Invitrogen, 15514-011) to preserve the stained cells 
and minimize refractive index changes during imaging. 
Confocal imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 980 
for cell culture characterization and Zeiss LSM 780 con -
focal microscopes (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, 
Germany) for cell culture characterization and senolytic 
regimen studies. The Zeiss LSM 980 was equipped with

imaging through its 32-channel GaAsP detector array.
Spectral scanning with linear unmixing
To resolve DAPI and Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary 
antibodies labeled with 488, 546, 594, 633, and 647 fluo -
rophores, spectral imaging with linear unmixing was per-
formed using the Zeiss LSM 780 confocal microscope. 
Imaging was conducted using the full spectral range of 
the 32-channel GaAsP spectral detector, along with the 
405, 488, 561, and 633 nm excitation lasers. Spectral ref -
erence fingerprints for each primary–secondary antibody 
pair were acquired from single-stained primary neuron 
cultures, rather than from generic fluorophore data -
bases, to accurately capture emission profiles within the 
specific background autofluorescence of the neuronal 
culture system. These reference spectra were applied in 
the system’s online fingerprinting mode to enable real-
time spectral unmixing during acquisition. Because all 
channels share a common gain setting, primary antibody 
clones were carefully selected and paired with second -
ary antibodies of similar expected fluorescence intensi -
ties. This approach optimized signal detection across 
the full fluorophore panel while avoiding saturation or 
under-detection caused by gain imbalances. To further 
optimize acquisition, primary antibodies targeting non-
overlapping epitopes and exhibiting comparable signal 
intensities were assigned to the same excitation laser—
for example, GFAP and MAP2 were labeled with Alexa 
Fluor 633 and Alexa Fluor 647, respectively, both excited 
by the 633  nm laser. This ensured consistent excitation 
efficiency and signal comparability under shared laser

to exhibit spatial overlap, fluorophores with maximally 
separated emission spectra were used to reduce spectral

within the lambda stack retained a clean and distinct 
emission profile, thereby improving the accuracy and

channels. All analyzed neurons were live cells positive for 
MAP2 and negative for GFAP while all analyzed astro -
cytes were live cells negative for MAP2 and positive for 
GFAP . These populations were then exported into new 
CSV files for subsequent statistical analyses. The total 
number of ROIs classified as neurons or astrocytes was 
used to analyze the viability of live cells. Mean HMGB1 
and aCasp3 intensity values within the ROIs were used 
for statistical analyses of nuclear HMGB1 and aCasp3 
levels. Additional steps were required to accurately assess 
LaminB1. Nuclear-based ROIs inherently overcapture 
the LaminB1 signal, a nuclear lamina protein, and aver -
age its signal with non-nuclear lamina regions within the 
ROI. This results in an underestimation of LaminB1 sig -
nal intensity in large nuclei relative to smaller nuclei. To 
address this issue, a new LaminB1 channel was created 
by setting LaminB1-positive voxels in the original chan -
nel to the maximum intensity (255 in an 8-bit image). 
Within each nuclear ROI, the sum intensity of this newly 
generated channel was divided by 255 to calculate the 
number of LaminB1-positive voxels, effectively exclud -
ing contributions from non-nuclear lamina regions. The 
sum signal intensity of the original LaminB1 channel was 
then divided by the number of LaminB1-positive voxels 
to obtain the true LaminB1 mean intensity for each ROI. 
This corrected LaminB1 mean was subsequently used for 
statistical analysis.
Apoptotic cell death analysis for NAV and DQ experiments
Pyknotic nuclei were identified in the DAPI channel using 
user-defined algorithms, generating corresponding ROIs. 
An enhanced aCasp3 channel (aCasp3*) was created by 
increasing the relative intensity of aCasp3-positive vox -
els inversely proportional to signals in other channels (as 
shown in Sup. Figure 5b). Noise in the aCasp3* channel 
was minimized using Gaussian, median, and threshold 
cutoff filters in IMARIS where necessary, standardizing 
the steps for all treatments within each replicate. This 
refined aCasp3* channel was used to define ROIs rep -
resenting apoptotic cell remnants. These ROIs were 
size limited to fragment larger apoptotic remnants into 
smaller, discrete ROIs overlapping with pyknotic nuclei 
ROIs. The pyknotic and aCasp3* ROIs corresponding to 
the same apoptotic cell remnant were identified based on 
spatial proximity and visually inspected to ensure accu -
racy. Subsequently, these ROIs were used to mask a new 
channel from which final ROIs representing apoptotic 
cell bodies were generated (Sup. Figure 5e). Additional 
ROIs were created to identify live cells, irrespective of 
cell type, to determine the total number of live cells in the 
cultures. The proportion of apoptotic cell ROIs to live cell 
ROIs was calculated and used for statistical analysis, pro -
viding a measure of apoptotic cell death relative to overall 
cell viability within the cultures.

Statistics
Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Sta -
tistics (RRID: SCR_002865). For an overview of the statis-
tical workflow used in this study, see Sup. Figure 9a. For a 
conceptual explanation of two-way ANOVAs with inter -
action terms, see Sup. Figure 9b, c. Significant results are 
reported in the main text, while non-significant findings 
are omitted from the text for clarity. Instead, details of 
all statistical tests, including assumption tests, are pre -
sented in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. Assumptions tests were 
carried out to detect extreme outliers (box plots), nor -
mality (Shapiro-Wilk test, p > 0.05, and Q-Q plots), and 
homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test, p > 0.05). Trans-
formations appropriate to the type of assumption viola -
tion were attempted and assumption tests re-run. When 
transformed data did not meet the assumptions, non-
parametric tests were run.
Synapse maturation
One-way ANOVA or Welch’s F omnibus tests and Tukey’s 
or Games-Howell multiple comparisons were performed 
as determined by Levene’s test for homogeneity of vari -
ances. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-
tailed). The sum voxel signal intensity within the ROIs for 
Synapsin, Homer-1, and PSD95 was calculated replicate 
by replicate for each DIV to quantify total protein levels. 
Colocalization between Synapsin and Homer-1, as well 
as Synapsin and PSD95, was assessed by calculating the 
total number of ROIs capturing colocalized puncta, as 
defined in the image processing protocol. The analysis 
was performed on the log10 transform on the sum signal 
intensity or sum number of colocalized puncta for each 
DIV replicate by replicate.
Senolytic regimens
For the analysis of the effects of senolytic regimens on 
viable neurons, LaminB1, and nuclear HMGB1 mea -
sured by ROI based on nuclei, Mahalanobis distances 
were used to detect and discard multivariate outliers 
from the nuclei ROI data, with statistical significance 
assessed using a Chi-squared test ( p < 0.01). The nuclear 
ROI predictive variables included voxel number, the 
length across the vertical axis, LaminB1 mean, HMGB1 
mean, the shortest, mid, and longest distances within the 
ROI bounding box. After removing outliers, treatment 
means for cell count (Live neurons/astrocytes), nuclear 
aCasp3, LaminB1, and HMGB1 were calculated replicate 
by replicate and normalized to the average of each repli -
cate. HMGB1Lo/LaminB1Lo were calculated as described 
in Sup. Figure 3e and normalized to the average of each 
replicate. For the analysis of apoptotic cell death (Sup. 
Figure 5e; see image processing), MAP2/GFAP volume 
per area (see image processing), and aCasp3 signal within 
MAP2/GFAP (see image processing), treatment means 
were calculated replicate by replicate and normalized to 
the average of each replicate. For the resulting variables, 
assumptions tests and transformations were carried 
out as described above for synapse maturation with the 
addition of scatter plots of predicted and residual values 
to complement Levene’s test. A two-way ANOVA was 
used to assess the interaction and the main effects. For 
2 × 2 ANOVA used in NK92, NAV 0.5 µM, and DQ, the 
interaction assesses whether the difference between the 
double vehicle and the senolytic regiment is significantly 
different from the difference between Aβ treatments and 
Aβ plus the senolytic regimen. Regardless of the presence 
of an interaction, hypothesis-driven simple main effects 
for Aβ and for the senolytic regimen were run to identify 
differences relevant to the study while omitting irrelevant 
comparisons. The simple main effects for Aβ compare 
the effects of adding Aβ in the absence of senolytics and, 
separately, in the presence of senolytics. The simple main 
effects for the senolytic regimen compare the effects 
of adding senolytic regimen in the absence of Aβ and, 
separately, in the presence of Aβ. Bar graphs with sim -
ple main effect p values are always plotted. Main effects 
are only plotted when they reach statistical significance 
in the absence of a significant interaction. Bonferroni 
correction was applied to correct for two simple main 
effects, declaring significance at p < 0.025. When two-way 
ANOVA assumptions were not met, a non-parametric 
one-way ANOVA on ranks (Kruskal-Wallis) was run fol -
lowed by Bonferroni all-pairwise comparisons ( p < 0.05). 
The assessment of HMGB1 Lo/LaminB1Lo populations 
was performed using two-tailed independent t-tests. 
Because the HMGB1 and LaminB1 datasets were previ -
ously analyzed in two-way ANOVAs, their reuse intro -
duced the potential for inflated Type I error. To mitigate
