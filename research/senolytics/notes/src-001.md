# Source src-001: Cedars-Sinai Medical Article on Senolytic Supplements

**Source:** Cedars-Sinai website (https://www.cedars-sinai.org/stories-and-insights/expert-advice/are-senolytic-supplements-right-for-me)
**Date:** November 21, 2024
**Author:** Victoria Pelham
**Key experts quoted:** James Kirkland, MD (Director, Cedars-Sinai Center for Advanced Gerotherapeutics); Nicolas Musi, MD (Translational Research Vice Chair, Director of Diabetes and Aging Center)
**Type:** Medical center health article / expert advisory
**Quality:** ok -- substantive expert commentary, no primary data

---

## Core Findings

This is a consumer-facing medical advisory from Cedars-Sinai (November 2024) that urges extreme caution with over-the-counter senolytic supplements. Two leading researchers -- James Kirkland (a pioneer in senolytics research) and Nicolas Musi -- warn that the field is in its infancy ("we know 2% of what we need to know"), that OTC products are typically underdosed, unregulated, and potentially harmful, and that consumers should not self-prescribe senolytic supplements.

## Specific OTC Senolytic Compounds Discussed

- **Fisetin** and **quercetin** are explicitly named as senolytic supplements widely available in pharmacies, supermarkets, and social media.
- The article notes these are "everywhere" but does not provide specific brand names, dosages, or product-level detail.
- No specific dosing information for commercial products is provided beyond the general warning that OTC supplements are "typically sold at much lower doses than needed to be effective."

## Human Clinical Evidence Mentioned

- No completed human clinical trial results are reported in this article.
- Musi's team is described as actively studying a "cocktail of senolytic drugs" in healthy people, investigating safety and potential to remove senescent cells.
- Another trial is investigating whether exercise and nutrition can lower senescence.
- Outcome measures being studied include: bone density, glucose, inflammation, lung function, kidney function, and muscle function.
- Already-approved drugs such as **metformin** are noted as potentially "geroprotective."
- Good manufacturing practices for senolytics are stated to be obtainable only through research protocols, not commercial products.

## Safety Concerns and Adverse Effects

- Kirkland warns that "some interventions that target fundamental aging processes could even cause a lot of harm."
- Potential risks include: interference with other medications, altered drug metabolism, unexpected side effects.
- Absorption and response to senolytic drugs vary "extremely" across ethnic groups (pharmacogenomic concern).
- "Natural" does not mean harmless.

## Expert Opinions and Cautions

- **James Kirkland, MD**: "People have to be very, very concerned about what they buy. Don't take advice over the internet and pay exorbitant prices for things that may or may not work." He recommends avoiding senolytics entirely "until and unless we have really rigorous, reproducible scientific data that pharmaceutical regulators and the medical community accept."
- **Nicolas Musi, MD**: Senolytics could be important for patients with serious conditions (diabetes, osteoporosis, skin/rheumatologic diseases), but patients should consult their doctor or a trained pharmacist/clinical pharmacologist. He recommends exercise for health span and longevity, and notes that common fruits (apples, strawberries) contain anti-aging properties.
- Both experts position current OTC senolytic supplements as premature and potentially dangerous.

## Regulatory and Marketing Claim Issues

- OTC supplements are not FDA-regulated and not held to the same standards as approved medications.
- Consumers cannot verify ingredients, quality, or compound amounts -- especially from online purchases.
- Products may be marketed with claims that don't match their actual content.
- Kirkland explicitly warns about paying "exorbitant prices" for products of uncertain efficacy.

## Limitations of This Source

- This is a health advisory article, not a peer-reviewed study. It contains no primary data, effect sizes, or quantitative results.
- No specific commercial products are named or evaluated.
- No dosing guidelines are provided.
- The article serves as a cautious expert perspective rather than a systematic review of evidence.
- Date is November 2024, which is current but the article does not reference specific 2023+ published trials.
