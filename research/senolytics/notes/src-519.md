## Core Findings

Blagosklonny argues that senolytics are overhyped and that their apparent benefits may be explained by off-target gerostatic (mTOR-inhibiting) effects rather than by selective killing of senescent cells. He advocates instead for gerostatics -- particularly rapamycin and rapalogs -- which inhibit growth-promoting pathways upstream of geroconversion and have far more robust evidence for lifespan extension.

## Key Arguments Against Senolytics

1. **Scarcity of lifespan evidence:** Only two studies show lifespan extension by senolytics in mammals -- fisetin in one small mouse study, and D+Q in one study showing a modest 6.3% increase in median lifespan (937 to 996 days). Neither has been independently reproduced.

2. **Selectivity problem:** Selective killing of senescent cells is harder than selective killing of cancer cells. Cancer therapy has pursued selective cell killing for nearly a century with limited success. Senescent cells cannot be targeted via proliferation (unlike cancer cells), and tissue-specific targeting is not applicable to anti-aging.

3. **Senolytics may not actually kill senescent cells in vivo:** By Kirkland's strict definition, no drug has been proven to selectively kill senescent cells in the organism. Decreased SA-β-gal and p16 markers could reflect marker reduction per cell or rejuvenation rather than cell death. Rapamycin (which does not kill cells) also decreases SA-β-gal and p16 expression.

4. **Confounded biomarkers:** A significant proportion of p16/SA-β-gal-positive cells in aging tissues are activated macrophages (per Gudkov and co-workers), not classically senescent cells. Quercetin and fisetin inhibit foam cell formation and prevent p16 induction -- a gerostatic effect on macrophages, not senolysis.

5. **Potential harm:** D+Q was found ineffective at clearing chemotherapy-induced senescent cells and exerted acute pro-tumorigenic effects (Kovacovicova et al.). D+Q also exacerbated obesity- and age-dependent liver disease progression. Dasatinib carries risks including pancytopenia, pulmonary toxicity, and increased ischemic heart disease mortality.

## Alternative: Gerostatics (Rapamycin)

- Gerostatics slow geroconversion (the transition from cell cycle arrest to irreversible senescence) by inhibiting mTOR and other growth-promoting pathways
- Rapamycin robustly extends lifespan in yeast, Hydra, C. elegans, Drosophila, and mice; effective even when given intermittently or transiently
- Fisetin is a natural dual inhibitor of PI3K/Akt and mTOR; quercetin also inhibits the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway -- thus their lifespan effects may be gerostatic rather than senolytic
- Blagosklonny proposes that two gerostatics combined (e.g., MEK inhibitor + pan-mTOR inhibitor) could act as a senolytic at high concentrations

## Hyperfunction Theory Framework

- Cellular senescence is caused by geroconversion (continuation of growth-promoting signaling in arrested cells), not by arrest itself
- Fully senescent cells are rare in organisms; most cells undergo partial geroconversion
- SASP is only one of numerous hyperfunctions; aging is driven by all gerogenic cells combined
- C. elegans senesces without senescent cells, demonstrating that cell senescence is not required for organismal aging

## Methodology

This is a perspective/opinion article drawing on the author's hyperfunction theory of aging and extensive oncology analogy. Arguments are supported by literature citations but no new experimental data are presented.

## Limitations

- Opinion piece, not a systematic review or meta-analysis
- Author has a known intellectual investment in rapamycin/gerostatics and the hyperfunction theory (he coined the term "gerostatic" and publishes extensively on rapamycin)
- Some arguments rely on analogy with cancer therapy rather than direct evidence
- The claim that senolytic lifespan effects are purely gerostatic is speculative -- not directly tested
