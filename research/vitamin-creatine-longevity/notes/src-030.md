# src-030: The influence of creatine supplementation on the cognitive functioning of vegetarians and omnivores

**Authors:** Benton D, Donohoe R (2011)
**Venue:** British Journal of Nutrition
**Citations:** 97 (Crossref; metadata shows 77)
**Design:** Double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT

## Core Findings

Creatine supplementation (20 g/d for 5 days) improved memory in vegetarians but not omnivores, and reduced intra-individual variability in reaction times regardless of diet. This is one of the first studies to systematically compare cognitive responses to creatine supplementation between vegetarians and omnivores, finding a diet-specific benefit for memory and a universal benefit for reaction-time consistency.

## Methodology

- **Participants:** 121 female undergraduates (mean age 20.3 years); 51 meat-eaters, 70 vegetarian/vegan. Note: abstract mentions n=128 but methods section reports n=121.
- **Design:** Double-blind, placebo-controlled. Random assignment to creatine (n=61) or placebo (n=60).
- **Supplement:** Creatine monohydrate 20 g/d (5 g x 4/day) for 5 days, consumed on empty stomach.
- **Outcome measures:** Word recall (memory), choice reaction times (1/2/4/8 lamps), vigilance (rapid information processing), verbal fluency (COWAT).
- **Analysis:** Three-way ANOVA (supplement x diet x time), with time as repeated measure.

## Key Evidence

1. **Memory (diet-specific effect):** Supplement x diet x time interaction significant (F(1,117)=6.41, P<0.01). After supplementation, vegetarians had better memory than meat-eaters (F(1,118)=5.06, P<0.03). Paradoxically, meat-eaters showed *worse* memory after creatine vs. baseline (F(1,117)=17.84, P<0.001). Baseline memory did not differ between dietary groups.

2. **Reaction time variability (universal effect):** Creatine reduced SD of decision times on the most demanding (8-lamp) condition (F(3,351)=2.59, P<0.05). Effect was independent of dietary style. Mean decision times and movement times were not affected.

3. **No effects on verbal fluency or vigilance:** No significant supplement x diet x time interactions for either measure.

4. **Side effects:** Blind appeared maintained; 85% of placebo and 73.8% of creatine group reported no side effects.

## Relevance to Research Questions

### Q1: Healthspan effects in non-athletes (cognitive)
Directly relevant. Demonstrates cognitive benefits of creatine supplementation in young, healthy non-athletes, specifically for memory (in vegetarians) and reaction-time consistency (universal). Shows creatine can influence brain function beyond exercise contexts.

### Q2: Baseline creatine status across diet patterns
Indirectly relevant. Cites Burke et al. (2003) showing lower muscle creatine in vegetarians at baseline. Notes creatine is found mostly in animal products, only trace amounts in plants. However, baseline cognitive performance did not differ between vegetarians and omnivores in this study, suggesting any baseline creatine deficit did not manifest as a cognitive difference.

### Q7: Direct vegan/vegetarian vs omnivore comparisons
Highly relevant. This is a direct comparison study. Key finding: vegetarians benefited more from creatine supplementation for memory, while omnivores showed a paradoxical decline. The reaction-time consistency benefit was diet-independent. Combined vegetarian and vegan participants into one group (n=70) which limits conclusions about vegans specifically.

## Limitations

- **Short supplementation period (5 days):** Does not assess chronic supplementation effects relevant to longevity/healthspan.
- **Young healthy females only:** Cannot generalize to males, older adults, or clinical populations.
- **Vegetarian and vegan groups combined:** Cannot distinguish vegetarian vs. vegan responses.
- **No baseline creatine measurement:** Did not measure tissue creatine levels to confirm lower baseline in vegetarians.
- **Sample size discrepancy:** Abstract states n=128, methods states n=121. Minor but notable.
- **Paradoxical omnivore memory decline:** Unexpected worsening of memory in meat-eaters after creatine is unexplained and raises questions about mechanism.
- **Glucose placebo:** Placebo was glucose tablets; authors argue short-term glucose effects would not impact measures, but this is debatable.
- **No dietary monitoring during study:** Only confirmed vegetarians avoided meat; no detailed food records.

## Claims to Verify

1. **n=128 vs n=121:** Abstract says "n 128" but Methods says "Female undergraduates (n 121)". The tables and statistical analyses reference df values consistent with ~121 participants. The abstract number appears to be an error or reflects initial recruitment before dropouts.
2. **"95% of total creatine pool is in skeletal muscle"** (line 24-25): Standard textbook figure, likely reliable.
3. **Brain accounts for 20% of BMR despite 2% body weight** (line 29-30): Standard physiological fact, reliable.
