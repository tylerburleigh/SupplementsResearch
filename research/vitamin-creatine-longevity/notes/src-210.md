# src-210: The Potential Role of Creatine Supplementation in Glycemic Control and Insulin Resistance

**Citation:** Królikowska K, Sikora J, Knychalska K, et al. (2025). *Journal of Education, Health and Sport*, 80:59992. DOI: 10.12775/jehs.2025.80.59992

**Type:** Narrative literature review (no systematic search protocol reported)

**Quality notes:** Published in a Polish open-access journal with ministerial points. Authors used ChatGPT for language editing. Not a systematic review or meta-analysis -- no PRISMA protocol, no formal quality assessment of included studies.

---

## Core Findings

Creatine supplementation may improve glycemic control and insulin sensitivity primarily through enhanced GLUT-4 translocation to the cell membrane and AMPK activation, mechanisms that mimic exercise-induced glucose uptake. The strongest clinical evidence comes from a single RCT (Gualano et al., 2011) showing significant HbA1c reduction in T2DM patients when creatine was combined with aerobic training. However, overall findings are inconsistent across studies, with animal models showing clearer benefits than human trials, and no consistent effect on insulin secretion or HOMA-IR in clinical settings.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Mechanisms of creatine on glucose metabolism
- **GLUT-4 translocation**: Creatine promotes GLUT-4 expression (via SLC2A4 gene) and translocation to the sarcolemma in animal models. In T2DM patients, improvements in glycemic control were associated with increased GLUT-4 translocation, not total GLUT-4 content (lines 230-248).
- **AMPK activation**: Increased GLUT-4 translocation correlated with elevated AMPK-alpha expression (Alves et al., 2012) (lines 244-245).
- **PKBalpha/Akt1**: Short-term creatine supplementation increased protein kinase B alpha levels, a key player in insulin-stimulated GLUT-4 translocation and glycogen synthesis (line 246-248).
- **Osmoregulatory effects**: Creatine-induced cell swelling serves as a stimulus for glycogen synthesis in muscles and liver, and increases IGF-1 levels (lines 273-282).

### Insulin secretion findings
- In vitro/ex vivo: High creatine concentrations moderately stimulate insulin secretion in isolated rat pancreas and mouse islets (lines 311-313).
- In vivo animal: Increased insulin levels and improved insulinogenic index (lines 313-314).
- **Clinical**: No confirmed effect in humans, either in healthy individuals or T2DM patients, alone or combined with exercise (lines 314-316).

### Antioxidant and mitochondrial effects
- Creatine protects mitochondria from oxidative stress, protects mtDNA, enhances antioxidant enzyme activity (lines 320-350).
- May reduce ROS through upregulation of peroxiredoxins and other antioxidant defenses (lines 339-347).

### Clinical evidence on glycemic control
- **Gualano et al. (2008)**: Creatine + aerobic training produced greater glucose reduction on OGTT vs. training alone in sedentary healthy males (lines 396-398).
- **Gualano et al. (2011)**: RCT in T2DM patients -- creatine + aerobic training significantly reduced HbA1c and improved GLUT-4 translocation (lines 399-402).
- **Op't Eijnde et al. (2001)**: Creatine prevented GLUT-4 decline during immobilization and promoted increased expression during rehabilitation, but only with training (lines 392-394).
- **Rocic et al. (2009)**: Preliminary study suggesting creatine may match metformin in short-term blood sugar reduction (lines 374-378).
- **Van Loon et al. (2004)**: Creatine increased glycogen storage but not GLUT-4 expression (line 39, ref [38]).
- **Newman et al. (2003)**: No significant changes in glucose metabolism or insulin response (line 372).
- **Oliveira et al. (2020)**: Pilot study in older adults -- creatine + resistance training did not improve insulin resistance (lines 404-406).

### Safety
- No significant adverse effects reported in clinical studies (lines 403-404).

## Methodology

This is a **narrative literature review** covering creatine's effects on glycemic control and insulin resistance. No systematic search strategy, inclusion/exclusion criteria, or quality assessment framework is described. The review covers mechanisms (GLUT-4, AMPK, osmoregulation, insulin secretion, oxidative stress) and clinical/animal evidence. References include 91 citations spanning in vitro, animal, and human studies.

## Limitations

1. **Not a systematic review** -- no formal search methodology, risk-of-bias assessment, or meta-analysis. Selection of studies may be subject to bias.
2. **Heterogeneous evidence base** -- mixes in vitro, animal, and human studies with varying dosages, durations, and populations.
3. **Inconsistent clinical findings** -- the review acknowledges that human trial results are inconclusive regarding insulin secretion and metabolic outcomes.
4. **No quantitative synthesis** -- effect sizes, confidence intervals, and pooled analyses are not provided.
5. **Authors used ChatGPT** for language editing (disclosed), which does not affect content validity but is worth noting.
6. **Venue** -- published in a journal focused on education and health sport, not a specialized endocrinology or nutrition journal.

## Claims to Verify

- **"Creatine supplementation increases intramuscular creatine stores by 10-30%"** (line 357): This is a widely cited figure but the specific primary sources should be confirmed.
- **"Creatine may match metformin in short-term blood sugar reduction"** (lines 377-378): Based on a single preliminary study (Rocic et al., 2009, ref [87]) published as a conference abstract. This claim needs strong qualification.
- **"Insulin resistance affected approximately 22% of American adults over 20 in 2003, rising to 40% among 18-44 year olds by 2021"** (lines 218-222): These NHANES-derived figures should be cross-checked with the original sources (refs [32, 33]).

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Healthspan effects / metabolic markers)**: Directly relevant. Provides mechanistic and clinical evidence on creatine's effects on glucose metabolism, insulin sensitivity, HbA1c, GLUT-4 translocation, and AMPK activation. The strongest finding is the HbA1c reduction with creatine + exercise in T2DM patients. Evidence strength is moderate due to inconsistency across studies.
- **Q3 (Lifespan-related outcomes / metabolic disease risk)**: Relevant but indirect. Discusses T2DM management and glycemic control as risk factors for cardiometabolic complications. The potential metformin-comparable effect is noteworthy but based on very preliminary evidence. One prospective cohort study (PREVEND) found elevated plasma creatine associated with increased T2DM risk, though causality is unclear.
