**Citations:** 0 (very recent)

## Core Findings

This is a comprehensive narrative review covering creatine metabolism, supplementation strategies, effects on athletic performance, non-muscular applications (brain, bone, metabolic), and safety. It synthesizes decades of creatine research and concludes creatine is safe across all age groups with virtually no significant adverse effects, while supporting dosing protocols of either loading (20-25 g/day for 5-7 days then 3-5 g/day maintenance) or steady-state (3-5 g/day achieving saturation in ~4 weeks). The paper advocates for consideration of lifelong supplementation at 3 g/day.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Metabolism and Pharmacokinetics (Q4)
- Synthesized from methionine, arginine, glycine in kidney/liver/pancreas
- 95% stored in skeletal muscle; also found in heart, brain, retina, sperm
- Daily turnover: ~1.1% of creatine, ~2.6% of phosphocreatine converted to creatinine non-enzymatically
- ~120 g total pool in 70 kg male, ~2 g/day converted to creatinine
- Average diet provides ~1 g/day; vegetarians/vegans have lower baseline stores
- GI absorption: nearly 100%; peak plasma at ~60 min post-ingestion
- Transport via creatine transporter type 1 (T1), activity inversely related to intracellular creatine
- Creatine monohydrate: most stable solid form; less stable in solution (cyclization to creatinine)
- Other forms (citrate, pyruvate, ethyl ester): no proven superiority in tissue saturation or efficacy
- After discontinuation: levels return to baseline in 4-6 weeks; no suppression below baseline

### Dosing Strategies (Q4)
- **Loading protocol:** 20-25 g/day (0.3 g/kg/day) for 5-7 days, divided into smaller doses (e.g., 4x5g), then 3-5 g/day maintenance
- **Steady-state protocol:** 3-5 g/day directly, achieving similar saturation after >4 weeks
- Sale et al. found 20x1g/day at 30-min intervals had lower urinary excretion than 4x5g/day (better absorption)
- Athletes with greater muscle mass may need 5-10 g/day
- Both protocols effective; lean mass gains observed regardless of protocol when combined with resistance training

### Safety and Adverse Effects (Q5)
- No confirmed evidence of kidney damage from creatine supplementation, even at higher doses and long-term (up to 5 years)
- Most kidney damage reports involved massively excessive doses, pre-existing renal disease, or concurrent nephrotoxins
- Serum creatinine is unreliable as renal function marker during supplementation (may be elevated without kidney damage)
- Only consistent side effect: weight gain (attributed to osmotic/intracellular water effects)
- Does NOT increase injury frequency, muscle cramps, dehydration, or GI disturbances at normal doses
- Single doses >=10 g may increase diarrhea risk
- Safe in children, adolescents, and elderly populations
- Not prohibited by IOC, WADA, or NCAA
- ISSN position: creatine monohydrate is the most effective legal supplement for lean mass and performance

### Mechanisms Beyond ATP Buffering (Q6)
- **Myostatin reduction:** Creatine + resistance training reduced serum myostatin more than training alone (p < 0.05), suggesting additional muscle-growth pathway
- **IGF-1 upregulation:** Higher intramuscular IGF-1 levels in creatine-supplemented group vs placebo
- **Brain effects:** 8.7% increase in brain creatine after 4 weeks at 20 g/day (vs 20-40% in muscle in 5-7 days); brain requires higher/longer dosing
- **Neuroprotection:** Reduced neurological deficits post-TBI in animal studies; improved outcomes in children with TBI (Sakellaris et al.)
- **Mental fatigue:** Reduction in mental fatigue, including under sleep deprivation
- **Cognitive function:** Improvements in memory and cognitive function reported
- **Glycemic control:** Increased GLUT-4 translocation, reduced HbA1c in type 2 diabetes (Gualano et al.)
- **Metabolic effects:** Potential reduction in liver steatosis, triglycerides, cholesterol
- **Bone health:** Combined with resistance training, improves bone mineral density and reduces bone resorption, especially in postmenopausal women
- **Phosphocreatine shuttle:** Detailed description of mitochondrial creatine kinase (Mi-CK) cycling phosphate groups from mitochondria to sites of ATP consumption

## Methodology

- Narrative review based on PubMed and Google Scholar searches
- Keywords: creatine supplementation, creatine monohydrate, creatine supplementation muscle, creatine neurodegenerative, creatine absorption, creatine kinase
- No systematic inclusion/exclusion criteria reported
- 124 references cited
- No external funding

## Limitations

- Narrative review (not systematic or meta-analytic), so subject to selection bias
- Very recently published (2025) with 0 citations, limiting validation
- Published in a Polish sports science journal with relatively narrow circulation
- No quantitative synthesis or meta-analysis of its own
- Some sections lack critical appraisal of cited studies
- Limited discussion of dose-response relationships for non-muscular effects
- No direct discussion of creatine and longevity/aging biomarkers per se

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q4 (Dosing strategy, mechanism, metabolism):** HIGH -- Comprehensive coverage of dosing protocols (loading vs steady-state), pharmacokinetics, absorption, bioavailability, and creatine monohydrate superiority
- **Q5 (Risks and adverse effects):** HIGH -- Dedicated safety section covering kidney function, GI effects, dehydration myths, weight gain, long-term safety (up to 5 years), and safety across age groups
- **Q6 (Mechanisms beyond ATP buffering):** MODERATE-HIGH -- Covers myostatin reduction, IGF-1 upregulation, brain/cognitive effects, neuroprotection, GLUT-4/glycemic control, bone mineral density, and phosphocreatine shuttle mechanism

## Claims to Verify

1. **Brain creatine increase of 8.7%** -- This is cited from Dechent et al. 1999 and Ipsiroglu et al. 2001; worth confirming the exact figure and conditions.
2. **Long-term safety studies up to 5 years** -- References Poortmans & Francaux 1999 and Schilling et al. 2001; the 5-year claim should be verified in the original studies as the actual duration may differ.
3. **Athletes requiring 5-10 g/day** -- This appears to be an extrapolation rather than a direct experimental finding; the exact citation (ref 24: Kreider et al. 2003) should be checked for the original claim.
