## Core Findings

This is a consumer-facing article summarizing creatine's expanding evidence base beyond athletics into aging, cognitive health, and women's wellness. It conveys established knowledge (muscle strength benefits) and emerging areas (cognitive function, sarcopenia, post-menopausal bone health) through expert quotes from a registered dietitian. It does not present new research findings or reference specific studies by name.

## Key Evidence and Claims

1. **Muscle and performance (well-established):** Creatine improves strength, power, and muscle mass. Supplementation raises muscle creatine stores by 20-40% from a baseline of 60-80% saturation.

2. **Cognitive benefits (emerging):** May help with memory, concentration, and fatigue under stress, sleep deprivation; particularly relevant for vegetarians and older adults.

3. **Aging and sarcopenia:** For adults over 65, creatine can help counter age-related muscle loss when combined with protein intake, hydration, and strength training 2-3 times per week.

4. **Women's health (early research):** May support muscle and bone health post-menopause; more research needed.

5. **Safety:** Long-term use does not harm kidney function in healthy adults. Short-term use appears safe even in mild kidney issues, but long-term data for that group is lacking. Small creatinine elevations are normal and do not indicate damage.

6. **Dosing:** 3-5 g/day for most adults; loading phase (20 g/day split into doses for 5-7 days) optional. Creatine monohydrate is the most studied form.

7. **Side effects:** Generally mild -- gas, bloating, stomach upset at higher doses. Temporary weight gain from water retention in muscle tissue.

## Claims to Verify

- "Supplementation can raise those stores by 20-40%" -- commonly cited figure but should be verified against primary literature.
- "Long-term use of creatine does not harm kidney function in healthy adults" -- broad claim; long-term RCT data is actually quite limited.
- "Creatine is safe for short-term use in people with mild kidney issues" -- specific clinical claim that needs verification from nephrology literature.
