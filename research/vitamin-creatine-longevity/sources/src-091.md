Abstract
Background Tangeretin, a flavone compound (O-polymethoxylated) naturally present in tangerine and other citrus 
peels has demonstrated effectiveness as an anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective agent in several disease model. 
This study evaluated the impact of tangeretin in mitigating cognitive dysfunction and oxidative stress induced by 
colchicine in rats, comparing its efficacy with donepezil hydrochloride.
Methods Cognitive dysfunction was induced by administering colchicine (15 µg/rat) intracerebroventricularly 
(ICV) via a stereotaxic apparatus in male Wistar rats. Colchicine resulted in poor memory retention in acquiring and 
retaining a spatial navigation task, passive avoidance apparatus, and Morris water maze paradigms. Chronic treatment 
with tangeretin (at doses of 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg, p.o. once daily) and donepezil hydrochloride (at a dose of 10 mg/
kg, p.o. daily) for 28 days, starting seven days before colchicine injection, significantly ameliorated colchicine-induced 
cognitive impairment.
Results The biochemical analysis showed that chronic administration of tangeretin effectively reversed the 
colchicine-induced increase in the level/activity of lipid peroxidation, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), myeloperoxidase 
(MPO), nitrite, reactive oxygen species (ROS), tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), 
interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-10 (IL-10), serotonin, dopamine, glutamate, amyloid beta (Aβ) 
peptide, and caspase-3. Tangeretin also reversed the colchicine-induced reduction in the level/activity of brain-
derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), amma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), acetylcholinesterase (AChE), glutathione 
S-Transferase (GST), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), glutathione reductase (GR), catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase 
(SOD), reduced glutathione (GSH), and total thiol (T-SH) in rat brains. However, donepezil hydrochloride did not 
prevent oxidative stress.
Tangeretin offers neuroprotection against 
colchicine-induced memory impairment 
in Wistar rats by modulating the antioxidant 
milieu, inflammatory mediators and oxidative 
stress in the brain tissue

Background
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurological illness that 
worsens with time, impacting behaviour, memory, and 
cognitive abilities [ 1]. It is the most common cause of 
dementia in the elderly, affecting millions worldwide. As 
the population ages, the prevalence of AD is expected to 
rise, posing challenges to healthcare systems and soci -
ety [ 2, 3]. While there is no cure for AD, several FDA-
approved medications like cholinesterase inhibitors (e.g., 
donepezil) and memantine can only manage symptoms 
in early to moderate stages [ 4]. Additionally, new treat -
ments like lecanemab and donanemab are showing 
promise in addressing the underlying biology of the dis -
ease, but they still have their side effects [ 5, 6]. Non-drug 
interventions such as cognitive training, diet, physical 
activity, and creating a supportive environment, which 
is also crucial in managing AD, may also not address the 
main problem [ 7]. Alternative treatments like vitamin E, 
curcumin, and omega-3 fatty acids have been studied but 
lack strong evidence for treating AD symptoms. Insights 
into the disease and developing effective treatments 
are essential for addressing the public health impact of 
Alzheimer’s disease, especially as it relates to memory 
impairment and cognitive decline.
Memory impairment and cognitive decline are stan -
dard features of various neurodegenerative conditions, 
including Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias. 
These conditions pose significant challenges to both 
affected individuals and healthcare systems worldwide. 
Consequently, there is a growing interest in exploring 
potential interventions that could mitigate memory defi -
cits and protect against neurodegenerative damage [8, 9].
Colchicine, a naturally occurring compound derived 
from the autumn crocus ( Colchicum autumnale ), has 
been employed in test subjects to cause memory impair -
ment because of its microtubule-disrupting properties 
[10, 11]. Among the effects it has on the central nervous 
system are interference with axonal transport, neural 
inflammation, and oxidative stress, all of which contrib -
ute to cognitive dysfunction [12].
Tangeretin, a flavonoid compound found primarily 
in citrus fruits, has garnered attention recently for its 
potential neuroprotective properties. Previous studies 
have suggested that tangeretin possesses antioxidant, 
anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic characteristics 
[13] that make it a promising candidate for alleviating 
memory deficits and preventing neural damage [ 14]. The 
present research aims to investigate whether tangeretin 
can offer neuroprotection against the colchicine-induced 
rat model of memory impairment.
Numerous in vitro or in vivo studies have demon -
strated that tangeretin exhibits potent antioxidant activ -
ity, capable of scavenging free radicals and enhancing 
the activity of endogenous antioxidant enzymes [ 15, 16]. 
For instance, Kou et al. [ 17, 18] reported that tangere -
tin increased the levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) 
and glutathione peroxidase (GPx) in neuronal cell lines 
subjected to oxidative stress, thereby reducing cellular 
damage. This ability to modulate the antioxidant defence 
system is critical, as oxidative stress is a crucial contribu -
tor to neuronal cell death and cognitive decline in vari -
ous neurodegenerative diseases, including Alzheimer’s 
disease (AD) and related dementias. In addition to 
its antioxidant effects, tangeretin has been shown to 
exhibit significant anti-inflammatory activity. In a study 
by Shu et al. [ 19], tangeretin reduced the production of 
pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 in 
microglial cells activated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS). 
This reduction in inflammatory mediators is particularly 
relevant in neurodegeneration, where chronic inflam -
mation contributes to neuronal damage and cognitive 
impairments. By modulating the inflammatory response, 
tangeretin may help mitigate the progression of neurode-
generative diseases [20].
Tangeretin’s neuroprotective potential has also been 
explored in animal models of neurodegeneration [ 21]. 
For instance, a study by Hung et al. [ 22] demonstrated 
that tangeretin significantly improved cognitive function 
in a cultured primary rat model of Alzheimer’s disease 
induced by amyloid-beta (Aβ) oligomers. The treatment 
was associated with reduced oxidative stress markers and 
decreased levels of inflammatory cytokines in the brain. 
This supports the hypothesis that tangeretin can mitigate 
the neurodegenerative processes through its combined 
antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Another study 
by Datla et al. [23] investigated the effects of tangeretin in 
a rat model of Parkinson’s disease induced by 6-hydroxy-
dopamine (6-OHDA). The results showed that tangeretin 
treatment not only improved motor function but also 
preserved dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. 
This neuroprotection was attributed to tangeretin’s ability 
to reduce oxidative stress and inhibit neuroinflammation, 
further reinforcing its potential as a therapeutic agent in 
neurodegenerative conditions [24].
Conclusions These findings suggest that chronic administration of tangeretin at 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg, p.o. once 
daily, was protective in mitigating colchicine-induced cognitive impairment and associated oxidative stress. At the 
same time, donepezil hydrochloride did not demonstrate similar effects.

Given tangeretin’s demonstrated ability to modu -
late oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators, it is 
hypothesised that this compound may offer neuropro -
tection against colchicine-induced cognitive deficits. The 
evidence strongly suggests that tangeretin could coun -
teract the pathological processes triggered by colchicine 
in the brain. Tangeretin may protect neuronal integrity 
and improve cognitive function in this model by enhanc -
ing the antioxidant defence system and reducing neuro -
inflammation. This hypothesis is supported by previous 
in vitro and in vivo findings, which highlight tangere -
tin’s potential to mitigate neurodegenerative processes 
through its dual antioxidant and anti-inflammatory 
actions.
Scientific insight into the impact of tangeretin on cog -
nitive function and its mechanisms of neuroprotection in 
the context of colchicine-induced memory impairment is 
crucial for elucidating potential therapeutic interventions 
for memory-related disorders. The study provides infor -
mation on comprehensive biochemical parameters like 
oxidative stress markers [Reactive oxygen species (ROS) 
concentrations, antioxidant enzymes including catalase 
(CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GPx), and superoxide 
dismutase (SOD), and lipid peroxidation products such 
malondialdehyde (MDA)]; neuroinflammatory markers 
[pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1β 
(IL-1β) and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), anti-
inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), ]; 
neurotrophic factors [neurotrophic factors, such as nerve 
growth factor (NGF) and brain-derived neurotrophic 
factor (BDNF)]; neurotransmitters (levels of critical neu -
rotransmitters involved in memory and cognitive func -
tion, such as serotonin, dopamine GABA and glutamate; 
apoptosis markers (Caspase-3 activity); neurotrophic 
factors [Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF), 
Amyloid Beta (Ab) Peptide]; inflammatory mediators 
[myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity, nitric oxide (NO) lev -
els. These biochemical parameters can provide insights 
into how tangeretin may exert its neuroprotective effects 
and mitigate colchicine-induced memory impairment in 
Wistar rats. By measuring these parameters, researchers 
can assess the impact of tangeretin on oxidative stress, 
neuroinflammation, apoptosis, neurotransmitter balance, 
and overall neuronal health.
This study, therefore, aims to contribute valuable 
insights to the field of neuroprotection and cognitive 
health, opening doors to novel strategies for managing 
and potentially preventing cognitive decline associated 
with neurodegenerative conditions.
Materials and methods
Chemicals
Acetylthiocholine iodide, donepezil hydrochloride, col -
chicine, glutathione, 5,5 ′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) 
(DTNB), tangeretin (TGN, CAS No: 481–53 − 8/purity, 
99%), epinephrine, hydrogen peroxide, 1-chloro-2,4-dini-
trobenzene (CDNB), 2-thiobabituric acid (TBA), bovine 
serum albumin (BSA), and thiobarbituric acid (TCA) was 
obtained from Sigma–Aldrich, USA.
Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kits 
for TNF-α, NF-κB, IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10; caspase-3; 
Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF); amyloid 
beta (Ab) peptide were respectively procured from the 
Institute of Biological Engineering of Nanjing Jiancheng, 
Nanjing, China; Elabscience Biotechnology Company, 
Beijing, China; Elabscience Biotechnology Company, Bei-
jing, China; and Boster Biological Technology, Fremont, 
California, USA; WKEA Med Supplies Corp., New York, 
USA (rat b-amyloid 1–42). Other reagents and chemicals 
were of the highest grade available.
Animals
Male Wistar rats, sourced from the Animal House of the 
Department of Biological Sciences at KolaDaisi Univer -
sity in Ibadan, weighing between 200 and 220  g at the 
onset of the surgical procedures, were utilised for the 
study. The animals underwent an acclimatisation period 
to the laboratory environment before the experiments. 
Post-surgery, these animals were housed in pairs in plas -
tic cages with comfortable bedding, maintaining stan -
dard light-dark cycles, and provided ad libitum access to 
food and water.
All experiments were conducted between 08:00 and 
16:00. The research procedure was approved by Ajayi 
Crowther University’s research ethics committee (FNS/
ERC/2022/020B) and followed the National Academy of 
Science’s (NAS) recommendations for the moral use and 
appropriate handling of laboratory animals.
Surgery and intracerebroventricular (ICV) administration 
of colchicine
With a few minor adjustments, the surgical process 
adhered to a predetermined protocol. All animals were 
placed in a stereotaxic device and given an intraperito -
neal dose of sodium pentobarbital (45 mg/kg). To insert 
the injection cannula into the lateral cerebral ventricle, a 
midline sagittal incision was created in the scalp, and two 
holes were drilled into the skull. The scalp was sutured 
after that. The method was the same in rats receiving the 
sham operation, except for the cannula implantation and 
hole drilling. The injection site was located 3.6 mm below 
the cortical surface, 1.8 mm lateral to the sagittal suture, 
and 0.8 mm posterior to the bregma [10].
To avoid infection, gentamicin (5  mg/kg, intraperito -
neal) was administered to every animal after surgery. 
Gentamicin was selected for its well-documented abil -
ity to induce oxidative stress and neuroinflammation, 
providing a reliable model to assess the neuroprotective

effects of tangeretin. Intracerebroventricular (ICV) infu -
sions of rats were made with either physiological saline 
or a solution that included 15 µg of dissolved colchicine 
in physiological saline. A Hamilton micro syringe placed 
within the injection cannula was used to inject these 
solutions (5  µl). The microsyringe was left in place for 
two minutes following the injection to promote diffusion. 
Throughout the recovery phase, extra care was taken to 
ensure the rat’s cage had food and water.
Drugs and treatment protocol
Fresh solutions of tangeretin and colchicine were pre -
pared for each experiment. The colchicine solution in 
physiological saline was prepared to deliver a dose of 
15 µg in a 5 µl volume for intracerebroventricular admin-
istration. For oral intake, tangeretin and donepezil hydro-
chloride were suspended in physiological saline and 
given 1  ml per 100  g of body weight. The animals were 
randomly allocated into six groups (n = 10).
Group A: As the control group, this group of healthy 
rats underwent an intracerebroventricular injection 
(ICV) of physiological saline. Group B: Rats given a 
bilateral ICV injection of 15  µg/rat of colchicine and 
physiological saline to develop Alzheimer’s disease (AD). 
Group C: The rats were given an oral dose of donepezil 
hydrochloride (10  mg/kg) 7 days before receiving a col -
chicine injection (15  µg/rat, ICV), and then they were 
given another 21 days of donepezil hydrochloride post-
treatment. Group D: The rats were given an injection of 
colchicine (15 µg/rat, ICV) after 7 days of pre-treatment 
with tangeretin (50  mg/kg, once daily orally), and then 
they were given another 21 days of post-treatment with 
the same dose of tangeretin. Group E: Rats were given 
a pre-treatment of tangeretin (100  mg/kg, once daily 
orally) for 21 days, then an ICV injection of colchicine 
(15 µg/rat), and finally a post-treatment of the same dose 
of tangeretin for another 21 days. Group F: The rats were 
given an injection of colchicine (15  µg/rat, ICV) after 7 
days of pre-treatment with tangeretin (200  mg/kg, once 
daily orally), and then they were given another 21 days of 
post-treatment with the same dose of tangeretin (Fig. 1).
Based on previously published research, the dosages 
of were chosen for tangeretin [ 14], colchicine [ 25], and 
donepezil hydrochloride [ 26]. The study’s duration was 
founded on the prior reports of Mohamed et al. [ 27] and 
Khurana et al. [25].
Behavioural assessment
Assessment of cognitive performance
The Morris water maze was used to assess the rats’ spa -
tial navigation skills or their ability to learn and remem -
ber a spatial navigation assignment. The animals were 
trained to get to a platform in a 180 cm by 60 cm circular 
pool with water kept at 28 ± 2º degrees Celsius and 40 cm 
deep. The four beginning locations on the pool’s wall—
A, B, C, and D—marked the division into four quadrants. 
A 9-cm-diameter circular platform was positioned on a 
column 2  cm below the water’s surface inside the pool. 
Throughout the experiment, this platform—positioned 
in the middle of one of the four quadrants—acted as the 
only means of escaping from the water. A coloured flag 
was placed outside the pool at a consistent distance from

(a) Maze acquisition phase (training): Rats had four 
trials, separated by ten minutes, during a training

The four trials all started from different positions. 
The initial acquisition latency (IAL) was measured

(b) Maze retention phase (testing for retention of the

and second retention latencies, or 1st and 2nd RL,

Assessment of gross behavioural activity
Rats’ spontaneous motor activity and overall behaviour 
were assessed in an open field arena on days 13, 14, and 
21 following the ICV injection. The wood-built, open-
field chamber was 72 cm by 72 cm and 36 cm high. It had 
a glass wall and a floor divided into squares of the same 
size. The open-field challenge immediately preceded the 
water maze exam [28].
Biochemical tests
Biochemical evaluations were conducted on day 22, 24 h 
after the last behavioural testing, and on day 21, after 
the colchicine injections. The animals were euthanised 
by injection of an intraperitoneal dose of sodium pento -
barbital (45 mg/kg), and after their brains were removed, 
they were washed in cold, isotonic saline. To homogenise 
brain tissue samples, ten times as much ice-cold 0.1  M 
phosphate buffer was used (with a pH of 7.4). After the 
final homogenate was centrifuged for 15  min at 10,000 
× g, some of the supernatant was extracted and used for 
biochemical analysis.
Oxidative stress markers
Hydrogen peroxide generation The method described 
by Wolff was followed to determine the hydrogen perox -
ide level [ 29]. After the sample was added, the reaction 
mixture, known as FOX 1, was made up of 10 millilitres of 
100 mM xylenol orange, 50 millilitres of 250 mM ammo -
nium ferrous sulfate, 10 millilitres of 100 mM sorbitol, 5 
millilitres of 25 mM H 2SO4, and 30 millilitres of distilled 
water. Following a half-hour incubation period at room 
temperature, the absorbance at 560 nm was determined. 
The data were represented as micromoles per milligram 
of protein and were calculated by extrapolating from the 
standard curve.
Total thiol content The method described by Ellman 
was followed to determine the total thiol content [ 30]. 
Exactly 20 µL of sample homogenate, 510 µL of phosphate 
buffer (pH 7.4, 0.1 M), 35 µL of DTNB (1 mM), and 35 
µL of distilled water were combined to create a reaction 
mixture. After 30 min, the process was stopped at room 
temperature, and the absorbance at 412  nm was then 
measured.
Measurement of lipid peroxidation According to ear -
lier protocols, the amount of malondialdehyde (MDA) 
in the rats’ brains was measured. Exactly 300 µl of brain 
homogenate was mixed with 30% TCA and 5 N HCl, and 
then 2% TBA was added in 0.5 N NaOH. After 15 min of 
heating at 90 °C, this mixture was centrifuged for 10 min 
at 12,000  g. Using a UV-visible spectrophotometer, the 
pink colour of the resultant supernatant was detected at 
532 nm. Using a standard curve made with tetra ethoxy 
propane, the concentration of MDA was calculated and 
expressed as nmol/mg of protein [31].
Levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) Bondy and 
Guo’s technique served as the foundation for determin -
ing ROS. Specifically, 2,7-dichlorofluorescin diacetate 
(DCFH-DA) is hydrolysed in homogenates to 2,7-dichlo -
rohydrofluorescein (DCFH), which is further oxidised by 
reactive oxygen species (ROS) to fluorescent 2,7-dichlo -
rofluorescein (DCF). In summary, 10 µL of homogenate 
and 10 µL of 1.25 M DCFH-DA dissolved in ethanol were 
added to 990 µL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The 
mixture was then exposed to light and incubated for 
30 min at 37 °C. At wavelengths A em = 525 nm and A ex 
= 488 nm, the product’s fluorescence was measured. The 
ROS was calculated using a standard curve containing 10 
µM DCF and expressed in µmoles of DCF/min/mg pro -
tein [32].

pH 7.0), EDTA (2.5 mM, 25 µL), pure water (10.5 µL), and 
GSH (500 µL, 0.1 M) were combined to create a solution. 
After adding CDNB (10 µL, 25 mM) and the sample (20 
µL) to the mixture, the reaction mixture was left for five 
minutes. Using a spectrophotometer, absorbance mea -

Glutathione peroxidase activity The method for mea -
suring glutathione peroxidase activity in brain tissue was 
established by Rotruck et al. and applied in this investi -
gation. A mixture of sodium phosphate buffer (500 µL), 
10 mM sodium azide (100 µL), 4 mM GSH (200 µL), 2.5 
mM H2O2 (100 µL), a 50 µL sample adjusted to 2.0 mL

stop it (1 mL). This mixture was incubated at 37  °C for 
three minutes. Using a spectrophotometer, absorbance 
measurements were made at 412  nm, and glutathione 
peroxidase activity was reported in nmol per milligram 
of protein [34].
Glutathione reductase activity This study’s evaluation 
of GR activity adhered to the procedure by Carlberg and 
Mannervik. Exactly 1.65  ml of 0.1  M phosphate buffer 
with a pH of 7.6, 0.1 ml of 0.5 mM EDTA, 0.1 ml of 0.1 
mM NADPH, 0.05 ml of 1 mM oxidised GSH, and 0.1 ml 
of 10% brain homogenate made up the reaction mixture 
for this test. With a spectrophotometer set to 340  nm, 
the changes in absorbance in the reaction mixture—
which represent the loss of NADPH at 25˚C—were mea -
sured. Using a molar extinction coefficient of 6.22 × 103 
M− 1cm− 1, the data were quantified and represented as 
nmol NADPH oxidised/min/mg protein [35].
Catalase activity The catalase activity in brain tissue was 
measured in this investigation using the methodology 
developed by Aebi. The reaction mixture contained 20 µL 
of the sample, 180 µL of 300 mM H 2O2, and 1800 µL of 
50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) (diluted at a 
ratio of 1:50). The catalase activity was measured by track-
ing the steady decrease in H2O2 absorbance over two min-
utes at 240 nm. The results were represented as µmol of 
H2O2 depleted per minute per milligram of protein [36].
Superoxide dismutase activity Superoxide dismutase 
activity (SOD) was measured using the procedure 
described by Misra and Fridovich. The foundation of this 
procedure is its capacity to prevent adrenaline autoxida -
tion at pH 10.2 and 30 °C. The sample (20 µL) and 0.05 M 
carbonate-bicarbonate buffer (2.5 mL, pH 10.2) made up 
the reaction mixture. After that, epinephrine (0.3 mL, 0.3 
mM) was added and well combined. A spectrophotom -
eter measured the absorbance at 480 nm for 150 s, spaced 
30 s apart. The findings were presented as SOD activity in 
nmol per milligram of protein [37].
Glutathione (GSH) level The glutathione levels in brain 
tissue were measured in this investigation using the 
method by Jollow et al. After adding an equivalent volume 
of 4% sulfosalicylic acid to each sample aliquot, centrifu -
gation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C was used to depro-
teinise the samples. After that, DTNB was combined with 
50 µL of the acquired supernatants (10 mM, 4.5 mL). At 
a wavelength of 412  nm, the absorbance was measured, 
and the results were represented as µmol/mg protein [38].
Neuroinflammatory markers
Cytokines level Cytokines such as TNF-α, NF-κB, IL-1β, 
IL-6, and IL-10 ELISA kits by the manufacturer’s The 
levels of these inflammatory mediators and cytokines in 
brain tissue were measured in picograms per millilitre 
(pg/mL).
Nitrite level Using the Griess reagent (0.1% N-(1-naph -
thyl) ethylenediamine dihydrochloride, 1% sulfanilamide, 
and 2.5% phosphoric acid), the nitrite levels in the brain 
homogenate were determined, adhering to the protocol 
described by Green et al. The treated tissue sample was 
mixed with an equivalent amount of Griess reagent, and 
the absorbance was measured at 542 nm using a UV-vis -
ible spectrophotometer. A sodium nitrite standard curve 
was used to calculate the nitrite concentration, which was 
then expressed as µg/mg of protein [39].
Myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity The method outlined 
by Granell et al. was used to assess the tissue’s myeloper-
oxidase (MPO) activity. The reaction mixture contained 
70 mL of diluted 3 mM H 2O2 in 80 mM phosphate buf -
fer, 10 mL of 1.6 mM tetramethylbenzidine dissolved in 
dimethyl sulfoxide, and 20 mL of homogenate superna -
tant (pH 5.4). Using a spectrophotometer, the absorbance 
was measured at 630  nm, and the activity of MPO was 
calculated as µmol of H 2O2 depleted per minute per mil -
ligram of protein [40].

serotonin in brain tissue was according to the protocol 
by Schlumpf et al. First, 0.25 mL of O-phthaldialdehyde 
(20  mg in 100 mL concentrated HCl) was added to 0.2 
mL of the supernatant from the brain tissue. The mixture 
was heated to 100 °C for 10 min to create the fluorophore. 
With a spectrophotometer, measurements were made 
between 360 and 470 nm after the samples were allowed to 
reach equilibrium at room temperature. Exactly 0.25 mL 
of strong HCl without O-phthaldialdehyde was applied to 
the serotonin tissue blank. A 1:1 combination of distilled 
water and HCl-butanol generated an internal standard

Estimation of glutamate and GABA The brain tissue 
supernatant was homogenised, and around 1.0 mL was 
dried in an oven at 70 °C. The remaining material was then 
reconstituted in 100 mL of distilled water. Using a micro-
pipette, standard solutions of GABA at a concentration of 
2 mM and glutamate (2.942 mg in 10 mL distilled water)

in a chamber with a solvent mixture of water, acetic acid, 
and butanol (12:3:5 v/v). The paper was taken out and 
dried once the solvent reached its top. A second run was 
conducted. Following drying, the papers were sprayed 
with a 0.25% ninhydrin reagent and baked for four min -
utes at 100 °C. The portions of the paper that, according 
to the standard, corresponded to glutamate were cut out 
and cleaned using 0.005% CuSO 4 in 75% ethanol. Using 
a spectrophotometer, their absorbance was calculated at 
515 nm about a blank [41].

and homogenised for about a minute in a 1:10 HCl-

This allowed for the aqueous phase (0.2 mL) extraction

of sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.9), 0.05 mL of 0.4 M HCl, 
and 0.2 mL of the aqueous phase were added. Then, 0.1

oxidation. After two minutes, 0.1 mL of sodium sulphite 
solution was added to stop the reaction. After 1.5 min, 0.1 
mL of acetic acid was added. For six minutes, the entire 
solution was heated to 100 °C. A spectrophotometer mea-
sured the excitation and emission spectra at 330–375 nm 
after the sample was warmed to room temperature. Tissue 
blanks were made for the dopamine estimate by adding 
the oxidation step reagents in the opposite order (sodium 
sulphite before iodine). The final dopamine assessment is 
expressed in pg/mg of tissue [41].

the test combination (Ellman reagent). At intervals of 
15 s, the enzyme activity was measured at 412 nm with 
a UV-visible spectrophotometer. The specific activity of 
AChE was presented in nmoles/min/mg protein [42].

the decrease in BDNF levels suggests impaired synaptic 
function, reduced neurogenesis, and compromised neu -
ronal survival, all of which can contribute to memory 
deficits [86, 88]. On the other hand, tangeretin may have 
attenuated Aβ peptide accumulation and aggregation 
by inhibiting the formation of Aβ fibrils and reducing 
Aβ-induced neurotoxicity, possibly due to its antioxidant 
and anti-inflammatory properties, which may be crucial 
in mitigating the neurotoxic effects of Aβ peptides [ 89]. 
Furthermore, increased BDNF levels following tangere -
tin treatment may be associated with improved learning 
and memory performance in animal models of neuro -
degenerative diseases. By enhancing BDNF expression, 
tangeretin, like donepezil hydrochloride, may support 
neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive func -
tion in the brain [84, 90].
Caspase-3 is a cysteine protease that plays a central 
role in the execution phase of apoptosis, a programmed 
cell death process [ 91]. In memory impairment, includ -
ing neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s dis -
ease (AD), alterations in the activity of specific enzymes 
such as Caspase-3 play crucial roles in the pathophysi -
ology of the condition [ 92]. The increase in the activity 
of this enzyme by colchicine suggests cleavage of vari -
ous cellular substrates, including structural proteins and 
DNA repair enzymes, resulting in apoptotic cell death, 
contributing to neuronal loss and synaptic dysfunction, 
ultimately impairing cognitive function and memory for -
mation in this study [ 93]. On the other hand, tangeretin 
action similar to that of donepezil hydrochloride, to have 
lowered the activity of Caspase-3, can be linked to its effi-
cacy in inhibiting caspase-3 activation or blocking down -
stream apoptotic pathways, which may hold promise for 
preserving neuronal integrity and ameliorating memory 
deficits in neurodegenerative diseases [94].
Conclusion
In conclusion, this study investigated the potential of 
tangeretin in alleviating cognitive dysfunction and oxi -
dative stress induced by colchicine in rats, comparing 
its efficacy with donepezil hydrochloride. The findings 
revealed that colchicine administration led to signifi -
cant cognitive impairment and increased oxidative stress 
markers in rat brains. Chronic treatment with tangere -
tin at doses of 50, 100, and 200 mg/kg orally once daily 
effectively ameliorated colchicine-induced cognitive 
deficits and reversed the altered biochemical parame -
ters associated with oxidative stress. Notably, tangeretin 
demonstrated a protective role by reducing Caspase-3 
activity and modulating various oxidative stress markers, 
neurotransmitters, inflammatory cytokines, and antioxi -
dant enzymes. In contrast, donepezil hydrochloride did

not exhibit similar effects in preventing oxidative stress. 
These results highlight the potential therapeutic benefits 
of tangeretin in preserving cognitive function and com -
bating oxidative stress in neurodegenerative conditions 
like Alzheimer’s disease. Further research is warranted to 
explore the underlying mechanisms of tangeretin’s neu -
roprotective effects and its implications for developing 
novel treatments for cognitive impairments associated 
with neurodegenerative diseases.
