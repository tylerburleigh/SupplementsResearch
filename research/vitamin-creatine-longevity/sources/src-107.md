ABSTRACT
Importance: Ischemic stroke leads to neuronal cell death due to a lack of oxygen and glucose. 
Baicalin is a flavonoid that has antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties.
Objective: The aim of this study is to elucidate the anti-oxidant and anti-apoptotic effects of 
baicalin in animal models of stroke.
Methods: Vehicle or baicalin (100 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally immediately 
after the middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) surgery. Neurobehavioral tests were 
conducted 24 h post-MCAO and brain tissue was isolated to assess histopathological changes 
and apoptosis-associated protein expression. Additionally, reactive oxygen species (ROS) and 
lipid peroxidation (LPO) assays were performed to evaluate oxidative stress.
Results: MCAO animals exhibited severe neurological deficits, which were significantly 
alleviated by baicalin treatment. Baicalin mitigated the up-regulation in ROS and LPO levels 
induced by surgery. MCAO damage led to severe histopathological lesions and an increase 
in terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling-positive reactions, these 
alterations were alleviated by baicalin treatment. MCAO damage decreases the expression 
of Bcl-2 and increases the expression of Bax, baicalin alleviates these changes. Baicalin also 
attenuated the upregulation of caspase-3 expression caused by MCAO injury.
Conclusions and Relevance: These results can suggest evidence that baicalin exerts 
neuroprotective effects by preventing apoptosis during cerebral ischemia. In conclusion, 
baicalin acts as a potent neuroprotective agent through its antioxidant and anti-apoptotic 
effects on neuronal cell damage.
Keywords: Baicalin; cerebral ischemia; neuroprotection; stroke

ischemic injury disrupts the blood-brain barrier (BBB), causing permanent neurological 
deficits [4 ]. It also increases intracellular calcium and harmful molecules, induces oxidative

Baicalin is a flavonoid compound derived from the roots of Scutellaria baicalensis and Scutellaria 
lateriflora , both of which are key components in traditional Chinese herbal medicine and 
Japanese Kampo medicine [7 ]. Baicalin exhibits anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, anti-
cancer, and anti-viral properties [8 -10 ]. Baicalin also exerts potent antidepressant effects 
by suppressing neuronal apoptosis in models of chronic and unpredictable mild stress [11 ]. 
Baicalin promotes the neuronal differentiation of neural stem cells and protects neurons 
against cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [12 ,13 ]. Furthermore, baicalin reduces BBB 
permeability during hypoxia and attenuates the inflammatory response following ischemic 
neuronal injury [9 ,10 ]. Although the benefits of baicalin have been reported, further studies 
are required to elucidate the protective effects of baicalin on cerebral ischemia. The aim of 
this study was to evaluate the neuroprotective effects of baicalin in animal models of stroke 
through anti-oxidation and apoptosis suppression. By investigating the effects of baicalin 
on neuronal survival, this study provides insight into the potential therapeutic benefits of 
baicalin in the treatment of stroke. We performed various neurological behavioral tests and 
histopathological evaluations for this study. We also investigated oxidative stress levels and 
assessed changes in typical apoptotic regulatory proteins Bcl-2, Bax, and caspase-3.
METHODS
Experimental animals
Sprague-Dawley rats (male, 7 weeks old, 210–230 g, n = 48; Samtako Co, Korea) was used as 
experimental animals. Animals were provided free to food and water, and were maintained 
in animal rooms where light (12-h light/dark) and temperature (25°C) were controlled. 
The animals were grouped as follows: vehicle + sham, baicalin + sham, vehicle + middle 
cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), and baicalin + MCAO. Baicalin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was 
freshly dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) immediately before administration. 
The solution was visually checked to confirm that no sediment had formed. Baicalin was 
administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 100 mg/kg immediately following MCAO surgery. 
We have determined the dosage of baicalin treatment according to a previous study [14 ]. 
Vehicle indicates only PBS administration. Twelve rats per group were used for the following 
experiments: reactive oxygen species (ROS) and lipid peroxidation (LPO) assay (n = 4 for 
each group), morphological study (n=4 for each group), and Western blot analysis (n = 4 for 
each group). Neurobehavioral tests were conducted in all animals (n = 48). All experimental 
procedures were carried out according to the guideline of the Institutional Animal Care and 
Use Committee of Gyeongsang National University (GNU-240527-R0110).
Middle cerebral artery occlusion surgical procedure
The surgical procedure was performed according to the manual described previously [15 ]. 
Anesthesia was induced by intramuscular injection of Zoletil (50 mg/kg; Virbac, France). 
The right common carotid artery (CCA) was exposed and isolated from nearby nerves and

3-0 black silk and disinfected with povidone-iodine. Neurobehavioral tests were carried out 
24 h after occlusion and animal were immediately sacrificed.
Neurological deficit scoring test
The animals were grouped into five groups by the following criteria: 1) score 0, no 
neurological impairment with a normal posture; 2) score 1, incomplete extension of the 
forelimb on the left side; 3) score 2, involuntary circling to the left side; 4) score 3, falling to 
the left side, seizures; and 5) score 4, lack of movement or consciousness.
Corner test
Two boards (30 × 20 × 1 cm 3 ) were placed at an angle of 30° and rats were placed in the space 
between the boards. As the rats moved towards the corner and turned to left or right. The 
number of turns was recorded in 10 trials and the interval for this test was 30 sec. Animals 
underwent a seven-day training period prior to MCAO surgery, in which animals with the 
same number of right and left turns were used for corner tests.
Grip strength test
We used grip strength meter (Jeungdo-Bio and Plant Co., Ltd., Korea) to measure grip 
strength. The animals were placed on a grip test device and gripped the wire grid of the 
device with their front paws. The animals were slowly pulled back until they let go of their 
grips and the measured maximum force was recorded. Three tests were performed on each 
animal, and the average value was considered as a result.
Vibrissae-evoked forelimb placing test
The evaluation of vibrissae-induced forelimb placement was performed to measure 
sensorimotor aptitude [16 ]. Animals were allowed to grip tightly around the torso and 
move freely on both legs. As described manual, the right or left vibrissae of animals were 
brushed on the edge of a table. Upon stimulation of the vibrissae, the rats placed exactly 
corresponding forelimbs. Since the vibrissae sensory input is processed by the ipsilateral 
somatosensory cortex and induces a forelimb placement response on the same side, damage 
to the corresponding hemisphere impairs both the sensory input and the ipsilateral forelimb 
response. Each animal underwent 10 trials and recorded the success rate of the forelimb 
placement response for both right and left forelimb responses. Animals were trained for this 
assessment prior to MCAO surgery.
ROS assay
The 2 ′,7 ′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) has been used to measure 
intracellular ROS. We used a ROS assay kit and processed according to manufacturer’s 
manual (Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., USA). The tissue was finely cut, reacted with an 
enzyme solution at 37°C for 30 min, rinsed with PBS, and reacted with 5 mM DCFH-DA 
at 37°C for 1 h. After centrifuging at 1,000 g for 10 min, the pellets were rinsed with PBS

LPO analysis
Malondialdehyde (MDA) has been used to measure LPO levels in the cerebral cortex, it is

acid, reacted at 95°C for 1 h, and cooled at 4°C for 10 min. The optical density at 532 nm 
wavelength was measured by spectrophotometer. Results were expressed in relative MDA 
pmol/mg of the protein.
2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining
Brain tissues were separated from the skull and were sectioned coronally at 2 mm intervals 
using a brain matrix (Doccol). These sections were reacted in TTC (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved 
in a phosphate buffer solution at 37°C for 10 min. They were fixed in a 4% neutral buffered 
paraformaldehyde solution for 4 h, sequenced according to brain regions, and digitally 
scanned. Quantitative analysis of intact and damaged areas was performed using Image 
J software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The percentage of infarcts was calculated 
according to the following formula:
 Infarct Percentage = (Infarct Volume/Total Slice Volume) × 100.
Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining
The tissue was immersed in a 4% neutral buffered paraformaldehyde solution for 48 h, rinsed 
with tap water, and dehydrated step by step in 70% to 100% ethanol. The tissue was replaced 
with paraffin (Leica, Germany) using the Leica EG1160 paraffin embedding center (Leica) 
and the paraffin block were cut using HistoCore AUTOCUT (Leica). These sections were 
stained according to the general manual of H&E staining. The stained tissue was covered with 
a cover glass and observed with a Leica microscope (Leica). Five fields of the cortical area 
were selected and the results were shown as a percentage of the number of damaged cells and 
the total number of cells.
Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay
ApopTag peroxidase in situ apoptosis detection kit (Merck, USA) was used to detect apoptotic 
cells. The sections were reacted with proteinase K (20 µg/mL) and rinsed with PBS. They were 
immersed in 3% hydrogen peroxide diluted with methanol for 5 min to inhibit endogenous 
peroxidase activity. After washing with PBS, they were incubated with equilibration buffer at 
4°C for 1 h, continuously incubated with terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase enzyme and 
reaction buffer mixture at 37°C for 90 min, and covered with stop buffer for 10 min. They were 
reacted with anti-digoxigenin conjugate for 1 h, reacted with 3,3 ′-diaminobenzidine (DAB; 
Sigma-Aldrich), and rinsed with tap water. They were counter stained with hematoxylin 
solution and mounted with a permount mounting medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific). We 
selected five fields in the cerebral cortical regions and counted TUNEL positive cells.

Western blot analysis
The right cerebral cortex was carefully collected and homogenized with lysis buffer 
containing phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (Sigma-Aldrich). Lysates were sonicated for 30 
sec and centrifuged at 15,000 g for 1 h at 4°C. The supernatants were carefully collected and 
the concentration of protein was measured using bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Pierce, 
USA). Total proteins (30 µg) from each sample were lysis with sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) 
buffer, heated at 100°C for 15 min, and cooled to room temperature. SDS polyacrylamide gel 
(12%) was used and sample was run at 10 mA for 90 min and transferred to a polyvinylidene 
fluoride membrane (GVS Filter Technology, USA) at 120 V for 2 h. The membranes were 
blocked with 5% skim milk solution in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) 
for 1 h, washed 10 min with TBST, and reacted for overnight at 4°C with specific primary 
antibodies: anti-Bcl-2, anti-Bax, anti-caspase 3, and anti- β-actin (diluted 1:1,000; Cell 
Signaling Technology, USA). They were washed with TBST and were reacted with horseradish 
peroxide-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (diluted 1:5,000; Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 h. 
They were incubated with enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (GE Healthcare, UK). The 
image was measured using ChemiDoc Image System (Bio-Rad, USA) and the density of band 
using Image J software. Results showed a ratio of relative density of specific protein to density 
of β-actin.
Immunofluorescence staining
The deparaffinized sections were rinsed with PBS and incubated with 1% normal goat serum 
for 1 h. They were reacted primary antibodies: anti-Bcl-2, anti-Bax, and anti-caspase-3 
rabbit IgG (diluted 1:100; Cell Signaling Technology). After incubation, they were washed 
with PBS and incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate-conjugated secondary antibody 
(1:100 dilution; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h. The sections were washed with PBS, 
stained with 4 ′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min, mounted 
using a fluorescent mounting medium, and examined with a confocal microscope (FV- 1000; 
Olympus). The results were expressed as a ratio of the number of caspase-3 positive cells to 
that of DAPI positive cells.
Data analysis
All experiment data were presented as the mean ± standard error of means. The results of 
each group were compared by two-way analysis of variance and post-hoc Scheffe’s test. The p 
value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Antioxidative effect of baicalin administration in MCAO animals
ROS and LPO levels were quantified using 2′,7 ′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCF) and MDA 
assays to evaluate oxidative stress. MCAO animals exhibited elevated DCF levels, which were 
significantly reduced by baicalin treatment. The DCF levels was 3.65 ± 0.19 pmol/mg protein in 
the vehicle + MCAO animals and 2.63 ± 0.16 pmol/mg in the baicalin + MCAO animals ( Fig. 2A ).  
MDA levels was increased in MCAO animals, this elevation was mitigated by baicalin 
treatment. MDA levels was 3.18 ± 0.31 pmol/mg protein in the vehicle + MCAO animals and 
2.46 ± 0.11 pmol/mg in the baicalin + MCAO animals ( Fig. 2B ).
Mitigation of infarction by baicalin administration in MCAO animals
A significant increase in infarct volume was observed in animals subjected to MCAO. 
However, baicalin administration attenuated the infarct volume compared to that in 
the vehicle-treated group (Fig. 2C ). Sham-operated animals exhibited no infarct region. 
Quantitative analysis revealed infarct volumes of 38.4% ± 5.15% and 21.7% ± 4.35% in the 
vehicle + MCAO and baicalin + MCAO groups, respectively (Fig. 2D ).
Mitigation of histopathological damage and apoptotic cells by baicalin 
administration in MCAO animals
Histopathological changes in MCAO animals were assessed using H&E staining (Fig. 3A ). 
Sham animals had normal cell morphology characterized by large and round nuclei, distinct 
nucleoli, and dense cytoplasmic structures. However, MCAO animals exhibited damaged 
neurons characterized by contracted or distended cell shapes, nuclear condensation, and 
loss of nucleoli. Baicalin ameliorated MCAO-induced histopathological alterations. The 
percentage of damaged cells was 78.23% ± 5.75% in vehicle + MCAO animals and 50.88% ± 
6.92% in baicalin + MCAO animals ( Fig. 3C ). Additionally, TUNEL histochemical staining 
was performed to detect apoptosis in the cerebral cortex (Fig. 3B ). The number of TUNEL-
positive cells was significantly higher in vehicle + MCAO animals compared to sham animals. 
However, these increases were significantly attenuated in baicalin + MCAO animals. The 
percentage of apoptotic cells was 90.15% ± 6.31% in vehicle + MCAO animal and 49.69% ± 
4.13% in baicalin + MCAO animals ( Fig. 3C ).

DISCUSSION
Ischemic stroke leads to cognitive impairment and hemiplegia, which leads to irreversible 
neurological disorders [18 ,19 ]. We clearly confirmed that MCAO damage results in significant 
neurobehavioral deficits. MCAO surgery is a widely used animal model for studying stroke. 
We demonstrated that baicalin significantly reduces neuronal damage induced by MCAO, 
as evidenced by various neurological behavioral tests. Baicalin effectively ameliorated 
sensorimotor impairment and body imbalance caused by MCAO. A previous study also 
demonstrated the neuroprotective effects of baicalin on neurological deficit scores in rats 
[20 ]. We extended the scope of the analysis using a wider range of behavioral tests, including 
neurological deficit score test, corner test, vibrissae-induced forelimb placement test, and 
grip strength test. Our results clearly highlight the protective effects of baicalin by improving 
neurological behavioral disorders.
Oxidative stress is a critical factor in the pathophysiology of ischemic stroke [21 ]. MCAO 
induces blockage of blood flow, deprivation of oxygen and nutrients, and subsequent energy 
deficiency, leading to neuronal cell death [22 ]. Ischemic stroke not only increases oxidative 
stress and pyroptosis but also activates neuroinflammation in microglia [23 ]. It also increases 
superoxide dismutase activity [23 ]. Excessive production of ROS induces oxidative stress, 
leading to cellular dysfunction, including DNA damage and membrane lipid peroxidation, 
subsequently triggers caspase activation and apoptosis [24 ]. Baicalin inhibits ROS production 
and stabilizes glutamine synthase under oxidative stress induced by glutamate exposure, 
thereby reducing excitotoxicity [25 ]. In particular, the reduction of LPO by baicalin suggests 
that baicalin effectively preserves membrane integrity by decreasing oxidative damage 
to polyunsaturated fatty acids, which play a key role in neuronal cell membranes [26 ]. 
LPO is recognized as a critical mechanism of oxidative damage in neuronal cells, directly 
disrupting membrane integrity and generating various secondary reactive compounds that 
amplify neuronal injury [ 27 ]. MDA, a major reactive aldehyde generated during LPO, leads 
to structural alterations and functional impairments, thereby contributing to neuronal 
dysfunction and neurodegeneration due to the high susceptibility of neuronal membranes 
to oxidative damage [28 ]. Our results demonstrated that baicalin treatment mitigated ROS 
accumulation and LPO during MCAO damage. These findings support the fact that baicalin 
acts as a potent antioxidant in ischemic stroke models.
Cerebral ischemia induces irreversible brain damage through complex pathophysiological 
mechanisms [29 ]. It degrades tight junction proteins, compromises BBB integrity, and 
leads to brain edema and nerve damage [30 ]. Baicalin decreases matrix metalloproteinase-9 
expression and decreases BBB permeability following MCAO, thereby reducing cerebral edema 
[31 ]. We showed that baicalin administration ameliorated infarction and histopathological 
damage caused by MCAO damage. In addition, TUNEL histochemical staining was conducted 
to evaluate apoptosis. MCAO animals showed an increased number of TUNEL-positive cells 
in the ipsilateral cerebral cortex, indicating that MCAO surgery induces apoptosis. However, 
the administration of baicalin alleviated this increase and reduced apoptosis by MCAO, 
demonstrating the neuroprotective effect of baicalin.
