29 Abstract
30 This study aimed to compare the effects of an 8-week Creatine (Cr) or placebo (PL) 
31 supplementation on muscle strength, thickness, endurance, and body composition employing 
32 different training paradigms with blood flow restriction (BFR) vs. traditional resistance training 
33 (TRAD). Seventeen healthy males were randomized between the PL (n = 9) or Cr (n = 8) groups. 
34 Participants were trained unilaterally utilizing a within-between subject bicep curl exercise where 
35 each arm was allocated to TRAD or BFR for eight weeks. Muscular strength, thickness, 
36 endurance, and body composition were evaluated. Creatine supplementation promoted increases 
37 in muscle thickness in TRAD and BFR, compared to their placebo counterparts, however, without 
38 significant difference between treatments (p=0.349). TRAD training-induced increases in 
39 maximum strength (1RM) compared to BFR after eight weeks of training (p = 0.021). Repetitions 
40 to failure at 30% of 1RM were increased in the BFR-Cr group compared to TRAD-Cr (p = 0.004). 
41 Repetitions to failure at 70% 1RM were increased from weeks 0-4 (p<0.05) and 4-8 (p<0.05) in 
42 all groups. Creatine supplementation exerted a hypertrophic effect when utilized with TRAD and 
43 BFR paradigms and increased muscle performance at 30% 1RM when utilized in conjunction 
44 with BFR. Therefore, creatine supplementation seems to amplify muscle adaptation following a 
45 BFR program.

55 Introduction
56
57 Increases in muscular strength and hypertrophy are important adaptations associated with 
58 resistance training that can translate to clinically meaningful outcomes in various populations, 
59 including athletes, the elderly, and the general population (Fry, 2004). The American College of 
60 Sports Medicine recommends resistance training with intensities over 70% of 1 repetition 
61 maximum (1RM) to optimize strength and hypertrophic adaptations (Ratamess, 2009). However, 
62 several studies have demonstrated that low-load (30% 1RM) training with blood flow restriction 
63 (BFR) is capable of producing similar improvements in muscular strength and hypertrophy as 
64 traditional high-load resistance training (TRAD) (Farup et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2017). For 
65 example, in a seminal study by Takarada et al. (2000), high-load training elicited increases in 
66 muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) and strength similar to that observed with low-load BFR. 
67 Notwithstanding, a frequent finding in the literature for low-load training is reduced gains in 
68 maximal strength compared to high-load, which seems to be due to lower neural changes 
69 (Duchateau et al., 2021). 
70 Regarding the mechanisms involved in such a response, it is clear that BFR significantly 
71 increases muscle protein synthesis and robustly activates the mTORC1 compared to a similar 
72 exercise performed in a free-flow manner (Gundermann et al. 2012). Factors involved in such a 
73 response are the accumulation of lactate, which has been shown to increase muscle hypertrophy 
74 in muscle cells (Ohno et al. 2018), the accumulation of H + ions resulting in increased muscle 
75 fatigue and subsequent type II fiber recruitment, increased venous pooling leading to increased 
76 cell swelling and possibly intracellular factors reinforcing protein synthesis (Cholewa et al. 2018; 
77 Suga et al. 2012; Petrick et al. 2019).
78 The principles of exercise biochemistry indicate that exercises performed with high loads 
79 heavily rely on phosphocreatine metabolism for energy production. In contrast, low-load 
80 exercises heavily rely on glycolysis for energy production (Sahlin et al., 2014). Although this is 
81 still a valid assumption, an exception occurs during low-load BFR exercises, where muscle 
82 phosphocreatine is robustly consumed, indicating a shift in energy metabolism compared to free-

83 flow exercises, possibly indicating a mismatch between phosphocreatine usage and resynthesis 
84 (Harris et al., 1976). Regarding the latter, Suga et al. (2012) observed that during BFR sets, 
85 phosphocreatine is consumed (although to a lesser extent if compared to a TRAD exercise). Still, 
86 it is not restored during the rest interval between sets, mainly because of muscle hypoxia (Harris 
87 et al., 1976). The final result of usage and lack of resynthesis is a progressive reduction of muscle 
88 phosphocreatine levels through multiple sets with BFR, which may be involved with the 
89 decreased muscle force response observed at the end of a BFR protocol (Suga et al., 2012).
90 Resistance training practitioners have used creatine supplementation due to its ability to 
91 increase creatine and phosphocreatine levels (Harris et al., 1992), thus enhancing muscle strength 
92 and muscle mass levels (Branch, 2003). Since decreased phosphocreatine levels are related to 
93 fatigue during BFR exercises (Suga et al., 2012), creatine supplementation would be an interesting 
94 alternative for enhancing muscle adaptations following a BFR program. Only one study 
95 investigated the interactions between BFR and creatine supplementation to the best of our 
96 knowledge. In this study, Medrano et al. (2019) did not observe any additional effect of creatine 
97 supplementation on BFR regarding increasing maximal isometric voluntary strength or muscle 
98 thickness in the elbow flexors. From this study, some important methodological questions arise: 
99 1) the lack of specificity of the isometric contractions to evaluate the effects of dynamic training; 
100 and 2) the short-term nature of this study (3 weeks), which seems more appropriate to study neural 
101 adaptations, given that muscle hypertrophy is a relatively slow process (Damas et al., 2016). 
102  Thus, the primary purpose of this study was to compare, in a time-course and double-
103 blind placebo-controlled study, the effects of 8 weeks of creatine supplementation during BFR 
104 (30% 1RM) or TRAD (70% 1RM) on dynamic muscle strength and muscle thickness. A within-
105 subject design and unilateral elbow flexion resistance training program was employed. Volume 
106 load of training (VLT [i.e., Sets x Number of Repetitions x Load]), 1 Repetition Maximum, 
107 repetitions to failure at 30% and 70% of 1RM, muscle thickness, and body composition were 
108 evaluated at baseline, four weeks, and eight weeks. We hypothesized that creatine 
109 supplementation would improve all supra-cited muscle performance variables in both resistance 
110 training paradigms compared to placebo controls.

112 Methods
113 Experimental Approach to the Problem
114 This study used a randomized, double-blind design. Those not directly involved in the data 
115 collection generated the random allocation sequence to ensure blinding. Participants were pair-
116 matched based on initial arm circumference and strength levels and then randomly assigned to 
117 one of two treatment groups: Placebo (PL) or Creatine (CR). Each arm was randomly assigned to 
118 either a blood flow restriction (BFR) or traditional (TRAD) resistance training condition. 
119 Participants were assessed for anthropometric measures and arterial occlusion pressure (AOP) on 
120 the first visit. During visit two, participants performed an elbow flexion 1 RM; during visits four 
121 and five, the repetitions to failure test with 30% and 70% of 1RM were performed within 48 hours 
122 between tests. Repetitions to failure, strength tests, muscle thickness, and body composition were 
123 also performed after four and eight weeks of the intervention. Training occurred for eight weeks, 
124 with each arm being trained twice weekly on separate days, and each session separated by 72 
125 hours. The total number of repetitions performed and load lifted was recorded for each set, and 
126 the VLT was calculated.

150 Procedures
151 Anthropometric Measurements, Body Composition, and Dietary Intake Assessments
152 Body mass was measured using an electronic scale (Filizzola PL 50, Filizzola Ltda., Brazil) 
153 with a precision of 0.1 kg. Height was measured on a fixed stadiometer (Sanny, São Paulo, Brazil) 
154 with an accuracy of 0.1 cm and a length of 2.20 m. The arm circumference was measured with a 
155 relaxed arm along the body, at a distance two-thirds distal to the acromial and olecranon at the 
156 largest portion of the volunteer's biceps. From the results obtained, the average error was 
157 estimated as follows: 0.04 cm for arm circumference, and the intraclass correlation was 0.98 to 
158 0.99.

161 the manufacturer’s user manual and procedures adopted (Schoenfeld et al., 2019). Total fat-free 
162 mass (FFM) was then estimated by subtracting fat mass from body mass (Total body mass minus 
163 total fat mass = FFM), utilizing the Jackson & Pollock three folds protocol (thigh, chest, and 
164 abdominal).

171 Determination of Arterial Occlusion Pressure
172 The cuff used to determine the total occlusion pressure was the 6 cm wide (Nylon) model 
173 (JPJ, Brazil). For the determination of Arterial occlusion of pressure (AOP), the cuff was placed 
174 near the glenohumeral joint. The pulse was initially detected using a portable Doppler probe 
175 (MEDMEGA, DV 610V), positioned on the radial artery, and subsequently inflated to 50 mmHg 
176 with a manual sphygmomanometer (MISSOURI, Brazil) following the same protocol as 
177 Loenneke et al., (2012). These procedures' re-test intraclass correlation coefficient proved reliable 
178 (ICC= 0.91).
179
180 Muscle Thickness 
181 A single-trained ultrasound technician obtained muscle thickness images of the elbow 
182 flexor using an A-mode ultrasound imaging unit (Bodymetrix Pro System; Intelametrix Inc., 
183 Livermore, CA, United States). Following applying a water-soluble transmission gel (Mercur 
184 S.A. - Body Care, Santa Cruz do Sul, Brazil) to the measurement site, a 2.5-MHz linear probe 
185 was positioned perpendicularly to the tissue interface without depressing the skin. All 
186 measurements were performed at the same point corresponding to 70% of the distance between 
187 the acromial process and the lateral epicondyle, respectively, as Kim et al. (2017) described. The 
188 images recorded in the computer were analyzed using the software Body View Pro-Fit 
189 (Bodymetrix Pro System; Intelametrix Inc., Livermore, CA, United States), and muscle thickness 
190 dimensions were obtained by the same ultrasound technician measuring the distance from the 
191 subcutaneous adipose tissue–muscle interface to the muscle-bone interface, as described by 
192 Brigatto et al., 2019. To help ensure that swelling in the muscles from training did not obscure 
193 results, images were obtained 48-72 hours before the commencement of the study and after the 
194 final training session. These procedures' re-test intraclass correlation coefficient proved reliable 
195 (ICC = 0.99).

197 Dynamic Strength Test
198 One maximum repetition strength test (1RM): A warm-up set of 10 repetitions was 
199 performed with an unloaded 2 kg dumbbell rod. To determine the 1RM value of unilateral elbow 
200 flexion, subjects followed the test protocol according to the study by Yasuda et al., (2015). During 
201 the test, participants had their heels and backs resting on the wall, and feet separated by shoulder 
202 width to ensure positional standardization.
203 Repetitions to failure test: Subjects performed one set to muscular failure of the unilateral 
204 elbow flexion exercise with a load corresponding to 30% and 70% of the 1RM (obtained during 
205 basal measurements), respectively, with a constant speed controlled by a metronome (2-s 
206 eccentric and 2-s concentric) and 48 hours between tests. The total number of repetitions 
207 performed was recorded.
208
209 Supplementation Protocol 
210 Initially, participants ingested 20 g of creatine (Creatine monohydrate Powder, 
211 Integralmedica®, Embu-Guaçu, Brazil) or a placebo (Maltodextrin – Integralmedica®, Embu-
212 Guaçu, Brazil) in 5 g doses four times per day (breakfast, lunch, snack, and dinner) for five days 
213 (Hultman et al., 1996). Next, each subject consumed either 5 g of Creatine or a placebo 
214 immediately post-exercise for the remaining eight weeks. On the non-training days (three days 
215 per week), participants took the supplement home and were instructed to consume it at the same 
216 time as their regular training. A nutritionist delivered the supplement upon the presentation of 
217 empty packs by the subjects, and this professional was not part of the research team. Both creatine 
218 and placebo were in powder form and mixed by the nutritionist with 1 g of strawberry flavor and 
219 250 ml of water (Clight Juice Kraft Foods Brazil - 200 mL: energy 21 kJ, 5 kcal, sodium 4,7 mg) 
220 to produce a taste, color, texture, and appearance that were indistinguishable between creatine 
221 and placebo. No subject reported any side effects of creatine supplementation (i.e., diarrhea or 
222 cramps).

225 Initially, the subjects were introduced to the laboratory, after which they were instructed 
226 on the exercise to be used in the study. They were then familiarized with the BFR and TRAD 
227 dumbbell biceps curl exercises. The training protocol was randomly selected for each subject’s 
228 arm, each arm was trained twice weekly, and each separate protocol was performed on non-
229 consecutive days (i.e., BFR: Monday and Thursday, and TRAD: Tuesday and Friday). Subjects 
230 were instructed to perform each repetition starting with a fully extended elbow, flexing at the 
231 elbow to full flexion, and then returning to full arm extension (May et al., 2018). Repetition time 
232 was controlled by a metronome, with 2 seconds for concentric and 2 seconds for eccentric. The 
233 training program consisted of two progressive 4-week phases: 1 st to 4 th week, for BFR, the 
234 subjects performed one set of 30 repetitions and two sets of 15 repetitions with 30% of 1RM and 
235 30 seconds of rest; For TRAD, three sets of 10 to 12 repetitions with 70% of 1RM and 120 seconds 
236 of rest were performed. After four weeks, from the 5 th to 8 th week of training, the BFR arm 
237 performed one set of 30 repetitions and three sets of 15 repetitions with 30% of 1RM and 30 
238 seconds of rest; and, for TRAD, four sets of 10 to 12 repetitions with 70% of 1RM and 120 
239 seconds of rest. Following the 4 th week, the 1RM was reassessed, and the load used in weeks 5 
240 through 8 was calculated based on the updated 1RM. For BFR, the subjects were equipped with 
241 a cuff to be placed near the glenohumeral joint and then inflated to a pressure corresponding to 
242 50% of the AOP of the arm blood flow. The occlusion pressure was maintained throughout the 
243 exercise session, including rest intervals, and released only at the end. For TRAD, the intensity 
244 was controlled by the zone of repetition, and subjects needed to complete the movement failure 
245 with no more than 12 and not less than 10 repetitions. For BFR, sets were conducted for concentric 
246 movement failure, and subjects failed at every set (Farup et al., 2015).

267 necessary. The data were analyzed using Statsoft Statistic software (version 10). Data are

355 Discussion
356 The main objective of this study was to examine if creatine supplementation would benefit 
357 a low-load/high-repetition resistance training paradigm with BFR, compared to a traditional high-
358 load/low-repetition resistance training paradigm regarding muscular adaptations. Our results 
359 demonstrated for the first time that eight weeks of creatine supplementation during BFR training 
360 enhanced the exercise volume load of training (VLT) compared to the BFR placebo. In some 
361 variables, such as repetitions to failure at 30% 1RM, the BFR creatine-supplemented group 
362 performed better than the BFR placebo, TRAD placebo, and the TRAD creatine-supplemented 
363 group. Regarding the muscle thickness response, creatine supplementation promoted increases in 
364 muscle thickness in both paradigms, TRAD and BFR, compared to their placebo counterparts. 
365 However, the increases were not statistically significant, which can be partially explained by the 
366 limited number of subjects. On the other hand, mean values from this study were in line with data 
367 from a previously published creatine study on TRAD (Candow et al. 2011), reinforcing the 
368 validity of such results. Our results suggest that creatine supplementation may be considered 
369 hypertrophic for both BFR and TRAD and ergogenic for the BFR under our experimental settings.

379 muscle thickness after eight weeks, indicating the efficacy of creatine for hypertrophy purposes. 
380 This magnitude of change in the elbow flexors is similar to Candow et al. (2011), who reported 
381 increases in elbow flexor muscle thickness of 6.3% and 16.4% for placebo and creatine, 
382 respectively, following 6 weeks of training. These results indicate that the TRAD method of 
383 training and creatine supplementation employed in this study was sufficient to increase muscle 
384 hypertrophy, similar to the greater body of research (Kreider et al., 2017).
385 To our knowledge, Kim et al. (2017) published the first study comparing low-load BFR to 
386 higher-load TRAD using a unilateral within-subjects design. In that study, 8 weeks of BFR and 
387 TRAD resulted in similar increases in elbow flexor hypertrophy, also observed in our 8-week 
388 study. Specifically, we found muscle hypertrophy of 9.1%, which is close to the 11.5% increase 
389 in 6 weeks presented by Farup et al. (2015) following a BFR program. In addition, in the present 
390 study, BFR training with creatine supplementation led to a 12.7% increase in muscle thickness. 
391 In a study performed by Medrano et al. (2019), it was demonstrated that BFR in association with 
392 creatine supplementation (3 weeks) did not enhance either bicep muscle thickness or maximal 
393 voluntary isometric strength compared with BFR alone. However, it should be noted that in this 
394 study (Medrano et al., 2019), the restriction pressure was 30% of AOP, and the rest between sets 
395 was 60 seconds, making the exercise protocol milder than ours. Interestingly, in our study, BFR 
396 groups (placebo versus creatine) were equalized to the number of repetitions and sets, making the 
397 only difference between both groups, the weight lifted per exercise session. Given the relationship

398 between load and hypertrophy (Radaelli et al., 2015), we hypothesize that greater rates of 
399 progressive overload with creatine supplementation likely presented a larger stimulus for muscle 
400 protein accretion, thus leading to greater muscle hypertrophy. Still, another known effect of 
401 creatine supplementation is to reduce markers of protein breakdown and such effects may have 
402 positively influenced our hypertrophy results (Parise et al. 2001).
403 Interestingly, in the TRAD paradigm, muscle hypertrophy was augmented by creatine 
404 supplementation (vs TRAD placebo group), despite no differences in VLT between groups. A 
405 possible explanation for the lack of increased VLT in the TRAD creatine group may be related to 
406 the task time of dynamic contractions. In the present study, TRAD subjects performed 10-12 
407 repetitions per set, within 4 seconds per repetition, totaling approximately 40 to 48 seconds spent 
408 in each set. According to a previous meta-analysis (Branch, 2003), creatine supplementation has 
409 the most pronounced effect on short-duration (< the 30s) high-intensity intermittent exercises. 
410 Thus, a possible explanation for the lack of creatine effects following this paradigm is that task 
411 time may have overreached the ideal range for the maximal performance benefits of creatine. 
412 Since VLT was not enhanced by creatine in the TRAD group, mechanisms other than 
413 increased exercise volume may have contributed to the increased hypertrophy response. Creatine 
414 supplementation has been shown to increase muscle differentiation in cell cultures and increase 
415 satellite cell activation in a murine model of compensatory hypertrophy (Dangott et al., 2000; 
416 Deldicque et al., 2007). Creatine supplementation has also been shown to increase muscle IGF-1 
417 mRNA expression (Deldicque et al., 2005) and decrease plasma leucine rate of appearance (an 
418 indirect marker of muscle protein breakdown) in humans (Parise et al. 2001). In addition, when 
419 associated with TRAD resistance training, creatine supplementation was shown to increase 
420 muscle mass and myofibrillar protein content (Willoughby et al., 2001), without affecting the 
421 ratio of skeletal muscle mass to intracellular fluid volume (Ribeiro et al. 2020). Altogether, physio 
422 pharmacological creatine effects, independently of enhanced muscle performance, should be 
423 taken into consideration since both, increased muscle thickness and increased lean mass (Table 
424 2), but not VLT was observed in this paradigm and treatment.

425 Creatine supplementation is widely known to be a performance enhancer (Casey et al., 
426 1996), mainly because of its properties to be transported and converted in the skeletal muscle 
427 tissue to phosphocreatine (Clark, 1997). At first glance, low-load training has little to do with 
428 phosphocreatine metabolism. However, as demonstrated previously during a multiple-set BFR 
429 session, phosphocreatine resynthesis is inhibited by blood flow restriction, resulting in the state 
430 known as metabolic freezing (Suga et al., 2012). Under this condition, phosphocreatine levels are 
431 not restored during the rest intervals between sets (as should occur in a low load free-flow 
432 exercise) and are utilized by the skeletal muscle during each set, therefore causing a progressive 
433 phosphocreatine decrement into a depletion state, which may be related with the decreased muscle 
434 force generated at the end of a BFR session (Suga et al., 2012). Thus, due to this specific aspect 
435 of BFR, we postulated that creatine supplementation could increase muscle performance and 
436 enhance muscle adaptations to this paradigm. To our results, when evaluated for a single set 
437 performed to the concentric failure at 30% of 1RM, the maximal number of repetitions performed 
438 in the BFR creatine group was significantly greater than BFR placebo or the TRAD groups at 8 
439 weeks. These findings are in line with our initial hypothesis that increased phosphocreatine stores 
440 may be related to increased work capacity in the BFR paradigm, which may be also related to the 
441 increased muscle hypertrophy response. 
442
443 Conclusion
444 The present study demonstrated multiple benefits of creatine supplementation in terms of 
445 muscle hypertrophy for both TRAD and BFR and muscle performance for BFR at 30% of 1RM 
446 and VLT. These specific synergistic improvements may have important implications not only in 
447 our model but also in rehabilitation or athletic programs, whereby BFR and creatine 
448 supplementation may be combined to augment muscular adaptations without heavy loading.

450 Limitations
451 Unilateral training has limitations mainly related to increases in muscle strength in the 
452 contralateral arm and this effect is more pronounced when this arm is kept untrained, which was

453 not the case in this investigation. As a positive factor, such an exercise model is known to decrease 
454 the variability between trained limbs, since factors such as nutrition, sleep, and supplementation, 
455 are delivered equally to both upper limbs (in our case). In this study, we employed a classical 
456 creatine supplementation schedule (saturation and maintenance), to promote the well-known 
457 benefits of creatine uptake and storage in the muscle. However, since we were not able to measure 
458 muscle or blood biochemical markers, the mechanisms involved in the results herein presented 
459 are not known. Future studies employing biochemical and/or molecular muscle fiber analysis are 
460 warranted to elucidate possible mechanisms of action on these specific and fascinating aspects of 
461 skeletal muscle and creatine metabolism.
