## Core Findings

Rapamycin, discovered in 1975 from soil bacteria on Easter Island, inhibits mTOR (mammalian Target of Rapamycin) -- a central nutrient-sensing pathway that toggles between anabolism and catabolism. Inhibiting mTOR mimics calorie restriction and extends lifespan in lab animals by 15-25%. The article reviews potential healthspan applications (immunosenescence, autophagy, atherosclerosis, neurodegeneration, oral health) while noting that human evidence for lifespan extension remains absent and side effects (glucose intolerance, immune suppression, wound healing impairment) are significant concerns.

## Key Evidence

### mTOR Mechanisms (Q1)
- mTOR senses nutrients (glucose, amino acids, oxygen, growth factors) and switches between anabolism (building) and catabolism (autophagy/breakdown)
- Two complexes: mTORC1 (inhibited by rapamycin; includes RPTOR, mLST8, PRAS40, DEPTOR) and mTORC2 (not well-inhibited by rapamycin; includes Rictor, mSIN1, Protor1/2)
- Rapalogs (rapamycin analogs) are being developed to better inhibit mTORC2

### Animal Studies (Q2)
- Lifespan extension of 15-25% in lab animals including mammals (lines 32-36)
- Rapamycin blocks/reverses cellular senescence in blood vessel cells, upregulating autophagy
- Animal studies show rapamycin blocks atherosclerotic plaque even with high cholesterol
- Mouse models of Alzheimer's show rapamycin targets tau pathology and improves blood-brain barrier function
- **Important caveat**: Rapamycin given after cognitive decline onset did NOT reverse decline in mice (line 233-237)
- Short-term rapamycin rejuvenated aged oral cavity in elderly mice -- regenerated periodontal bone, reduced inflammation, restored youthful oral microbiome (line 243-260)

### Human Clinical Evidence (Q3)
- No solid data from trials showing rapamycin extends lifespan in humans (line 123)
- Rapalog (not rapamycin) clinical trial in elderly: significantly decreased infection rate, upregulated antiviral gene expression, improved flu vaccine response over 1 year follow-up after 6-week trial
- Safety trial in adults aged 70-95: side effects minimal, BUT no significant effects on physical performance, cognitive enhancement, or immune function (line 281-283)
- No human clinical trials showing benefit for Alzheimer's disease (line 231)

### Dosing Tradeoffs (Q4)
- Timing and duration of dosing matter for glucose intolerance
- Rapamycin can raise blood glucose levels; metformin may counteract this
- Combination of rapamycin + metformin proposed for anti-aging but long-term safety unknown
- Low-dose regimens being explored for healthspan applications

### Translational Disagreement (Q5)
- Clear gap between robust animal data (15-25% lifespan extension) and absent human lifespan data
- Mouse Alzheimer's studies show benefit when given early, but harm/no benefit when given after cognitive decline -- translational timing concern
- Some researchers theorize mTOR inhibition with existing dementia may be harmful

### Adverse Effects (Q6)
- In renal transplant patients: altered wound healing, diarrhea, pneumonia risk, altered electrolytes
- Immune suppression (potentially serious in certain situations)
- Glucose intolerance / raised blood glucose levels
- Topical rapamycin (0.2% sirolimus cream) shows localized mTOR inhibition with benefit for skin lesions

### Immune Paradox (Q7)
- Rapamycin is traditionally immunosuppressive (used post-transplant)
- Yet low-dose rapalog in elderly IMPROVED immune function: decreased infections, improved antiviral gene expression, better flu vaccine response
- Immunosenescence (age-related immune decline) involves both innate (decreased interferon) and adaptive (decreased response to new pathogens) impairment
- Low-dose vs high-dose may explain paradoxical immune enhancement vs suppression
