# src-163: Impact of Rapamycin on Longevity: Updated Insights

**Authors:** Sanati M, Afshari AR, Aminyavari S, Banach M, Sahebkar A
**Venue:** Archives of Medical Science (Arch Med Sci)
**Year:** 2024 (published online July 25, 2024)
**DOI:** 10.5114/aoms/191565
**Type:** State-of-the-art review (comprehensive narrative review)
**Quality note:** Metadata flagged as "degraded" due to paywall detection, but full text was successfully extracted via raw PDF conversion. Content is substantive and authoritative.

## Core Findings

This is a comprehensive 2024 narrative review covering rapamycin's mechanisms of action for longevity extension across multiple organ systems. The paper synthesizes evidence from invertebrate and vertebrate models, detailing five major pro-longevity mechanisms downstream of mTORC1 inhibition: (1) improved protein synthesis fidelity and scope, (2) enhanced autophagic flux, (3) preserved mitochondrial function and biogenesis, (4) attenuation of inflammaging and immunosenescence, and (5) revitalization of adult stem cells. The authors conclude that clinical translation remains limited by adverse effects (especially metabolic disturbances from mTORC2 inhibition with prolonged use), unresolved sex-specific differences, and the need for optimal dosing regimens.

## Key Evidence by Research Question

### Q1: mTOR Mechanisms
- Detailed mechanistic review of mTORC1 and mTORC2 signaling pathways including upstream regulators (Rag/Rheb GTPases, TSC, AMPK, PI3K/Akt) and downstream effectors (4EBP, S6K1, ULK1, TFEB/TFE3).
- Rapamycin inhibits mTORC1 through FKBP12 binding; prolonged use also sequesters mTOR from mTORC2, causing insulin resistance.
- Five pro-longevity mechanisms elaborated: protein expression modulation (improving translational fidelity via eEF2K), autophagy induction (ULK1/ATG13 de-repression), mitochondrial function preservation (NAD+ redox balance, PGC1a/TFAM upregulation, mtDNA quality), inflammaging suppression (NF-kB inhibition, NLRP3 inflammasome suppression), and stem cell revitalization (restoring quiescence, promoting differentiation).

### Q2: Animal Studies
- Lifespan extension demonstrated in yeast, nematodes, fruit flies, and mice via mTORC1 inhibition.
- Comprehensive Table I catalogs preclinical findings across cardiovascular, nervous, gastrointestinal, hepatic, respiratory, renal, reproductive, and skeletal muscle systems with specific doses and durations.
- Key study: 3-month rapamycin (8 mg/kg) increased lifespan of middle-aged mice by up to 60% (Bitto et al., ref 222).
- Early-life rapamycin (42 ppm, first 45 days) increased median lifespan in UMHET3 mice while slowing growth and delaying reproductive age (Shindyapina et al., ref 223).
- Brief rapamycin in early adulthood produces lasting geroprotection via persistent intestinal autophagy increase (Juricic et al., Nature Aging 2022).

### Q3: Human Clinical Evidence
- Multiple clinical trials listed (Table II): doses range from 0.5 mg/day (long-term) to 2 mg/day (short-term).
- Phase II RCT (Kraig et al.): 1 mg/day rapamycin for 8 weeks in 11 older adults -- 5 reported facial rash, stomatitis, GI issues; blood markers (Hb, Hct, RBC) decreased but not clinically relevant; no significant cognitive, immune, insulin, or physical performance changes.
- Phase I trial: 0.5 mg/day for 12 weeks reduced SASP generation in elderly cardiac rehabilitation patients but failed to alleviate frailty.
- Active trials include NCT05237687 (0.5 mg/day, 1 year), NCT04488601 (5 mg/week, 1 year), NCT04742777 (1 mg/day, cardiac function), NCT02642094 (2 mg/day, breast cancer markers).
- No completed human longevity trials with lifespan endpoints yet.

### Q4: Dosing Tradeoffs
- Intermittent dosing (2 mg/kg once every 5 days) is the most commonly used regimen that preserves efficacy without glucose homeostasis disruption in mice.
- Biphasic (hormetic) dose-response: rapamycin is lethal at high doses but extends lifespan at low doses via partial (not total) mTOR inhibition.
- Long-term high-dose rapamycin (2 mg/day for 12 months) in LAM patients caused mucositis, diarrhea, nausea, hypercholesterolemia, acneiform rash, and lower extremity swelling.
- Low-dose rapamycin (0.5 mg/day for 24 weeks) in RA patients was well tolerated with no evaluable side effects.
- Clinical trials use <1 mg/day for long-term and up to 2 mg/day for short-term treatments.
- Blood level monitoring is important; maintaining lower trough concentrations may enhance tolerability but must be balanced against efficacy.

### Q5: Translational Disagreement
- Sex-specific differences create complexity: rapamycin extends longevity in female but not male Drosophila; mouse studies show sex differences in autophagy response across intestine, brown adipose tissue, and muscle.
- However, some mouse studies show the opposite (Strong et al.: 3-month rapamycin had greater effect on males; Shindyapina et al.: developmental rapamycin favored males).
- Proposed explanations: Rictor/mTORC2 disruption affects males more; females have naturally higher mTORC1 signaling so benefit more from inhibition; smaller sex may incur lower fitness cost.
- Evidence insufficient for definitive conclusions on sex-specific effects.
- Translation from animal models to humans remains uncertain due to limited clinical data.

### Q6: Adverse Effects
- Oral mucositis, GI illness, metabolic disorders, arthralgia, thrombocytopenia, anemia, renal toxicity, rash/eczema, delayed wound healing associated with rapamycin use in transplantation.
- Prolonged rapamycin inhibits mTORC2 assembly, causing insulin resistance, glucose intolerance, and abnormal lipid profiles (Lamming et al., Science 2012).
- Adverse events are largely dose- and time-dependent.
- Ten years of cardiac transplant data show association between blood levels and adverse events.
- In LAM: 12-month 2 mg/day caused significant AEs (mucositis, diarrhea, hypercholesterolemia).
- In TSC: 1 mg/day for 1 year caused oral aphthous ulcers, hypertriglyceridemia, microcytosis.

### Q7: Immune Paradox
- Dose-dependent immunological effects: higher doses cause immunosuppression; lower doses produce immunostimulation.
- Rapamycin suppresses pro-inflammatory T-cell lineage specification while expanding CD4+CD25+FoxP3+ regulatory T-cells (demonstrated in SLE patients after 1-year treatment).
- At low doses, rapamycin enhances memory CD8+ T-cell production and improves vaccine efficacy (BCG vaccine, Trypanosoma cruzi immunization).
- Brief high-dose rapamycin promotes CD8+ T-cell persistence after viral vaccination rather than effector maturation; extended high-dose suppresses memory responses.
- Short-term rapamycin enhances dendritic cell activation of naive CD8+ T-cells; long-term may cause autoimmunity.
- Rapamycin reduces chronic inflammation (inflammaging) via NF-kB inhibition but paradoxically may impair wound healing and increase infection risk (e.g., pneumocystis pneumonia in transplant patients).

## Methodology
- Narrative review (not systematic). No PRISMA flowchart or formal search strategy reported.
- Covers literature through mid-2024. Extensive reference list (245+ citations).
- Includes comprehensive preclinical evidence table (Table I) spanning multiple organ systems with doses, models, and outcomes.
- Includes clinical trial registry table (Table II) with active and completed trials.

## Limitations
- Narrative review without systematic methodology; potential for selection bias in cited studies.
- No quantitative meta-analysis or effect size calculations.
- Some dosing claims for mice are not directly compared head-to-head.
- Conflicting evidence on sex-specific effects is acknowledged but not resolved.
- Human clinical data remains preliminary (small sample sizes, short durations, surrogate endpoints rather than lifespan).

## Claims to Verify
- "Three-month rapamycin (8 mg/kg) administration caused a significant increase in the lifespan of middle-aged mice by up to 60%" (line 1511-1514) -- the "up to 60%" claim should be verified against the original Bitto et al. study, as this is a large effect size.
- "2 mg/kg once every 5 days" as the optimal intermittent dosing regimen (line 1495-1502) -- needs verification of whether this is truly the "most commonly employed" regimen across the field.
- "Five of the 11 subjects" in the Kraig et al. trial (line 1701) -- small sample, and the specific trial number and details should be cross-referenced.
