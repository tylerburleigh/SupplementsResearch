# src-073: Rapamycin Extends Murine Lifespan but Has Limited Effects on Aging

**Citation:** Neff F, Flores-Dominguez D, Ryan DP, et al. "Rapamycin extends murine lifespan but has limited effects on aging." *J Clin Invest.* 2013;123(8):3272-3291. doi:10.1172/JCI67674.

**Quality note:** Metadata flagged as "degraded" with paywall detection, but actual content is substantive full-text (~97K chars, 792 sentences). Content quality is high.

## Core Findings

1. **Rapamycin extended lifespan but did not broadly slow aging in male C57BL/6J mice.** This is one of the most comprehensive phenotypic assessments (>150 phenotypes across >25 tissues), finding that rapamycin's lifespan extension was dissociable from genuine anti-aging effects.

2. **Most "rescued" aging phenotypes were aging-independent drug effects.** When rapamycin was tested in young animals, it produced the same effects seen in aged animals for exploratory activity, learning and memory, thyroid follicle size, T cell subsets, body weight, and RBC counts -- indicating direct pharmacological effects rather than retardation of aging.

3. **Cancer suppression is the likely primary mechanism for lifespan extension.** Cancers are the leading cause of death in C57BL/6J mice; rapamycin significantly reduced cancers/precancerous lesions in the 16-month cohort (P = 0.0166). Reference to Miller et al. data: 95.6% of controls and 88.6% of rapamycin-treated animals died of neoplastic disease.

## Key Evidence

### Lifespan Extension
- Three cohorts: treatment starting at 4 months (n=20/group), 13 months (n=21/group), or 20-22 months (n=27/group)
- 1 year of treatment before analysis (at 16, 25, or 32-34 months)
- Rapamycin at 14 mg/kg food (microencapsulated, same protocol as Harrison et al. 2009)
- Cox proportional hazards: P = 0.0326, n = 68 per group (all cohorts pooled)

### Phenotypes NOT improved by rapamycin (Table 2)
- Motor coordination (rotarod)
- Grip strength and muscle fiber area
- Visual acuity and lens transparency (cataracts)
- Cardiac function (ejection fraction, fractional shortening)
- Hepatic periportal fibrosis and polyploidy
- Glomerulosclerosis
- O2 consumption and body temperature
- NK cell frequency
- Most clinical chemistry parameters

### Phenotypes "rescued" but shown to be aging-independent (Table 1)
- Exploratory activity: increased in both aged and young mice
- Learning and memory (Morris water maze, fear conditioning): enhanced in both aged and young mice
- Thyroid follicle size: decreased in both aged and young mice
- Heart dimensional measures: decreased in both aged and young mice
- RBC counts: increased in both aged and young mice
- Body weight/lean mass: decreased in both aged and young mice

### Cancer suppression
- 16-month cohort: vehicle 4/10 cancers vs rapamycin 0/15 (P = 0.0166)
- 25- and 34-month cohorts: no significant effect, but limited by small sample sizes and dropout from natural deaths

## Adverse Effects

- **Nephrotoxicity and gonadotoxicity**: described as "considerable"
- **Impaired glucose tolerance**: rapamycin exacerbated age-related glucose tolerance impairment
- **Hyperglycemia**: consistent with known mTOR inhibitor induction of insulin resistance
- **Hypercholesterolemia**: observed as side effect
- **Further reduction in muscle fiber area**: in at least one cohort
- **Possible worsening of cardiac function**: ejection fraction and fractional shortening trended lower in rapamycin-treated animals (not statistically significant)

## Methodology

- **Subjects:** Male C57BL/6J Rj mice (Janvier), single inbred strain, single sex
- **Dose:** 14 mg/kg food, microencapsulated rapamycin (same as Harrison et al. 2009)
- **Design:** Three age-of-onset cohorts + young control validation cohort
- **Assessment breadth:** >150 molecular, cellular, histopathological, and functional phenotypes across >25 tissues
- **Key methodological innovation:** Testing rapamycin effects in young animals to distinguish aging-independent drug effects from genuine anti-aging effects
- **Analysis:** Linear fixed-effects models with age and treatment factors; logistic regression for count data; 95% CI reported throughout

## Limitations

- Single inbred mouse strain (C57BL/6J) and single sex (male); effects may differ in females or other strains
- Relatively small sample sizes for some histopathological assessments in older cohorts due to natural attrition
- Could not assess rapamycin's effects on non-neoplastic causes of death (small fraction of deaths)
- Young-animal validation was not performed for all "rescued" phenotypes (e.g., hepatic microgranulomas, Ig concentrations)

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (mTOR mechanisms):** Tangential. Paper uses rapamycin as a tool but focuses on phenotypic outcomes rather than mTOR signaling mechanisms. Notes mTOR's role in carcinogenesis and lipid biogenesis.
- **Q2 (animal studies):** Highly relevant. One of the most comprehensive phenotyping studies of rapamycin in aging mice, with rigorous aging-independent controls.
- **Q3 (human clinical evidence):** Not directly relevant (mouse study only).
- **Q4 (dosing tradeoffs):** Relevant. Uses standard 14 mg/kg food dose; documents significant adverse effects (nephrotoxicity, gonadotoxicity, metabolic impairment) alongside lifespan benefits.
- **Q5 (translational disagreement):** Highly relevant. Central thesis challenges the assumption that rapamycin slows aging; argues lifespan extension is primarily through cancer suppression. This is a key dissenting voice in the field.
- **Q6 (adverse effects):** Highly relevant. Documents nephrotoxicity, gonadotoxicity, impaired glucose tolerance, hyperglycemia, hypercholesterolemia, and possible cardiac worsening.
- **Q7 (immune paradox):** Moderately relevant. Reports effects on T cell subsets (CD25+CD4+, CD44hi, gamma-delta T cells) but these are characterized as aging-independent effects. Reduced Ig levels in some cases.

## Claims to Verify

- **Sample sizes:** N = 68 per group for survival analysis seems precise (20 + 21 + 27 = 68). Verified from methods.
- **Cancer statistics:** 4/10 vs 0/15 with P = 0.0166 by Fisher exact test is plausible for those counts.
- **"95.6% of control animals" cancer deaths:** This statistic is attributed to Miller et al. 2011 (ref 10), not from this study's own data. Should be verified against the original source.
