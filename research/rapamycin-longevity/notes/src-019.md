## Core Findings

This is a skeptical consumer-health article summarizing the state of rapamycin for anti-aging use. It argues that current scientific evidence is insufficient to justify rapamycin supplementation for longevity in humans. The article reviews the drug's history (FDA-approved immunosuppressant since 1999), key clinical trials (Mannick 2014, PEARL), side effects, dosing approaches, and reports that some biological age tests suggest rapamycin may *accelerate* rather than decelerate aging.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Mouse Studies (Q2)
- Rapamycin extended lifespan of some mice by ~10%, but only when given early in development (slowing growth and limiting body size)
- German researchers found no significant benefit when rapamycin was given later in life

### mTOR Mechanisms (Q1)
- mTOR regulates cellular metabolism, growth, and proliferation
- Rapamycin inhibits mTOR, originally discovered as antifungal but switched to immunosuppressant use
- Hyper-activation of mTOR weakens the immune system; anti-aging advocates aim to inhibit hyper-activation
- Complex effects on immune system: IL-12 up/IL-10 down (immunostimulatory), TNF/IL-6 down (immunosuppressive)
- Duration and extent of mTORC1 vs mTORC2 inhibition not well understood

### Human Clinical Evidence (Q3)
- **Mannick 2014 trial:** 218 healthy elderly (65+), 6-week placebo-controlled, 4 groups (0.5 mg daily, 5 mg weekly, 20 mg weekly, placebo). 5 mg weekly group showed 20% enhanced flu vaccine response with virtually no side effects
- **PEARL trial:** 200 participants, 4 groups, started Jan 2020. Double-blind RCT results (Oct 2024) showed improvements in select biomarkers (muscle mass, reduced pain in females). However, 16 independent biological age tests concluded rapamycin accelerates aging
- 1,070 human clinical trials for rapamycin as of Jan 2023, 500+ completed (most not anti-aging specific)

### Dosing Tradeoffs (Q4)
- No established anti-aging dosage; off-label users aim for ~5 mg once weekly adjusted for body size
- Clinical trial dosing groups: 2.5 mg 3x/week, 5 mg 1x/week, 5 mg 2x/week, 10 mg 1x/week
- Sirolimus half-life: 63 hours (creates risk of overdose with frequent dose adjustments)
- Prescription drug (Rapamune by Pfizer); wholesale price >$3,000 for 100 x 2 mg tablets

### Translational Disagreement (Q5)
- Article's central thesis: scientific understanding is insufficient to justify rapamycin for anti-aging in humans
- Brian Johnson's science advisors dropped rapamycin from his protocol in 2025 due to lack of clear benefits and serious concerns
- Proponents believe mTOR inhibition and autophagy activation could help many degenerative diseases; article says this awaits clinical confirmation
- 636 known drug interactions with sirolimus (169 major, 459 moderate, 8 minor)

### Adverse Effects (Q6)
- At high doses, adverse reactions in >=30% of patients: peripheral edema, hypercholesterolemia, abdominal pain, headache, nausea, diarrhea, hypertriglyceridemia, hypertension, increased creatinine, fever, UTI, anemia, arthralgia, thrombocytopenia
- 5% treatment discontinuation rate
- Classified as immunosuppressant; serious adverse side effects for >30% at high doses

### Immune Paradox (Q7)
- Complex and partially contradictory immune effects
- Anti-aging users take low doses to avoid immunosuppression, attempting to immunomodulate by inhibiting mTOR hyper-activation
- Difficult to continuously monitor immune system to determine appropriate mTOR inhibition level
- No established way to measure if rapamycin produces desired anti-aging effects
