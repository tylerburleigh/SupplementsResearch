## Core Findings (Summary of Claims)

1. **mTOR mechanism (Q1):** Rapamycin targets mTOR (mammalian target of rapamycin), an enzyme activated by nutrients, growth factors, energy, and stress, that promotes cell growth and multiplication. mTOR is "inappropriately activated" in aging, cancer, and autoimmune disease. These are accurate but generic statements found in any review article.

2. **Animal studies (Q2):** Claims "dozens of studies since 2009" show lifespan and healthspan benefits in mice, including fewer age-related cancers, protection against cognitive decline, improved cardiovascular function, restoration of immune function, improved renal function, improved oral health, improved intestinal function, and preserved ovarian function. No specific studies or data cited.

3. **Human clinical evidence (Q3):** No human clinical trial data cited. The article is entirely anecdotal and mechanistic in its claims.

4. **Dosing tradeoffs (Q4):** States anti-aging dosing is "a fraction" of transplant doses — typically 2-6 mg per week in a single dose given once weekly (pulsatile/intermittent), versus daily dosing for transplant patients. Claims that at low pulsatile doses, rapamycin "can actually perform the opposite of its high dose effect and improve immune status."

5. **Adverse effects (Q6):** Lists transplant-dose side effects (hypercholesterolemia, hypokalemia, anemia, thrombocytopenia, hypertension, hypertriglyceridemia, kidney disease, etc.) and low-dose side effects (nausea, diarrhea, dizziness, back ache, sore throat, sweating, lack of appetite, runny nose). Notes that transplant patients receive rapamycin alongside other immunosuppressants, making it hard to isolate side effects.

6. **Immune paradox (Q7):** Claims that at lower intermittent doses, rapamycin improves rather than suppresses immune function — the opposite of its high-dose effect. This is stated without evidence or citation.
