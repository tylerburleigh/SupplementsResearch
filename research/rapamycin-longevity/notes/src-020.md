# src-020: Understanding Rapamycin: Benefits, Side Effects, and What the Research Shows

**Peer-reviewed:** No
**Quality assessment:** Non-academic; secondary reporting of published research

## Core Findings

This is a promotional article from a regenerative medicine clinic offering rapamycin therapy. It contains no original research but references several well-known studies (NIA 2009 mouse lifespan study, Novartis 2014 influenza vaccine response study, a 2020 GeroScience vascular function study). The content is primarily aimed at patient acquisition and should not be cited as a primary source for any claims.

## Key Evidence/Data Points

- References NIA Interventions Testing Program (2009, Nature): rapamycin extended mouse lifespan by up to 14% (females) and 9% (males), even when started late in life
- References Novartis/Science Translational Medicine (2014): low-dose mTOR inhibition improved immune response to flu vaccination in elderly by ~20%
- References a 2020 GeroScience study showing improved vascular function in older adults on low-dose rapamycin
- Lists common low-dose side effects: mouth sores (most common), mild GI discomfort, elevated lipids, mild glucose elevation
- States longevity dosing as 3-6mg once weekly, with blood work monitoring every 3-6 months

N/A -- this is not a research publication. The article does not describe any study methodology.

## Limitations

- **Not a primary source.** All cited findings are secondary reports of published research. The article does not link to DOIs or provide enough detail to verify specific claims.
- **Conflict of interest.** The article is written by a clinic that sells rapamycin therapy; it has a strong commercial incentive to present rapamycin favorably.
- **No quantitative rigor.** The "approximately 20%" immune improvement claim and lifespan percentages are reported without context (doses, durations, sample sizes).
- **Clinical protocol claims are anecdotal.** The dosing recommendations (3-6mg weekly) reflect the clinic's practice but are not attributed to published protocols.

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (mTOR mechanisms):** Tangential -- briefly describes mTOR inhibition, autophagy, inflammaging, and senescent cell clearance at a lay level. Not useful for mechanistic understanding.
- **Q2 (animal studies):** References the well-known NIA 2009 mouse study but adds no new data or analysis.
- **Q3 (human clinical evidence):** References the Novartis 2014 study and a 2020 GeroScience study but without primary data. Not citable for human evidence.
- **Q4 (dosing tradeoffs):** Describes intermittent weekly dosing rationale vs. continuous transplant dosing. Reflects common clinical practice but not from a controlled study.
- **Q5 (translational disagreement):** Not addressed.
- **Q6 (adverse effects):** Lists common side effects at low doses (mouth sores, GI issues, lipid/glucose elevation). Useful as a secondary confirmation of known side effect profiles but not primary evidence.
- **Q7 (immune paradox):** Briefly notes the paradox that high-dose rapamycin suppresses immune pathways while low doses may enhance immune response in older adults. References the Novartis 2014 study.

## Claims to Verify

- "14% in females and 9% in males" lifespan extension -- these are well-known figures from Harrison et al. 2009 (Nature) but should be verified from the primary source.
- "improved immune response to influenza vaccination in elderly patients by approximately 20%" -- this refers to Mannick et al. 2014 (Sci Transl Med); the exact percentage should be verified.
- "3-6mg once weekly" dosing -- this is a commonly cited longevity protocol dose but is not from a single published RCT.

This source should NOT be used as a primary citation for any claim. The side effect listing and dosing protocol description reflect common clinical practice knowledge.
