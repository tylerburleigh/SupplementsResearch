11 Abstract 
12 Aging is the gradual accumulation of structural and functional changes in an organism over time, including 
13 immune remodeling and a progressive increase in basal inflammation, or inflammaging. The mTOR 
14 pathway is a central driver of aging-related diseases, such as cancer, chronic inflammation and 
15 neurodegeneration; pharmacological inhibition with rapamycin is associated with reduced aged-related 
16 morbidity and increased lifespan across species. Nonetheless, concerns remain about the use of 
17 rapamycin, a well-established immunosuppressant in transplant medicine, as an anti-aging intervention. 
18 Here, we evaluated the impact of prolonged low-dose dietary rapamycin on the aging immune system. 
19 Treatment did not significantly alter innate or adaptive immune cell populations, including brain resident 
20 microglia; however, it attenuated the age-associated accumulation of IL-17–producing γδ T cells, 
21 particularly in the peritoneal cavity. After a peripheral inflammatory LPS challenge, circulating IL-17 levels 
22 were significantly reduced and correlated with an attenuation of microglia inflammatory phenotype. 
23 These findings suggest that prolonged low-dose rapamycin exposure exerts minor systemic immune 
24 changes, while selectively limiting age-related γδ T cell expansion and neuroinflammation associated with 
25 systemic inflammation.
26 Introduction
27 Aging is a process characterized by the progressive manifestation of hallmarks such as stem cell 
28 exhaustion, cellular senescence, chronic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction, leading to the loss of 
29 physiological homeostasis and increasing risk of death (1, 2). The mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) 
30 complex 1 (mTORC1) pathway fine-tunes a majority of these aging hallmarks (3). Therefore, significant 
31 attention has been dedicated to understanding the role of the mTORC1 pathway in aging. 
32 The serine-threonine kinase mTOR is activated by a broad range of stimuli, such as growth factors, 
33 cytokines and amino acids (4-6) and, when associated with other proteins to form mTOR complex 1, it 
34 integrates catabolic and anabolic signals to balance cell metabolism, survival, growth, proliferation, 
35 migration, differentiation and immune response (6). Given its critical role in regulating cell responses,

38 In the past two decades, numerous studies have investigated the potential of mTORC1 inhibitors as 
39 treatment for several cancers and as immunosuppressants (8-10). One of these inhibitors is rapamycin, 
40 the first TOR inhibitor to be identified (11). Rapamycin or Sirolimus has been widely used in 
41 transplantation for its immunosuppressive properties (12). Paradoxically, short-term rapamycin 
42 treatment has also been shown to enhance vaccine responses, an effect attributed in part to heightened 
43 myeloid cell activity (13-16). Beyond its immunomodulatory roles, renewed interest in rapamycin and 
44 mTORC1 inhibition was sparked by the discovery that rapamycin extends lifespan (17, 18), an effect 
45 thought to be mediated largely through suppression of cancer development (8). Nonetheless, while 
46 accumulating evidence suggests that rapamycin ameliorates certain aspects of age-associated 
47 inflammation, its overall impact on the aging immune system remains incompletely understood. Notably, 
48 literature remains elusive on how rapamycin modulates acute systemic inflammatory responses in aging, 
49 for example responses to systemic LPS. For these reasons, doubts remain on whether long-term in vivo 
50 rapamycin treatment, and therefore mTORC1 inhibition, would be a safe anti-aging approach or cause 
51 excessive immunosuppression. Indeed, in rats, rapamycin inhibited wound healing (19) and in Tuberous 
52 Sclerosis Complex patients, which have an aberrant hyperactivation of mTOR signaling, rapamycin led to 
53 an increase in dermatologic adverse events such as oral ulceration and acneiform eruptions (20). 
54 In this study, we aimed to understand how long-term, low-dose rapamycin could affect the ageing 
55 immune system under both basal and inflammatory conditions. To this end, mice were given a low dose 
56 of dietary rapamycin for a period of 5 months and immune cell proportions and inflammatory markers 
57 were then measured at steady state and upon and LPS challenge. We found that long-term treatment did 
58 not substantially alter immune cell populations, nonetheless, it counteracted the age-related increase of 
59 IL-17 producing γδ T cells and moderately blunted peripheral and brain-related inflammation. Together, 
60 our data reveal that long-term rapamycin treatment could be a potential intervention to modulate age-
61 associated IL-17-responses.

88 Rapamycin does not affect B cell populations in older mice
89 We next investigated if a rapamycin-containing diet could impact the lymphoid lineage, focusing first on 
90 B cells in the bone marrow, lymph nodes, peritoneal lavage, and blood ( Fig 2A). 
91 B-cell differentiation in the bone marrow was assessed by measuring cells expressing B220 and the -
92 chain of immunoglobulin M (IgM). No significant changes were observed in the proportions of pro-B cells, 
93 pre-B cells and immature B cells (Fig 2B). However, although not statistically significant, we noted a trend 
94 towards an increase in mature and transitional B cells in aged mice receiving rapamycin, resembling levels 
95 observed in young mice (Fig 2B ). 
96 Regarding naïve B cells in lymph nodes and spleen, no substantial differences were noticed across the 
97 three groups (Fig 2C). 
98 Finally, we investigated B1 and B2 cell populations in the spleen and peritoneal lavage based on IgM, 
99 CD11b and CD5 markers. Consistent with previous findings, no significant differences were detected 
100 across B cell subpopulations, including subsets of B1 cells such as B1a and B1b, in the spleen and in the 
101 peritoneal lavage ( Fig 2D).
102 Altogether, these results indicate that low-dose rapamycin administration has a minimal impact on B cells 
103 in aging.

105 Rapamycin-treated mice show minor changes in naïve and memory T-cell populations
106 Aging is associated with a decline in naïve T cell numbers and an increase in memory T cells (24, 25). In 
107 previous studies, rapamycin has been shown to increase differentiation of memory CD8 T cells (26, 27), 
108 however, mTOR-deficiency impairs differentiation of CD4 T cells to Th1, Th2 and Th17 subsets (28). 
109 Therefore, we assessed whether rapamycin treatment could influence the age-associated skewing of 
110 naïve to memory T cell populations. To this end, we assessed naïve (CD62L+CD44–), central 
111 (CD62L+CD44+) and effector memory (CD62L–CD44+) CD4 and CD8 T cells by measuring the expression 
112 of lymph node homing receptor, CD62L, a marker for naïve and central memory T cells, together with 
113 CD44, a marker of T cell memory and activation. Helper (CD4+) and cytotoxic (CD8+) T cells were assessed 
114 from lymph node, spleen and peritoneal lavage (Fig 3A). 
115 CD4+ T cells isolated from lymph node and spleen of rapamycin-fed mice showed a slight increase in naïve 
116 T cells in lymph nodes and spleen, as well as in central memory CD4+ T cells in the spleen, although 
117 differences did not reach statistical significance. 
118 No differences were observed for CD8⁺ T cells in the lymph node, albeit an increase was observed in CD8⁺ 
119 central memory T cells in the spleen of rapamycin-fed mice compared to both the young and old Eudragit 
120 groups (Fig 3E). This corroborates previous literature reporting that mTORC1 inhibition via rapamycin can 
121 induce an increase in the generation of CD8+ memory T cells (29). 
122 In the peritoneal lavage, CD4+ T cells showed a switch from naïve to effector T cells (Fig 3F&G), which 
123 appeared specific to the rapamycin treatment. To a certain extent, CD8+ T cells in the peritoneal lavage 
124 showed a similar trend, although it did not reach statistical significance.
125 Overall, these data show modest changes in T cell populations and differentiation to CD8+ T memory cells 
126 following rapamycin treatment, in line with previous reports.

128 Rapamycin diet induces a decrease in IL-17-producing γδ T cells 
129 While the effect of rapamycin on T cells expressing the main alpha/beta T cell receptor have been 
130 investigated, the effect on the less abundant gamma-delta T cell population, which express a type of T cell 
131 receptor that does not require MHC recognition, are not well described. In the context of cancer, 
132 rapamycin has been shown to enhance γδ T cell responses (30), however, the effect of long-term 
133 rapamycin administration on γδ T cell proportion remains unknown. For this reason, we next assessed 
134 whether rapamycin diet could have an effect on γδ  T cell subsets.

136 surface marker CD27 (31). CD3+, CD4–, CD8–, γδ TCR+, CD27+ and CD27– cells were assessed in the 
137 peritoneal lavage, as well as spleen and lymph node (Fig 4).

146 Rapamycin diet modulates microglia reactivity
147 Our data indicated that long-term rapamycin did not significantly affect the proportion of peripheral 
148 immune cells, except for a clear effect on a subset of IL-17-producing γδ T cells. 
149 Given these differences, we investigated whether systemic levels of inflammatory cytokines were also 
150 altered (Schematic in Fig 5A). To this end, serum cytokine levels were assessed, however, we did not 
151 observe any differences in serum concentration of IL-17A together with IL-6, IL-10, IL-27 (Fig 5B ). 
152 Nonetheless, since IL-17 levels can modulate neuroinflammation (32), we next investigated if the 
153 rapamycin diet affected the phenotype of central nervous system (CNS) tissue resident immune cells, 
154 microglia. To this end, we assessed the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and reactive microglial 
155 markers using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) in microglia cells. Although aging is known 
156 to induce upregulation of inflammatory cytokines and receptors in microglia, our data indicate that 
157 rapamycin did not counteract age-associated neuroinflammation (Fig 5C&D ). 
158 Although no changes at steady state were observed, we reasoned that systemic and brain inflammation 
159 might be affected in response to a strong inflammatory stimulus, such as intraperitoneal exposure to 
160 lipopolysaccharide (LPS). 
161 Thus, a separate cohort of mice were fed with either Eudragit or rapamycin diet from 6 months of age for 
162 5 months, then challenged with 2 mg/kg of LPS intraperitoneally and serum cytokine levels and microglia 
163 activation were analyzed 4 hours later.
164 Serum cytokines IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-23, IL-12p70, IFN-β, and IFN-γ were not different between the two diets 
165 (Fig 5E). However, the inflammatory cytokines IL-12p40, IL-17A and IL-27 were significantly reduced in 
166 rapamycin-fed mice upon LPS stimulation, along with the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Fig 5E). 
167 Examining the microglial response, we found that rapamycin administration moderately reduced 
168 inflammatory markers Tnf-α, Il6 and Tlr2 gene expression while others, such as Il-12a and Il-12b remained 
169 unaffected ( Fig 5F). 
170 Taken together, these results suggest that rapamycin diet can in part reduce systemic and brain 
171 inflammation in response to a strong systemic inflammatory stimulus.

173 Discussion
174 Hyperactivation of mTORC1 signaling has been associated with impaired metabolic health and reduced 
175 longevity across multiple species, including mice and humans (33-34). The detrimental effects of sustained 
176 mTORC1 activity may stem, at least in part, from remodeling of the immune landscape and its contribution 
177 to inflammation (35). Accumulating evidence demonstrates that attenuation of mTORC1 activity 
178 promotes healthy aging and extends lifespan (36–39). Nonetheless, the long-term consequences of 
179 chronic, low-level mTORC1 inhibition on the immune system remain incompletely understood. To address 
180 this, we tested the effect of low-dose, long-term rapamycin administration, based on the hypothesis that 
181 the resulting mild mTORC1 inhibition could remodel the immune landscape of mice and fine-tune their 
182 inflammatory status. 
183 In this study, we investigated how the peripheral immune system and the brain tissue-resident 
184 macrophages responded to a rapamycin-containing diet administered in adulthood (6-8months) or later 
185 in life (17-19 months). Our findings demonstrate that the treatment was well tolerated and overall safe; 
186 and while it did not induce major alterations in immune cell populations, it produced subtle yet significant 
187 beneficial effects on immune function in old mice.
188 We first examined the impact of the rapamycin-containing diet on myeloid cell populations in an aged 
189 cohort. Previous studies in adult mice have reported that rapamycin treatment can promote expansion of 
190 myeloid cells (40) and reshape their transcriptome under chronic conditions (41). In contrast, in our study 
191 of C57BL/6J mice, rapamycin supplementation did not significantly affect the abundance of myeloid cells 
192 across multiple compartments. Regarding the lymphoid lineage, likewise we observed minimal effects on 
193 the abundance of different subpopulations. However, we observed a downregulation of the CD27–, IL-17-
194 producing γδ T cells, more pronounced in the peritoneal lavage, where they have been implicated in 
195 cancer (42) and bowel inflammation (43). The decrease in CD27– γδ T cells could be linked to the role of 
196 mTORC1 in Th17 cell differentiation, mediated through the PI3K–mTORC1 axis (44–46).
197 As rapamycin has been proposed also as an anti-aging agent to preserve brain health and cognition, we 
198 were particularly interested in assessing the effect of systemic mTORC1 inhibition on brain-resident 
199 microglia. It is known that with age, microglia acquire a primed phenotype, characterized by increased 
200 reactivity to an inflammatory stimulus (47). In this study, we demonstrated that rapamycin attenuates the 
201 priming phenotype, since in response to a peripheral LPS challenge, the upregulation of inflammatory 
202 cytokines and receptors was reduced. Although modest, this reduction could prove beneficial to 
203 counteract brain inflammation that accompanies peripheral infections in the elderly (48). Our study did 
204 not determine the mechanism underlying the reduction in microglia priming, particularly since rapamycin 
205 does not efficiently cross the blood-brain barrier (49–51). However, reduced microglia reactivity is likely 
206 either mediated by reduced systemic inflammation or by modulation of the gut-brain axis, driven by 
207 rapamycin-induced alterations of the gut microbiome. Notably, even non-oral rapamycin treatment can 
208 affect the gut microbial composition (52), a core component of the gut–brain axis that has been identified 
209 as a contributor to alterations in microglial behavior (53-55). Indeed, upon LPS, we could observe 
210 diminished levels of circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-27 and IL-17. On the other hand, 
211 the immunomodulatory cytokine IL-10 was also decreased. This effect might be related to the decrease 
212 in inflammatory cytokines inducing a weaker feedback signal that upregulates anti-inflammatory 
213 responses (56).

214 Together, our results provide additional insights into how dietary rapamycin modulates the aged immune 
215 system: the absence of major changes in immune cell composition, combined with evidence of beneficial 
216 effects on CD27– γδ T cells, microglia and cytokine balance, supports the notion that rapamycin can be 
217 administered safely without causing overt immunosuppression. This is particularly relevant given the 
218 increasing clinical interest in rapamycin and rapalogs as geroprotective interventions (57, 58). Our data 
219 highlight the need to carefully dissect tissue-specific and age-dependent responses to mTORC1 inhibition, 
220 and they underscore that the beneficial effects of rapamycin in aging likely arise from subtle recalibrations 
221 of immune and inflammatory networks rather than extensive immune remodeling, at least at the dose 
222 investigated by us.
223 Further work is needed to determine whether these modest but significant immune effects translate into 
224 improved resilience to age-related pathologies, including neuroinflammation, infections, and systemic 
225 inflammation. Recent work suggest that peripheral immune signals may influence innate immune cells in 
226 the aged brain: IL-17 produced by γδ T cells may act on IL-17 receptors expressed by microglia and 
227 modulate their activation state (59). It has recently been shown that gut-derived γδ T17 cells migrate to 
228 the meninges, where IL-17A can alter the mitochondrial function of microglia and trigger downstream 
229 inflammatory pathways leading to microglial synaptic pruning in mouse models of encephalopathy (60). 
230 This raises the intriguing possibility that dietary rapamycin may exert some of its effects on brain microglia 
231 indirectly via modulation of peripheral γδ T cell responses, although further work is needed to test this 
232 hypothesis in the context of aging. In summary, our findings contribute to a growing body of evidence 
233 suggesting that dietary rapamycin is a safe and promising strategy for promoting healthy aging by 
234 modulating the aged immune system.

350 obtained from each mouse was potentially a limiting factor. Briefly, capture antibody was diluted in PBS 
351 and added to each well of a 96-well plate. The plate was then incubated overnight at 4°C, prior to being

357 ELISA plate and incubated for 2 hours at room temperature. Samples that required dilution were diluted 
358 in assay diluent. Following incubation, the plate was washed 5 times with wash buffer and then the
