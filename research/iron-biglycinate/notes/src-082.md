# src-082: Low-Dose Prophylactic Oral Iron Supplementation (Ferrous Fumarate, Ferrous Bisglycinate, and Ferrous Sulphate) in Pregnancy

**Authors:** Nils Thorm Milman, Thomas Bergholt
**Year:** 2024
**Venue:** Journal of Pregnancy (Wiley), Volume 2024, Article ID 1716798
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1155/2024/1716798

## Core Findings

Low-dose oral iron supplementation (20-60 mg/day elemental iron) in pregnancy produces no clinically significant GI side effects -- none of the 482 participating women required dose reduction, formula change, or discontinuation. Ferrous bisglycinate 25 mg elemental iron/day has the most favorable GI tolerability profile among three equipotent prophylactic iron formulations, with significantly fewer total GI complaints (16.5%) compared to ferrous sulphate 50 mg (20.1%, p=0.042) and ferrous fumarate 40 mg (19.5%). Black stools -- a proxy for unabsorbed iron -- were lowest with bisglycinate (8%) versus fumarate (22%) and sulphate (31%), consistent with bisglycinate's higher bioavailability.

## Study Design

Two pooled randomized, double-blind studies in healthy Danish pregnant women:
- **Gentofte study:** 404 women, dose-response of ferrous fumarate (20, 40, 60, 80 mg/day) from gestational weeks 17-18 to delivery.
- **Naestved study:** 78 women, comparing ferrous bisglycinate 25 mg/day vs ferrous sulphate 50 mg/day from gestational weeks 15-19 to delivery.
- GI complaints recorded by structured yes/no interview at inclusion and regular intervals through delivery.
- No placebo group (considered unethical); comparison against baseline GI complaints at inclusion.

## Key Evidence

### Dose-response findings (Gentofte)
- 20-60 mg ferrous fumarate/day: no dose-dependent GI side effects.
- 80 mg/day: significantly higher constipation (32.6% vs ~20% for lower doses) and laxative use (7.1%).
- Total combined GI complaints at inclusion: 20.4% (Gentofte), 25.8% (Naestved).
- Nausea and vomiting declined during pregnancy regardless of iron dose; belching and loose stools increased (normal pregnancy physiology).

### Bisglycinate vs other formulations (Naestved)
- Bisglycinate 25 mg: total combined GI complaints 16.5% during supplementation vs 24.0% at inclusion (p=0.001).
- Sulphate 50 mg: total combined GI complaints 20.1% during supplementation vs 27.5% at inclusion (p=0.002).
- Bisglycinate showed significantly lower total combined GI complaints than sulphate (p=0.042).
- Bisglycinate had significantly less meteorism (11.6%) than fumarate (26.4%, p=0.022) and sulphate (34.0%).
- Black stools: bisglycinate 8.1%, fumarate 21.6%, sulphate 30.9% (p=0.0008).

### Absorption implications
- Lower black stool frequency with bisglycinate attributed to higher bioavailability, requiring less elemental iron (25 mg) for equivalent prophylactic efficacy to 50 mg sulphate.
- Compliance index: bisglycinate 0.89 +/- 0.16 vs sulphate 0.80 +/- 0.22 (not statistically significant).

## Methodology Notes

- Yes/no questionnaire rather than validated VAS scale -- may underdetect graded symptoms.
- No placebo group limits causal attribution of GI complaints to iron vs normal pregnancy physiology.
- Studies conducted at different sites with different demographics (urban higher-income vs rural lower-income populations), with BMI differences.
- Gentofte study excluded women taking <90% of tablets (n=30 excluded).
- Naestved study had small sample (78 women), limiting statistical power for individual complaint comparisons.

## Limitations

1. **Population specificity:** Healthy ethnic Danish pregnant women only -- may not generalize to non-pregnant populations, other ethnicities, or those with pre-existing GI conditions.
2. **No placebo group:** Cannot definitively separate iron-related GI effects from pregnancy-related GI changes.
3. **Small Naestved sample:** 78 women limits power for detecting differences between bisglycinate and sulphate on individual GI symptoms.
4. **Binary outcome measurement:** Yes/no responses lack granularity; a VAS scale would capture severity differences.
5. **Industry funding:** Naestved study supported in part by Pharmovital ApS and Vitalkost AS; present study supported by unrestricted grant from Pharmovital ApS.

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q3 (bisglycinate vs other forms):** Strong direct evidence. Bisglycinate 25 mg has superior GI tolerability to equipotent doses of fumarate 40 mg and sulphate 50 mg, with fewer total GI complaints and black stools. The lower dose needed (25 mg vs 40-50 mg) suggests higher bioavailability.
- **Q4 (low-dose/alternate-day protocols):** Strong evidence that 20-60 mg/day is well tolerated with no clinically significant GI effects. Threshold for increased GI side effects appears to be ~80 mg/day.
- **Q5 (risks of supplementing non-deficient):** Indirectly relevant -- shows low-dose iron is safe in terms of GI tolerability, but the study is in pregnant women who have increased iron requirements, not general population.
- **Q6 (absorption optimization):** Moderate evidence. Bisglycinate's lower black stool rate suggests better absorption (less unabsorbed iron reaching colon). Tablets taken between meals or at bedtime.
- **Q7 (clinical guidelines):** Reports Danish guideline of 40-50 mg/day for pregnancy prophylaxis; supports bisglycinate as preferred formulation.

## Claims to Verify

- "40 mg ferrous fumarate daily prevents ID and IDA in 90% and 95% of pregnancies" -- this is attributed to a prior study [2] (Milman 2005), not directly verified in this paper's methods.
- Compliance index values (0.89 +/- 0.16 vs 0.80 +/- 0.22) reported without raw tablet counts.
- Sample sizes: Gentofte 404 and Naestved 80 (total 484), though the abstract mentions 482 women at inclusion -- minor discrepancy likely due to 2 exclusions not detailed.
