# src-284: Hyperferritinemia in the Elderly Can Differentiate the Bad from the Worst

**Citation:** Goldhaber G, Segal G, Dagan A. *Medicine*. 2020;99(31):e21419. DOI: 10.1097/MD.0000000000021419

**Study type:** Retrospective observational cohort study (hospitalized elderly patients)

**Quality:** Peer-reviewed, open-access. Full text available. Content is substantive and reliable.

## Core Findings

This study examined 242 elderly inpatients (age >70) with extreme hyperferritinemia (ferritin >1000 ug/L) at a tertiary medical center in Israel (2012-2015). The prognosis was dismal: median survival was only 4.7 months, with 6-month mortality of 52.5% and overall mortality of ~70% during a median follow-up of 18.6 months. Higher ferritin levels independently predicted mortality after adjusting for multiple confounders (CRP, albumin, platelets, transferrin, hemoglobin, uric acid, WBC). The most common diagnoses were solid malignancy (23.5%), severe infection/sepsis (31%), and hematologic malignancy (9.5%).

## Key Evidence

- **N=242**, median age 79 years, 63.2% male, median ferritin 1436 ug/L
- **Median survival: 4.7 months** for the whole cohort; 3.7 months excluding iatrogenic causes
- **6-month mortality: 52.5%** overall; 57.8% excluding iatrogenic group
- **Solid malignancy**: ferritin 1751.8 vs 1346.9 ug/L (P<.001), HR=1.9 (P<.01)
- **Septic shock**: HR=1.48 (P<.05); ferritin levels did not differ from rest of cohort
- **Sepsis without shock**: ferritin 1181.1 vs 1470.3 ug/L (P=.001), HR=0.56 (lower mortality)
- **Iatrogenic causes** (iron/blood transfusions, EPO): lower ferritin (1321.9 vs 1517 ug/L, P=.008), HR=0.22 for 30-day mortality (P=.001)
- **Multivariate analysis**: Higher ferritin, lower albumin, and higher uric acid independently correlated with mortality. CRP was NOT independently associated.
- Chronic comorbidities (HTN, CHF, CRF, DM) did not significantly affect ferritin levels or survival.

## Methodology

- **Design**: Retrospective chart review of inpatients at Chaim Sheba Medical Center (tertiary center)
- **Inclusion**: Age >70, ferritin >1000 ug/L on first in-hospital measurement, hospitalized Jan 2012 - Dec 2015
- **Exclusions**: Only first ferritin >1000 measurement used; serial measurements during stay not the basis for inclusion
- **Outcome**: All-cause mortality from Israeli Ministry of Health national registry
- **Statistics**: Cox regression (univariate and multivariate with backward LR method), FDR correction for multiple comparisons, one-tailed tests, SPSS v24
- **Diagnoses grouped**: metastatic malignancy, sepsis/septic shock, iatrogenic, miscellaneous

## Limitations

1. **Retrospective observational** -- no causality can be inferred
2. **No external control group** -- all comparisons within the hyperferritinemia cohort (no comparison to normal-ferritin patients)
3. **Hospitalized elderly only** -- not generalizable to community-dwelling or younger populations
4. **Extreme hyperferritinemia threshold** (>1000 ug/L) -- findings do not apply to mild-moderate ferritin elevation or normal iron status
5. **Ferritin as both iron store marker and acute phase reactant** -- impossible to disentangle iron overload from inflammation as the driver of mortality
6. **Single-center study** in Israel

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q2 (Iron-longevity U-curve)**: Provides strong evidence for the right tail of the U-curve -- extremely high ferritin/iron stores in elderly hospitalized patients are associated with dramatically elevated mortality. Supports the concept that elevated iron markers predict poor outcomes, though this study cannot distinguish iron-mediated harm from ferritin as a bystander of inflammation.
- **Q5 (Risks of supplementing non-deficient)**: Indirectly relevant. While this study examines extreme hyperferritinemia (>1000 ug/L) in sick elderly patients, it reinforces the broader principle that high iron/ferritin status is associated with poor outcomes. Does not directly address risks of iron supplementation in non-deficient individuals at more modest ferritin levels.
- **Q1 (Health consequences of low-normal iron)**: Not directly addressed -- this study focuses on the high end of ferritin.
- **Q3 (Biglycinate vs other forms)**: Not addressed.
- **Q4 (Low-dose/alternate-day protocols)**: Not addressed.
- **Q6 (Absorption optimization)**: Not addressed.
- **Q7 (Clinical guidelines)**: Suggests clinicians should view ferritin >1000 ug/L in elderly inpatients as a grave prognostic sign, but does not provide supplementation guidelines.
