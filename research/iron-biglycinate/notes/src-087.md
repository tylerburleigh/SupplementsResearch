# src-087: Oral iron supplementation: new formulations, old questions

**Citation:** Pantopoulos K. Haematologica. 2024;109:2790-2800. DOI: 10.3324/haematol.2024.284967
**Type:** Review article (peer-reviewed). Citation count: 37.
**Quality:** ok. Full text extracted (raw text fallback from PDF).

## Core Findings

This is a comprehensive 2024 review of oral iron supplementation formulations, their efficacy, tolerability, and optimal dosing strategies. The review covers ferrous salts (including ferrous bisglycinate), ferric complexes (polysaccharide, polymaltose, citrate, maltol, sucrosomial), and other formulations. Key takeaways: (1) Ferrous sulfate remains the gold standard for efficacy but has the worst GI tolerability. (2) Newer formulations (ferric maltol, sucrosomial iron) show comparable hemoglobinization but are more costly and do not always replenish iron stores. (3) Alternate-day dosing is supported by hepcidin physiology and may improve tolerability while maintaining efficacy. (4) Different iron formulations have distinct effects on gut microbiome and inflammatory markers.

## Relevance to Research Questions

### Q1 (health consequences of low-normal iron)
- IDA and non-anemic ID are the most common pathologies worldwide; anemia was the third cause of years lived with disability in 2021 with 24.3% global prevalence (1.92 billion cases).
- ID causes fatigue, immunological, developmental, and neurocognitive defects.
- Iron deficiency may impair adaptive immunity (T lymphocyte and neutrophil proliferation).
- Moderate relevance -- provides background on iron deficiency consequences but focuses on therapeutic replacement rather than health effects of low-normal ranges.

### Q2 (iron-longevity U-curve)
- Both iron deficiency and overload cause morbidity; "a balanced iron supply is important."
- There is no mechanism for iron excretion; dietary absorption compensates for nonspecific losses.
- Excess unshielded iron promotes oxidative tissue damage via reactive oxygen species.
- Low relevance -- mentions the dual risk of deficiency and excess but does not address longevity endpoints.

### Q3 (biglycinate vs other forms)
- Ferrous bisglycinate listed as a ferrous salt with 50 mg elemental iron/tablet at ~$0.15/tablet (vs ferrous sulfate: 65 mg, $0.07).
- In mouse models of IBD, ferrous bisglycinate had beneficial effects against colitis, while ferrous sulfate exacerbated inflammation (line 589-591).
- Ferrous salts in general exhibit high efficacy but poor GI tolerability. Ferrous sulfate vs placebo: OR 2.32 for GI side effects (43 RCTs, n=6,831).
- No head-to-head RCT data specific to bisglycinate efficacy vs sulfate in humans is discussed in this review.
- Sucrosomial iron and liposomal iron improved hemoglobinization but did not always replenish iron stores (serum ferritin).
- No oral iron formulation has shown non-inferiority to intravenous iron.

### Q4 (low-dose/alternate-day protocols)
- Strong coverage. Doses of 60+ mg elemental iron trigger hepcidin induction lasting up to 24 hours, reducing absorption of the next day's dose. Hepcidin returns to baseline at 48 hours.
- Cross-over study: total iron absorption from 200 mg on alternate days was almost twice that from 100 mg on consecutive days.
- RCT: 60 mg twice daily reached Hb endpoint faster than 120 mg on alternate days, but alternate-day was better tolerated and the difference narrowed by 6 weeks.
- British Society of Gastroenterology guidelines now recommend starting with one tablet/day; if not tolerated, reduce to every other day.
- Important caveat: all alternate-day data are from ferrous sulfate studies only. Whether this applies to bisglycinate or other formulations is unknown.
- Ferric maltol induces hepcidin similarly to ferrous sulfate; iron protein succinylate and sucrosomial iron did not affect hepcidin in animal models.

### Q5 (risks of supplementing non-deficient)
- Ferrous sulfate causes transient increase in NTBI (non-transferrin bound iron), which is redox-active and potentially toxic. Ferric polysaccharides had negligible NTBI effects.
- Ferrous sulfate promotes unfavorable gut microbiome shifts and increased pathogenic bacteria; excess iron may alter microbiota diversity and promote infections.
- Iron supplementation may pose infection risk in malaria-endemic areas; iron sequestration is a host defense strategy ("nutritional immunity").
- These are indirect safety signals relevant to supplementation in non-deficient individuals.

### Q6 (absorption optimization)
- Only 14-18% of ingested iron is absorbed from mixed diets, 5-12% from vegetarian diets.
- Fe3+ must be reduced to Fe2+ prior to absorption via DMT1; ascorbate (vitamin C) enhances reduction.
- Phytates, polyphenols, fibers, tannins, calcium supplements, antacids, and PPIs inhibit absorption.
- Iron supplements are better absorbed on an empty stomach.
- Slow-release/enteric-coated formulations perform poorly (release past duodenum where absorption is optimal).
- Hepcidin-mediated feedback is the major physiological brake on absorption.

### Q7 (clinical guidelines)
- British Society of Gastroenterology (2021): first-line ferrous sulfate, fumarate, or gluconate, one tablet/day; if not tolerated, every other day, alternative oral preparations, or IV iron.
- Optimal response: hemoglobin increase of 2 g/dL within 3-4 weeks; 1 g/dL in 4 weeks is reasonable.
- Iron store repletion (ferritin >100 mcg/L) often requires longer treatment (3-12 weeks at 50-200 mg/day elemental iron).
- Choice between formulations should depend on individual iron status, tolerance, absorption capacity, and specific therapeutic goals.

## Methodology
- Narrative review. No systematic search methodology described.
- Synthesizes data from RCTs, meta-analyses, and observational studies across multiple iron formulations.
- Author is a recognized iron biology researcher (McGill University, CIHR-funded).

## Key Evidence Points
- Ferrous sulfate GI side effects: OR 2.32 vs placebo, OR 3.05 vs IV iron (43 RCTs, n=6,831)
- Hepcidin induction threshold: 60 mg elemental iron
- Alternate-day 200 mg absorption nearly 2x consecutive-day 100 mg
- BESTIRON RCT (n=80): ferrous sulfate outperformed ferric polysaccharide in pediatric IDA
- Iron protein succinylate: systematic review of 8,454 patients over 30 years showed similar or higher efficacy vs other treatments

## Limitations
- Narrative review (not systematic); risk of selective citation.
- Ferrous bisglycinate data limited to one mouse study on colitis and cost/elemental iron listing; no human efficacy or tolerability head-to-head trials discussed.
- Alternate-day dosing evidence comes exclusively from ferrous sulfate; generalizability to other formulations is uncertain.
- Does not address supplementation in iron-replete individuals or longevity endpoints.

## Claims to Verify
- "In 2021 anemia was the third cause of years lived with disability" -- cited to GBD 2021, seems plausible but specific ranking should be verified.
- "Total iron absorption from a single dose of 200 mg given on alternate days was almost twice that from 100 mg given on consecutive days" -- this is a notable claim from a crossover study; the original study (ref 91, Stoffel et al.) should be checked for exact numbers.
- "Only ~10-20% of oral iron gets absorbed" -- consistent with other sources but is a broad range.
