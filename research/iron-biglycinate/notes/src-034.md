# src-034: Iron Levels and Cognitive Function in University Students

**Source:** PubMed abstract (PMID 39203944) -- full text not available; abstract only from MDPI journal.
**URL:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39203944
**Type:** Cross-sectional study, peer-reviewed (MDPI)
**Content quality:** Abstract-only (PubMed landing page captured, no full text). Findings based solely on abstract.

## Core Findings

A cross-sectional study of 132 Spanish university students (18-29 years) found that low iron intake is associated with poorer intellectual ability. The effect was predominantly observed in women, where a positive association between iron intake, serum ferritin, and total IQ was found. Among women, iron deficiency (ID) prevalence was 21% and iron deficiency anemia (IDA) prevalence was 4.2%; no ID or IDA was found in men. The authors conclude an iron-rich diet is necessary to maintain academic performance.

## Key Evidence

- **Sample:** 132 university students (18-29 years), University of Castilla-La Mancha, Spain
- **Design:** Cross-sectional; dietary questionnaire + blood parameters + anthropometric measures
- **Cognitive assessment:** Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-IV (total IQ, VCI, WMI, PSI, PRI)
- **Iron deficiency prevalence (women):** ID = 21%, IDA = 4.2%
- **Iron deficiency prevalence (men):** ID = 0%, IDA = 0%
- **Key finding:** Positive association between iron intake, serum ferritin, and total IQ in women; not observed in men
- **Correlation analyses:** Spearman correlations between total IQ and % recommended iron intake, stratified by gender and IQ group (Figures 1-3); correlation between serum markers and cognitive indices in women with >100% RI (Figure 4)

## Methodology

- Cross-sectional design (no causal inference possible)
- Dietary record via questionnaire for iron consumption
- Blood parameters measured (serum ferritin, iron)
- WAIS-IV for cognitive assessment
- Spearman correlation analyses

## Limitations

- **Cross-sectional design** -- cannot establish causation
- **Small sample** (n=132) from a single university
- **Young adult population only** (18-29) -- limited generalizability to older adults
- **Abstract only available** -- effect sizes, p-values, and detailed statistical results not extractable
- **No intervention** -- observational only; does not address supplementation effects
- **No ferritin thresholds reported** in abstract -- unclear how ID/IDA were defined

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (health consequences of low-normal iron):** Moderate relevance. Shows cognitive consequences of low iron intake in young women. However, study addresses frank deficiency more than low-normal iron status, and the population (university students) differs from the longevity-focused demographic.
- **Q2 (iron-longevity U-curve):** Not directly relevant. No mortality or longevity outcomes measured.
- **Q3 (biglycinate vs other forms):** Not relevant. Study examines dietary iron intake, not supplementation forms.
- **Q4 (low-dose/alternate-day protocols):** Not relevant. No supplementation protocol tested.
- **Q5 (risks of supplementing non-deficient):** Not directly relevant. Study focuses on deficiency, not excess supplementation.
- **Q6 (absorption optimization):** Not relevant. No absorption data.
- **Q7 (clinical guidelines):** Tangentially relevant as evidence that iron deficiency has cognitive consequences, supporting screening in at-risk populations.
