## Core Findings

This is a comprehensive 15-page review of oral iron supplementation strategies, covering molecular mechanisms of intestinal iron absorption, regulation by hepcidin, and comparative efficacy of various iron formulations. The review systematically evaluates iron salts, amino acid chelates (including ferrous bisglycinate), liposomal/nanoparticle iron, haem-iron polypeptides, ferritin-based supplements, and lipophilic iron chelates.

Three key findings: (1) Alternate-day dosing produces higher fractional absorption and fewer GI side effects than daily dosing due to hepcidin-mediated feedback. (2) Iron-amino acid chelates like ferrous bisglycinate may achieve equivalent efficacy at lower doses with better GI tolerability, possibly via PEPT1-mediated absorption that partially bypasses DMT1/hepcidin regulation. (3) Iron supplementation should be targeted only to iron-deficient individuals, as supplementation of iron-replete persons risks gut microbiome disruption, increased infection, and impaired infant growth and cognitive development.

## Key Evidence

### Alternate-Day Dosing (Q4)
- Oral iron doses >=60 mg trigger hepcidin increases persisting up to 24 h, reducing next-day absorption by 35-45% (lines 437-441).
- Participants on daily supplementation had 33% higher GI side effects vs alternate-day (line 445-447).
- Weekly (1-2x/week) supplementation had similar efficacy to 3-7x/week in children/adolescents (line 420-421).
- Systematic review: intermittent iron in pregnancy had fewer side effects with no significant difference in birth outcomes (lines 448-456).

### Ferrous Bisglycinate vs Iron Salts (Q3)
- Fe-Gly at 25 mg/day showed similar efficacy to FeSO4 at 50 mg/day in pregnancy prevention study (n=40/group), with fewer GI complications (lines 912-919). Note: different doses complicate interpretation.
- Systematic review found Fe-Gly more effective at increasing Hb in pregnant women with fewer adverse GI events (lines 920-928).
- Iron-AA chelate (15 mg Fe) achieved similar repletion to ferrous fumarate (115 mg Fe) in 150 pregnant women over 12 weeks; faster Hb rise and better tolerability (41.3% vs 60% constipation) (lines 896-910).

### Absorption Mechanisms for AA/Peptide Chelates (Q3, Q6)
- Fe-Gly consists of Fe2+ bound to two glycine molecules via covalent and coordinate covalent bonds (lines 940-943).
- Two hypothesized absorption pathways: (1) dissociation at enterocyte surface with DMT1/FPN uptake, or (2) intact absorption via peptide transporters like PEPT1, bypassing DMT1 (lines 930-951).
- DMT1 knockdown impaired Fe-Gly absorption in Caco-2 cells, suggesting DMT1 involvement (lines 853-856).
- Four amino acid formulation (aspartate, glutamate, glutamine, glycine) increased DMT1 expression and iron flux in mouse models (lines 866-880).

### Risks of Supplementing Non-Deficient Individuals (Q5)
- Supplementation of non-anaemic or iron-replete individuals may harm gut microbiome, increase infection risk, reduce infant growth, and impair cognitive development in children (lines 1081-1087).
- Excess unabsorbed iron negatively impacts gut microbiome composition (lines 103-104).

### Absorption Optimization (Q6)
- Ascorbic acid (40-50 mg) with meals increased iron absorption ~2.5-3 fold (lines 661-668).
- However, one RCT in 400 adults with IDA found vitamin C did not increase efficacy of ferrous succinate (lines 673-678).
- Polyphenols, phytate, oxalate, calcium inhibit NHI absorption; dosing 30 min before meals recommended (lines 229-234).
- PPIs, H2 blockers, and gastric bypass surgery impair absorption (lines 243-249).

### Clinical Guidelines (Q7)
- WHO recommendations: 30-60 mg elemental iron daily for 3 consecutive months/year for women in high-prevalence areas (lines 396-408).
- Adequate replacement when serum ferritin reaches ~100 ug/L (line 737).
- Reticulocytosis evident 7-10 days after initiating therapy; Hb may increase 0.7-1.0 g/dL per week (lines 734-736).

## Methodology
- Narrative review synthesizing mechanistic, preclinical, and clinical evidence.
- Covers human clinical trials, animal models, and cell culture studies.
- No systematic search protocol described; selection appears comprehensive but not formalized.

## Limitations
- Many comparative trials of Fe-Gly vs iron salts used different elemental iron doses, confounding efficacy comparisons.
- The authors note that "additional clinical trials comparing Fe-Gly to other iron supplements are required in other, more diverse populations and geographical locations."
- Limited long-term safety data for newer formulations.
- Most clinical evidence focuses on pregnancy and IDA treatment; minimal data on low-dose supplementation in non-anaemic individuals or longevity contexts.
