# src-457: Fighting Iron-Deficiency Anemia: Innovations in Food Fortificants and Biofortification Strategies

**Citation:** Liberal et al., 2020, *Foods*, 9(12), 1871. DOI: 10.3390/foods9121871
**Type:** Narrative review
**Quality:** ok (82K chars, 1475 sentences, alpha ratio 0.76)

## Core Findings

This review covers iron-deficiency anemia from causes through dietary strategies, with a primary focus on food fortification and biofortification approaches to address global iron deficiency. It confirms the dual nature of iron (essential micronutrient at physiological levels but pro-oxidant and toxic at excess), details enhancers and inhibitors of iron absorption, and reviews regulatory guidance on safe iron forms for fortification. The paper is oriented toward population-level public health in developing countries rather than individual supplementation protocols.

## Key Evidence

### Iron as a Double-Edged Sword (Q2, Q5)
- Iron catalyzes ROS formation at excessive concentrations (lines 69-88)
- High iron stores may contribute to CVD, type 2 diabetes, and cancer in developed countries; however, serum ferritin as a biomarker is unreliable in inflammatory conditions (lines 594-604)
- Oral iron supplementation causes GI adverse effects (dyspepsia, nausea, vomiting, constipation, diarrhea) that limit adherence (lines 117-119)

### Iron Absorption: Enhancers and Inhibitors (Q6)
- Heme iron bioavailability: 15-35%; non-heme iron: <10% (line 188)
- Ascorbic acid enhances non-heme iron absorption up to 84% (maize) and 48% (wheat) (line 214)
- Major inhibitors: phytates, tannins, calcium, polyphenols (lines 193-200)
- Traditional processing (soaking, malting, fermentation) reduces phytate and improves absorption (lines 196-198)
- Adding small amounts of pork meat to phytate-rich meals enhances iron absorption by 57% (line 202)
- Iron-manganese interaction: excessive Mn in iron-deficiency states can worsen deficiency and cause Mn toxicity (lines 222-224)

### Iron Forms and Regulatory Status (Q3, Q7)
- EFSA safe sources for fortification: ferrous bisglycinate, ferric sodium EDTA, iron (II) taurate, iron L-pidolate (lines 301-303)
- WHO preferred cereal fortificants: ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate, ferric pyrophosphate, electrolytic iron (lines 304-306)
- Tolerable upper intake level (UL): 45 mg/day for adults (line 244)
- Potentially fatal oral dose: 60 mg/kg body weight (line 240)
- Daily iron losses: 1-2 mg/day (line 239)
- For vegetarians, RDAs are 1.8x higher than for non-vegetarians (line 231)

### Health Consequences of Iron Deficiency (Q1)
- Even without frank anemia: impaired cognitive development (children), reduced physical performance and work productivity (adults), pregnancy complications (lines 105-110)
- Additional symptoms: pallor, fatigue, dyspnea, headache, alopecia, thermogenesis changes, thyroid metabolism disruption, muscle weakness, increased infection susceptibility (lines 107-110)

### Fortification Safety (Q5)
- Iron fortification at recommended doses diluted in food weight is among the safest approaches (lines 605-609)
- Benefits of fortification substantially outweigh risks in both developed and developing countries (lines 603-604)
- No adverse effects from iron fortification promoting anemia intensity or causing diarrhea have been reported (lines 595-596)

## Methodology

Narrative review. No systematic search strategy described. Covers literature on iron deficiency, absorption mechanisms, fortification technologies, and biofortification (conventional, agronomic, transgenic). Primarily focused on food science and public health nutrition perspectives.

## Limitations

- **Not focused on individual supplementation or low-dose protocols.** The paper addresses population-level fortification and biofortification, not targeted supplementation in non-deficient individuals.
- **No primary data on iron biglycinate specifically.** Ferrous bisglycinate is mentioned only as an EFSA-approved safe source; no comparative bioavailability or clinical trial data for biglycinate vs. other forms is presented.
- **No discussion of alternate-day dosing or low-dose protocols.** Dosing schedules and optimization of absorption timing are not addressed.
- **Limited coverage of iron-overload risks in non-deficient populations.** The safety discussion centers on fortification programs, not individual supplementation by health-conscious consumers.
- **No longitudinal data on health outcomes from mild/subclinical iron deficiency.**
