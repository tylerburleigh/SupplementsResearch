Abstract
Plain Language Summary

Purpose:

Investigate the effects of two consecutive demanding military field exercises on iron status and associations with physical performance in male and female conscripts, and assess potential sex differences.

Methods:

Blood sampling and physical tests were performed before a 6-day field exercise (Exercise I), during a 2-wk recovery period, and after a second 10-day field exercise (Exercise II) in 23 men and 12 women (median age 19). Of these, 21 men and 9 women completed Exercise II.

Results:

A large proportion of men and women had low iron status before the field exercises, including suboptimal ferritin levels in 67% of women and low hemoglobin concentrations in 57% of men. Several iron biomarkers, except ferritin, decreased in response to Exercise I followed by an increase during the recovery period and a decrease in response to Exercise II. Ferritin levels, inflammation- and tissue damage biomarkers increased in response to Exercise I, decreased during recovery, and remained unchanged in response to Exercise II. Red blood cells and hemoglobin levels were unaffected by Exercise I, but increased during recovery, and decreased in men only in response to Exercise II. Iron saturation decreased more in women (–10.4 [–14.3, –6.5]%) than men (–5.5 [–8.3, –2.6]%) and ferritin increased more in men (79.5 [69.0, 90.0] µg/L) than women (42.8 [28.3, 57.2] µg/L) in response to Exercise I. Recovery of jump performance correlated with increased iron and iron saturation in men and increased red blood cells and hemoglobin in women.

Conclusions:

Iron status was diminished after the field exercises in both sexes, and the decrease was slightly more pronounced in women. The recovery period was not sufficient to ensure full restoration of iron status, which may have impacted physical performance. The results encourage future investigations into individualized improvement in iron status before, and possibly during, strenuous military training.

Plain Language Summary
This study examined how demanding military field exercises affect iron levels and physical performance in young male and female conscripts. Blood tests and physical assessments were conducted before, during, and after two exercises. Many participants had low iron levels initially, with 67% of women showing low ferritin and 57% of men having low hemoglobin. Several iron biomarkers, except ferritin, decreased in response to the first exercise, increased during recovery, and decreased again after the second exercise. Ferritin levels increased after the first exercise, decreased during recovery, and remained unchanged after the second exercise. Recovery improved jump performance, which correlated with increased iron and iron saturation in men and increased red blood cells and hemoglobin in women. The findings suggest that tailored strategies to boost iron levels could enhance performance during intense military training.

FAQ

Field exercises are used as an important part of military training and selection, and maintaining optimal soldier performance is essential to execute physically demanding military tasks (
1
). During such field exercises, soldiers are often exposed to very high workloads combined with food and sleep deprivation, which has been reported to result in severe energy deficits (~2000–6000 kcal/day) (
2–7
). High workloads combined with limited food intake can, in turn, lead to large changes in body composition and declines in physical performance (
8–11
). Iron is an essential micronutrient for physical performance because of its role in energy metabolism (as part of iron-containing enzymes) and transport and storage of oxygen (as part of hemoglobin and myoglobin, respectively), and diminished iron status can compromise physical performance (
12
).

Iron status is usually assessed using a combination of blood biomarkers, including ferritin, iron saturation, iron-binding capacity (transferrin), and hemoglobin concentration (
13
). However, several of these markers are acute phase proteins and can be falsely elevated or reduced in response to inflammation (
13
). Therefore, including an inflammatory marker to assess potential coexisting inflammation is helpful in the interpretation of iron status (
14
). The assessment of iron status is further complicated by the inconsistency in how iron deficiency is defined in athletic populations, with lower ferritin levels ranging from 12 to 35 µg/mL (
15–17
). The progression of iron deficiency can be viewed as a three-stage process (
18
). In the first stage, iron stores decrease, which is characterized by low ferritin levels (stage 1 iron depletion, ferritin <35 µg/L). When the iron supply becomes insufficient to meet the body’s needs, ferritin continues to drop accompanied by decreased iron saturation and increased iron-binding capacity (stage 2 iron deficiency erythropoiesis, ferritin <20 µg/L). If the negative iron balance continues, it will eventually affect the ability to produce red blood cells, and the hemoglobin levels will start to drop. Left untreated, it will progress to stage 3 iron deficiency anemia, which will impact oxygen transportation (ferritin < 12 µg/L (
15
,
19
), hemoglobin concentration <11.7 g/dL in women and <13.4 g/dL in men (
20
)).

Demanding exercise is associated with increased loss of iron through processes like gastrointestinal bleedings, mechanical hemolysis (e.g., breakdown of red blood cells in blood vessels from footstrike), sweating and hematuria (blood in urine), with greater loss after high intensity, prolonged activities (
12
). Additionally, exercise-induced inflammation with increased levels of cytokines like IL-6 upregulates hepcidin, the major regulator of systemic iron homeostasis, which leads to reduced iron recycling and intestinal absorption (
21
). Increased erythropoietic activity from training will also increase iron requirements (
22
). Consequently, soldiers, and in particular female soldiers because of additional iron loss through menstrual bleedings, may be at increased risk of developing iron deficiency (
23–25
).

Military training has been found to decrease iron status, which has been associated with reduced physical performance (
1
,
25–27
). When the body is iron-depleted, energy production and aerobic work capacity can be impaired due to reduced iron availability and reduced oxygen transport to exercising muscles. Although the role of iron in physical performance is primarily associated with aerobic performance, iron also plays a role for muscle strength and function, but the precise mechanisms await further investigation (
22
,
28
). The negative consequences of iron deficiency anemia, that is, depleted iron stores combined with low hemoglobin (stage 3), are well described in the literature (
29
,
30
), whereas the effects of iron deficiency with normal hemoglobin levels (stages 1 and 2) are more equivocal. However, evidence suggests that iron deficiency without anemia may also compromise performance (
22
,
24
,
31
).

Limited data exist for the differences between men and women regarding changes in iron status in response to military field exercises. However, previous studies have shown that iron status decreases more in women compared with men during military training (
26
,
32
). A recent study on British Army recruits undergoing basic military training reported that ferritin levels decreased more in men, whereas women had larger declines in hemoglobin (
27
). However, this study followed the recruits during 13 wk of basic training. More data are needed on the acute effects of strenuous short-term military field exercises with large energy deficits and how recovery in iron status after such exercises might impact physical performance. Therefore, the major aim of the current investigation was to investigate the effects of two consecutive demanding military field exercises on biomarkers of iron status and inflammation in male and female soldiers. In addition, we investigated potential sex differences in the response to these exercises and explored the associations between changes in iron biomarkers and physical performance during the recovery period between the two field exercises. We hypothesized that iron status would decline in response to the military field exercises in both sexes, with larger declines in women. Also, our group has previously shown that the recovery period following an extremely demanding field exercise is important for recovery in physical performance (
9
), and we hypothesized that this recovery was associated with better recovery in iron status.

Blood sampling and physical performance tests were performed 2–3 days before Exercise I (pre) and during the 2-wk recovery period (post-24 h, post-72 h, post-1 wk, and post-2 wk). The last postmeasurement (post-2 wk) was used as the prevalue when investigating the responses to Exercise II. After Exercise II, blood samples were taken post-72 h. No blood samples were taken the day the conscripts returned from the field exercises because of ethical, practical, and methodological challenges with standardizing the blood sampling (i.e., food and liquid intake, arrival time, and physical activity). To assess menstrual status, the women filled out a questionnaire about menstrual cycle and use of contraceptives (Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content,
https://links.lww.com/MSS/D305
).

The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and evaluated by the Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (reference number 2016/632/REK sør-øst A). Participation in the annual selection process and participation in the current study were voluntary, and all participants gave written informed consents before inclusion. Participants were free to withdraw from the study at any time.

Military Field Exercise I and II and the Recovery Period

The ~6-day extremely demanding selection exercise (Exercise I) has been described previously (
9
). Briefly, Exercise I was performed simultaneously but separately for men and women and consisted primarily of loaded marching and various tasks testing the conscripts’ mental and physical resilience under conditions of large sleep and food restrictions. The conscripts received one freeze-dried military ration (575 kcal, Drytech) per day, and all the provided food was eaten. The average macronutrient content of the field rations was 31.8% fat, 53.7% carbohydrates, and 14.5% proteins. Total energy expenditure during Exercise I was estimated to an average (standard deviation) of 7235 (408) kcal/day (90.4 [4.8] kcal/kg/day) in men and 6041 [357] kcal/day (88.9 [3.9] kcal/kg/day) in women (for details on calculations, see Vikmoen et al. (
9
)). During the recovery period, the conscripts performed military activities of mild intensity with normal sleep and food intake.

The following 10-day SERE (Survival, Evasion, Resistance, Escape) exercise (Exercise II) consisted of a physically and mentally challenging exercise with limited access to food. During the first week, the conscripts received one freeze-dried military ration (~684 kcal, Drytech) per day. During the second week, the food intake consisted of meat obtained from the slaughter of sheep (one divided between all conscripts) and poultry (one per individual), in addition to berries if available.

Blood Sampling

Fasting blood samples were taken in the morning between 6:00 and 8:00
am
. Participants were instructed to avoid physical activity in the period between they woke up and the blood sample was taken. Whole blood was mixed and left for coagulation at room temperature for 30 min followed by centrifugation at 1300 g for 10 min. Serum was aliquoted into tubes and stored at –20°C until further analyses. In serum samples, biomarkers of iron status (ferritin, iron, iron saturation, and iron-binding capacity), inflammation (C-reactive protein [CRP]), and tissue damage (lactate dehydrogenase [LDH]) were measured by an accredited laboratory (Fürst Medical Laboratory, Furuset, Oslo, Norway). Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid samples were analyzed using Advia 60 (Bayer HealthCare/Siemens, Germany) for the hematological indices red blood cells, hematocrit, hemoglobin concentration, and mean corpuscular volume (MCV). Iron status was characterized using the Nordic reference intervals (
20
,
33
,
34
) (age ≥ 18 years) and athletic-specific recommendations from Peeling et al. (
19
) for ferritin levels. Depleted iron stores were defined as serum ferritin < 35 µg/L in both sexes, and anemia was defined as hemoglobin concentration <11.7 g/dL in women and <13.4 g/dL in men.

Physical Performance Tests

Medicine ball throw, countermovement jump (CMJ), and an evacuation test (EVAC) were completed at each time point, as previously described (
9
). In short, CMJ measures explosive strength performance of the leg muscles, medicine ball throw measures explosive strength in the upper body, and EVAC measures anaerobic performance. The EVAC test is measured as time to completion, where shorter times indicate better performance.

Imputation of Missing Data

Due to injuries, sickness, or other logistical restrains during the study period, some participants had missing data, particularly for the physical tests. In a dataset with a restricted sample size, like ours, missing data or the choice of imputation method might influence the validity of the results. Missing values were imputed using the K-nearest neighbors algorithm (R, v.4.3.2;
laeken package
, v.0.5.2;
VIM package
, v.6.2.2) separately for each variable and sex with
k
equal to the square root of the number of individuals. This method has been shown to outperform simpler imputation methods (
35
). All values were adjusted for the corresponding prevalue before imputation. For the blood biomarkers, three men and four women had missing data, but no individuals were missing more than one time point. Two of the men were lacking the first time point (pre), in which the value at post-2 wk was directly replicated and used as an approximation for the missing values. The imputed serum ferritin value for one woman was not physiologically meaningful, and this specific imputation was performed without correction for the prevalue. No data were missing post-Exercise II. For the physical tests, imputation was not performed for individuals missing more than two postmeasurements (Supplemental Fig. 1, Supplemental Digital Content,
https://links.lww.com/MSS/D305
, flow chart).

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean with standard deviation or median with lower and upper quartiles (Q1, Q3). The two field exercises were analyzed separately to address our predefined aims, reduce model complexity, and facilitate comparison with previously published data on Exercise I (
9
). To investigate sex differences in iron status in response to Exercise I, linear mixed effects models with random intercept and maximum likelihood estimation were applied. A mixed effects model is preferred for the analysis of longitudinal data with continuous outcome variables (
36
). For each blood variable, a model was fitted with sex (females and males), time (pre, post-24 h, post-72 h, post-1 wk, and post-2 wk), and the interaction between sex and time included as fixed effects. A random intercept was assigned to each individual to account for within-person dependencies in the repeated measurements. The mixed effects model formulation (
lme4 package
, v.1.1-34) was variable ~ sex × time + (1|subject) with time included as a factor and the first time point (pre) as the reference category. Significance and estimated marginal means with 95% confidence intervals (lower limit, upper limit) were obtained using Satterthwaite’s method for computation of degrees of freedom (
lmerTest package
, v.3.1-3). To obtain overall (global) effects of sex, time, and their interaction, an analysis of variance was applied on the fitted models. Since a major aim of the study was to assess sex differences in response to the exercise, the sex × time interaction was kept in all models regardless of overall significance. Within-sex changes were reported, also when the overall interaction was not statistically significant. Assumptions for the mixed effects models were checked by visual inspection of the model residuals (marginal/conditional residuals and random effects) for normality (using histograms and QQ-plots) and homogeneity of variance (plotting residuals against fitted values). Alternative analytic approaches (functional data analysis, inclusion of random slopes, exclusion of outliers, parametric vs nonparametric comparisons) were tried for most analyses and supported the same overall conclusions as those reported. Therefore, these results are not presented in the paper.

The post-2 wk measurement was used as the prevalue for Exercise II. To investigate sex differences in response to Exercise II, we used a change scores analysis with adjustment for the baseline (pre) value of the outcome variable (model formulation: change score ~ pre + sex). The change score was calculated by subtracting the prevalue from the post-72 h value. This approach is mathematically equivalent to an analysis of covariance with follow-up as the outcome and baseline as a covariate (
37
,
38
). Paired samples
t
-tests were used to investigate changes within each sex (model formulation: variable ~ time).

For skewedly distributed data, nonparametric tests or analyses of log-transformed data were also performed, but these analyses came to the same overall conclusions, and the results are reported for the parametric methods for simplicity in presentation.

In exploratory analysis, we also assessed the associations between changes in iron status biomarkers and changes in physical performance during the 2-wk recovery period by computing Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (
Hmisc package
, v.5.1-1) between the change from the first postmeasurement (post-24 h) to each of the following postmeasurements (post-72 h, post-1 wk and post-2 wk). The change scores were calculated by subtracting the post-24 h value (start of the recovery period) from each of the following postvalues. In follow-up analyses, we investigated whether baseline iron values were important for the significant findings using linear regression models. However, because baseline iron was not a significant predictor in any of the linear regression models (all
P
> 0.05), only the results from the correlation analyses are included.

Statistical analyses and visual presentations of data were performed using R (v.4.3.3) and RStudio (v.2023.12.1 Build 402). The significance level was set to 0.05. Because the current investigation reports on secondary outcomes and many of the iron biomarkers are highly intercorrelated, reflecting physiologically overlapping aspects of iron homeostasis (e.g., serum iron and iron saturation),
P
values were not corrected for multiple comparisons. In such cases, correction can be overly conservative and may obscure meaningful physiological patterns due to the reduced number of effective comparisons (
39
,
40
). However, all
P
values are reported to support transparent interpretation (
41

DISCUSSION

We found that serum iron and iron saturation declined in response to the two field exercises, being most pronounced in women. Serum ferritin increased in response to Exercise I, most profoundly in men, accompanied by increased inflammation (CRP) and tissue damage (LDH). All blood biomarkers recovered to prevalues or improved during the 2-wk recovery period, with larger increases in serum iron and iron saturation in men compared with women. However, the recovery period was not sufficient to ensure full restoration of iron status, and a large proportion of men and women still had suboptimal levels before Exercise II. The recovery of iron biomarkers seemed to be important for recovery in CMJ performance, but for different aspects in men and women. While CMJ performance was associated with the hematological markers (i.e., red blood cells, hematocrit, and hemoglobin concentration) in women, it was associated with serum iron and iron saturation in men.

The current investigation sheds new light on how participation in demanding military field exercises affects iron status in men and women and how the response differs between sexes. It also highlights the importance of recovery periods between such field exercises for restoration of iron status, potentially contributing to more rapid recovery in physical performance. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effects of two consecutive short-term demanding military field exercises on iron status in men and women. Apart from the obvious significance for military populations, our findings are also of value for athletes exposed to very high activity levels who struggle to maintain energy balance, such as cyclists and runners, particularly those involved in competitions associated with high levels of physical and mental stress.

Effects of the Field Exercises on Blood Biomarkers

In agreement with our findings, decreased serum iron and iron saturation in response to military training have been reported by others (
1
,
25
,
43
). The decreased serum iron and iron saturation indicate insufficient ability to meet the increased iron demand during strenuous exercise, particularly in combination with severe energy deficits. Furthermore, while the levels returned to prevalues within 1 wk of recovery, several individuals still had serum iron and iron saturation below recommended levels. The consecutive decrease in serum iron and iron saturation in response to Exercise II could therefore be expected given the initial low iron status and sustained limited food intake.

The larger decreases in iron saturation in women in response to Exercise I might be explained by the higher prevalence of low ferritin stores in women (67%) than men (9%) before the exercise. However, the decrease in serum iron and iron saturation in response to Exercise I was not accompanied by decreases in serum ferritin, an indicator of iron storage. Instead, we found that the ferritin levels increased in response to Exercise I. This finding is in contrast to previous studies reporting declines in iron stores during military training in both sexes (
1
,
25
,
26
,
43–46
). The discrepancy between our findings and those of previous studies is probably related to the high intensity of training in the current study as ferritin is known to increase after strenuous exercise as a result of exercise-induced inflammation (
47
) and tissue damage, which results in increased leakage of ferritin into serum (
48
). Therefore, rather than reflecting an actual increase in iron stores, the transient increase in ferritin is likely due to inflammation and/or tissue damage caused by exercise and stress. This hypothesis is supported by the increased CRP and LDH levels above reference values, which correlated with changes in ferritin (Supplemental Fig. 8, Supplemental Digital Content,
https://links.lww.com/MSS/D305
, scatterplots) similar to what has been reported after ultramarathon (
49
), and our previous finding of increased creatine kinase (
9
). It is also important to mention that iron deficiency can impair the function of iron-dependent antioxidants, such as catalase and glutathione peroxidase (
50
), which can exacerbate the inflammatory response and contribute to tissue damage through increased oxidative stress. Similar acute inflammation response with concomitant increase in ferritin has been reported during ultramarathon (
51
) and military training (
52
), including McClung et al. (
53
) who found that a 7-day winter training exercise among Norwegian male soldiers (
n
= 21) resulted in increased inflammation accompanied by a 25% increase in ferritin.

Although no sex differences were observed for CRP and LDH, the higher increase in ferritin in men might be explained by men having higher initial ferritin levels, potentially leading to larger leakage during tissue damage. The increased ferritin levels might also be related to hemolysis as the change in ferritin from pre- to post-24 h Exercise I was negatively correlated with changes in hemoglobin concentration (Supplemental Fig. 9, Supplemental Digital Content,
https://links.lww.com/MSS/D305
, scatterplot), which has also been reported by others during ranger training courses (
54
). Since we only observed this in men, the higher increase in ferritin in men might, in part, also be explained by different degrees of hemolysis between sexes. The serum ferritin, CRP, and LDH levels returned to prevalues during the 2-wk recovery period and were not significantly affected by Exercise II. This could indicate an adaptive effect of Exercise I, in which the soldiers are more resistant to exercise-induced damage. However, since Exercise I is considered somewhat more physically demanding than Exercise II, this would likely explain the lack of changes in CRP and LDH in response to Exercise II.

Iron-binding capacity, or transferrin, is known to increase during iron deficiency (
18
); however, the levels decreased in both sexes in response to Exercise I and, although not significant for women, Exercise II. This is probably, at least in part, related to the previously explained inflammation-like response to the exercise, as transferrin is a negative acute phase protein (
13
). In line with this, the change in iron-binding capacity from pre- to post-24 h Exercise I was negatively correlated with changes in CRP (Supplemental Fig. 8, Supplemental Digital Content,
https://links.lww.com/MSS/D305
, scatterplot), but only in women. The increase in iron-binding capacity during the 2-wk recovery period suggests an emerging regulation of iron homeostasis to restore iron status.

The finding that the hematological indices were unaffected by Exercise I is contradictory to previous studies reporting reduced levels during basic military training (
27
,
43
,
55
). The lack of effect in our study can be due to the initial low levels of these markers. For example, Hennigar et al. (
43
), who observed reduced hemoglobin during 9 wk of military training, reported a mean prehemoglobin of 15.5 g/dL in men (
n
= 50) and 13.5 g/dL in women (
n
= 25), while the values were 13.1 and 12.0 g/dL in our study, respectively. The increase in hemoglobin concentration during the recovery period in the current study, resulting in levels within normal ranges for both sexes, could reflect a shift in iron metabolism, from storage of iron to production of more hemoglobin to support oxygen transport, as an adaptive response to the training. Similar adaptations have been reported by others during basic military training (
1
,
25
,
32
,
45
). Of note, training is known to increase plasma volume, which, in turn, can result in apparently low hemoglobin, a phenomenon referred to as “sports anemia” commonly observed in athletic populations (
12
,
18
). The increases in the hematological indices during recovery might therefore in part also represent a normalization of the initial low levels observed both before Exercise I and at post-24 h. In response to Exercise II, the hematological indices decreased in men, which can, as previously explained, be related to hemolysis. However, we did not find any associations between changes in hemoglobin and ferritin after Exercise II (data not shown), and the decrease might instead be related to plasma volume expansion in response to the exercise.

Studies investigating sex differences in iron status during military training are very limited, particularly for demanding field exercises. Yanovich et al. (
26
) compared the longitudinal effects of a 10-wk basic combat training course on iron status in men (
n
= 89) and women (
n
= 65) and found that iron status declined more in women than men, including iron saturation, which is in accordance with our observations. Additionally, a recent study by O’Leary et al. (
27
) reported that 13 wk of basic military training resulted in larger declines in ferritin in men (
n
= 720) and larger declines in hemoglobin in women (
n
= 329). The authors also reported decreased MCV in men only, similar to what was found in our study in response to Exercise I. In contrast, we also found that the MCV levels increased in women; however, the levels were still within the reference range for both sexes, and these changes may not be physiologically relevant. Moreover, in line with our finding of increased levels of the hematological indices during recovery with no difference between sexes, Taube et al. (
32
) reported that 5 months of basic combat training increased hemoglobin concentrations to similar extend in men (
n
= 104) and women (
n
= 58).

Iron Status and Physical Performance

While both sexes displayed positive correlations between changes in serum iron and changes in CMJ performance during the recovery period, the strongest associations were observed in women for the hematological markers, indicating improved lower body performance with increased levels of red blood cells, hematocrit, and hemoglobin concentration. Our group has previously shown that the recovery period following Exercise I was important for recovery in physical performance and that women recovered CMJ performance faster than men (
9
). The observed associations between CMJ performance and the hematological markers in women might partly have contributed to the faster recovery in CMJ performance. However, causality cannot be deducted from these associations, and thus, this is merely speculation. In contrast, the negative correlation at post-2 wk between iron saturation and CMJ performance in women was unexpected. However, the correlation was only borderline significant (
P
= 0.05), and when reanalyzing the data using Pearson’s correlation (data not shown), the correlation did not reach significance (
P
= 0.08). Whether this finding is clinically relevant remains uncertain.

Other studies have also shown that strenuous short-term military training reduces lower body performance (
6
,
7
,
11
,
56
,
57
). Except one recent study showing inconsistent results for associations between iron status (ferritin) and CMJ performance (
58
), to our knowledge, no other studies have investigated this relationship. While it is well known that iron status is important for endurance and aerobic work capacity (
30
), which require efficient oxygen transport, the potential effects on muscle strength and power have received less attention. However, previous research has shown that hemoglobin levels are associated with muscle strength (
59
,
60
) and that iron supplementation improves strength (
61
,
62
) and muscle fatigability (
63
). Moreover, Myhre et al. (
64
) found that anemic soldiers performed worse in push-up- and sit-up tests compared with nonanemic soldiers at the beginning and end of an 8-wk basic training course. It is possible that we would have found stronger associations if we had used physical tests relying to a larger degree on aerobic performance rather than power and anaerobic capacity. Such associations between iron biomarkers and aerobic performance have been found by O’Leary et al. (
27
) where higher ferritin levels were associated with faster 2.4-km run time during basic military training. Additionally, McClung et al. (
1
) found that hemoglobin and iron saturation were associated with running performance in female soldiers.

Limitations

The outcomes analyzed here were secondary to the main outcomes already published (
9
), and the study was not specifically designed for the assessment of changes in iron status. In particular, we did not have detailed dietary recordings during the study period, which ideally could have been used for adjustment in the statistical analyses investigating sex differences. However, the supplied food was standardized during both field exercises and the recruits ate at the same military dining facility with a set menu during the recruit- and recovery period. Also, the physical performance tests included in the study did not measure aerobic work capacity and were, therefore, not optimal for assessment of associations with iron status. However, aerobic tests could not be performed due to logistical challenges. Of note, the conscripts had already been through a quite strenuous 3-wk recruit period before the first exercise, and it is likely that the preiron status measured in the current study was lower than it would have been with moderate activity levels. The suboptimal initial iron status, with high prevalence of depleted iron stores in women and low hemoglobin in men, may have impacted how they responded to the two consecutive exercises.

Regarding iron status and the inflammatory response to the exercises, there are several potential biomarkers that could have been measured, such as hepcidin, which is a negative regulator of iron status (
14
), and IL-6, which increases dramatically in response to strenuous exercise (
21
). Other tissue-specific damage biomarkers, such as the liver damage biomarkers aspartate- and alanine aminotransferase, could have been included. As liver is the main site for iron storage (
13
), damage to hepatocytes can have contributed to the elevations in serum ferritin. It should also be noted that at one time point during the study (post-72 h Exercise I), most men were not fasting before the blood sampling. However, the biomarkers assessed in the current study are not to a large degree affected by fasting versus nonfasting status (
65
), and the fasting instruction was followed at all the other blood samplings.

Another limitation was that we did not record previous use of iron supplementation or possible consumption of multivitamins with iron during the study. However, the conscripts were not encouraged to ingest multivitamins, and most importantly, there were no sex-specific recommendations. Therefore, intake of such supplementation is not likely to explain the differences between the sexes found in the current study. Furthermore, we did not account for women’s menstrual cycle in our analyses and potential iron loss due to menstruation may have influenced some of the observed sex differences. However, the majority of women reported using contraceptives and either had no menstruation or irregular menstruation (Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content,
https://links.lww.com/MSS/D305
). Thus, it is unlikely that menstruation was the primary reason for the larger declines in iron status in women or the initial low ferritin levels in the current study. It is also important to mention that the current study population was small with an asymmetric distribution of sexes, which may have reduced the statistical power to detect all potential sex differences. Finally, the relatively high number of statistical tests increases the risk of type I errors. While adjusting for multiple comparisons can reduce this risk, it may also increase the probability of type II errors (
66
) and be overly conservative, potentially overlooking meaningful physiological effects. The consistent results from several iron biomarkers support the conclusion of an overall effect of the field exercises on iron status, and the agreement in
P
values reflects the previously discussed intercorrelation among these biomarkers. In a study with a limited sample size and intercorrelated outcome variables, such adjustments would not be advisable. Although it could be argued that these results should be interpreted with caution, it could also be argued that the consistency is a strength (
40
).

CONCLUSIONS

Findings from the present study indicate that participation in extremely demanding military field exercises diminishes iron status in both men and women, with larger declines in iron saturation in women. In contrast, ferritin levels increased in response to Exercise I, most likely due to concurrent inflammation and tissue damage, with larger increases in men. While the 2-wk recovery period was sufficient for the blood biomarkers to return to prevalues, the observed declines in iron status after Exercise II, particularly in men, imply that the conscripts’ initial iron status and the duration of the recovery period were insufficient to withstand the repeated exposure.

PERSPECTIVES

The suboptimal initial iron status and further declines during the field exercises suggest a need for regular monitoring of iron status in military populations. The current study emphasizes the challenges in interpreting iron status during demanding military field exercises, with the confounding effects of inflammation that persist for several days after completion. Therefore, iron status should be assessed before such field exercises. If diminished iron status is detected, measures should be taken to replete iron stores before participation in demanding training to promote optimal performance and wellbeing of the conscripts. Notably, our results indicate that the recovery period between the two field exercises should have been longer to allow for sufficient restoration of iron status. However, the duration of the recovery period is probably dependent on the baseline iron status, and it is possible that interventions implemented to increase iron stores before, or during, the exercises could allow for a shorter recovery period. More studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to investigate the impact of such interventions for short- and long-term changes in iron status during strenuous military training, particularly in the aspect of sex differences and implications for performance.
