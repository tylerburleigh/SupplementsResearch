ABSTRACT  33 
BACKGROUND: Regular blood donation is associated with an increased risk of iron deficiency (ID; 34 
ferritin <15 µg/L). Oral iron supplementation is known to shorten iron store recovery time and could 35 
serve as a more effective alternative to extended donation intervals. We aimed to determine the 36 
optimal ferritin-guided iron supplementation protocol in terms of donor health, side-effects, donor 37 
return, and treatment adherence. 38 
METHODS: In this prospective randomized placebo-controlled trial donors with ferritin levels ≤30 µg/L 39 
were enrolled in the study and randomly assigned to one of six groups, stratified for sex and age. 40 
Depending on the study arm, donors were asked to adhere to a ferrous bisglycinate supplementation 41 
protocol for 56 days, taking capsules containing 0 mg (i.e. placebo), 30 mg or 60 mg of elemental iron, 42 
either on alternate days or daily. The primary outcome was iron deficiency (ID) at 56 days follow-up, 43 
while secondary outcomes included low ferritin (≤30 µg/L), low Hb (≤135 g/L for men and ≤125 g/L for 44 
women), iron deficiency-related symptoms, side effects, treatment adherence, and evaluations of all 45 
outcomes at 122 and 180 days follow-up. This trial is registered in the Dutch trial registry 46 
(NL73283.018.20).  47 
FINDINGS: Of the 2,052 donors who provided their informed consent, 830 donors (464 women)  had 48 
ferritin levels ≤30 µg/L and were included in the trial. Compared to placebo, all iron supplementation 49 
groups exhibited similar significantly lower odds of ID at 56 days, with odds ratios (OR) ranging from 50 
0.60 (95% CI [0.55–0.66]) to 0.65 (95% CI [0.59–0.72]). Similarly, for low Hb, the OR’s ranged from 0.74 51 
(95% CI [0.64–0.86]) to 0.80 (95% CI [0.68–0.93])  For low ferritin, 60 mg of daily iron supplementation 52 
yielded an OR of 0.52 (95% CI [0.47–0.57]), markedly lower than those of other protocols, which ranged 53 
from 0.61 (95% CI [0.55–0.68]) to 0.82 (95% CI [0.74–0.91]). Iron supplementation did not result in any 54 
significant differences compared to placebo in ID-related symptoms, gastrointestinal side effects, 55 
intention to return to donate, or treatment adherence. 56 
INTERPRETATION: In regular donors, 60 mg  iron supplements taken daily was shown to be the most 57 
effective strategy for mitigating ID, low Hb, and especially low ferritin without introducing 58 
gastrointestinal discomfort or changes in the donors intention to return to donate. Ferritin-guided iron 59 
supplementation is an effective iron management strategy as an alternative or supplement to 60 
extended donation intervals.   61

INTRODUCTION  62 
Worldwide, over 100 million whole blood donations are made each year, providing a critical resource 63 
for life-saving transfusions and treatments. 1 This invaluable supply depends on the generosity of 64 
millions of blood donors. Regular whole blood donation is associated with  loss of iron and an elevated 65 
risk of developing iron deficiency (ID; ferritin <15 µg/L) and low haemoglobin (Hb) levels, potentially 66 
threatening donor health and donor availability. 2 Blood services primarily focus on monitoring Hb 67 
levels to prevent anaemia and ensure the quality of blood products. European legislation mandates 68 
that the donor eligibility threshold for Hb levels among male and female whole blood donors be set at 69 
>135 g/L and >125 g/L, respectively. However, Hb levels do not accurately reflect the body's iron stores, 70 
and anaemia can be a late consequence of ID. Consequently, ferritin-based deferral policies are 71 
increasingly common among blood services as a means to monitor donors’ iron stores and prevent 72 
ID.3,4 We have recently shown that ferritin-guided donation intervals significantly improved Hb and 73 
ferritin levels in whole blood donors. However, donor availability was negatively impacted due to 74 
longer donation intervals and decreased donor return.5 75 
An alternative approach to enhance iron and Hb recovery in regular whole blood donors is post-76 
donation iron supplementation, which is already used by blood services in the USA, Denmark, and 77 
Finland.6 In a randomized clinical trial, Kiss et al. previously showed that iron supplementation for 56 78 
days following blood donation can lead to fully recovery of ferritin to pre-donation levels 7. However, 79 
the optimal supplementation strategy for whole blood donors to improve iron stores remains unclear. 80 
Studies examining supplementation of iron in whole blood donors examined dosages ranging from 19 81 
mg/day to 240 mg/day. 8,9 However, studies have shown that lower dosed iron supplements (19 mg) 82 
may be  equally effective compared to higher dosed supplements (38 mg) in improving ferritin levels.9 83 
Additionally, recent studies suggest that alternate days, rather than daily iron supplementation, may 84 
improve iron uptake in the intestine. 8,10,11 This may potentially reduce gastrointestinal discomfort 85 
associated with iron supplementation, but also impact treatment adherence. 86 
At present, the optimal dosing strategy and intake frequency of iron supplementation for improving 87 
iron recovery, minimizing gastrointestinal discomfort, and ensuring treatment adherence remain 88 
elusive, and its efficacy in both male and female whole blood donors has yet to be established. 89 
Therefore, we aimed to evaluate the effects of varying iron supplement dosages and intake 90 
frequencies on ID and ID-related symptoms in whole blood donors, compared to placebo 91 
supplementation. 92

METHODS  94 
Setting and design  95 
We used a randomized double-blind placebo controlled trial design to study the effect of different iron 96 
supplementation dosages and dosing frequencies on ID and related symptoms using the Sanquin blood 97 
services infrastructure of the Netherlands. Sanquin is a not-for-profit organisation and the only 98 
organisation in the Netherlands with a legal permission and obligation to collect, process, and provide 99 
blood products for transfusion and the production of blood-derived medicinal products. Eligibility for 100 
donation is evaluated through a donor health questionnaire and onsite assessment prior to every 101 
donation. Donors can donate when they are in good health, at least 18 years old, and not at risk for 102 
any transfusion-transmissible infectious diseases. Hb levels should be >135 g/L and >125 g/L for men 103 
and women, respectively, and are measured in capillary blood (HemoCue 201, Angelholm, Sweden). In 104 
the Netherlands, donors with Hb levels below the cut-off are deferred from donation for at least 3 105 
months. Additionally, starting November 2017, Sanquin has implemented a ferritin-guided donation 106 
interval policy. Ferritin levels are measured in serum (Architect Ci8200, Abbott Laboratories, Illinois) 107 
post-donation in newly registered donors and at every fifth whole blood donation. Donors are deferred 108 
for 6 or 12 months as a consequence of ferritin levels between ≥15-≤30 µg/L or <15 µg/L, respectively.  109 
The protocol of the FORTE trial has been previously described in detail.12 In brief, between August 2021 110 
and January 2023 donors were invited via email and provided with the baseline questionnaire if their 111 
ferritin levels were measured at their next donation. Donors were excluded from participation when 112 
they had taken iron supplements regularly in the 3 months prior to enrolment. If their ferritin level was 113 
≤30 µg/L, and thus deferred for at least 6 months, donors were randomized into one of six groups 114 
based on iron supplementation dosage and intake frequency: placebo daily (PD), placebo alternate day 115 
(PA), 30 mg daily (30D), 30 mg alternate day (30A), 60 mg daily (60D), and 60 mg alternate day (60A). 116 
Donor were asked to adhere to the intake protocol for 56 days, and were subsequently invited for their 117 
first follow-up visit. During the post-supplementation period, donors were invited for follow-up visits 118 
2 and 3 at 122 and 180 days, respectively. This study is performed according to the Declaration of 119 
Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The Medical Research Ethics Committee Amsterdam 120 
UMC has approved the study protocol (trial ID NL8590) and is registered in the Dutch trial registry 121 
(NL73283.018.20). 122

capsules for daily intake, along with instructions regarding their prescribed intake frequency. The 125

Outcomes  133 
The primary outcome of this study was the presence of ID, defined as a serum ferritin <15 ug/L at 56 134 
days follow-up. Secondary outcomes included presence of low ferritin, defined as serum ferritin 15-30 135 
µg/L, and low Hb, defined as Hb levels ≤135 g/L for men and ≤125 g/L for women. Other secondary 136 
outcomes included gastrointestinal side-effects evaluated by the Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating 137 
Scale13; ID-related symptoms including general health ( 36-Item Short-Form Health Survey 14), fatigue 138 
(Fatigue Assessment Scale 15), cognitive failure ( Cognitive Failure Questionnaire 16), restless legs 139 
syndrome (RLS) (Cambridge Hopkins Restless Legs Syndrome Questionnaire 17); intention to return to 140 
donate (Theory of Planned Behavior18); and treatment adherence. Treatment adherence was evaluated 141 
by counting the number of returned capsules at 56 days and, if not available, through the data from 142 
the treatment adherence application MedApp (MedApp Nederland B.V., Eindhoven, The Netherlands, 143 
https://medapp.nl), which has been described previously12. 144 
Statistical analysis 145 
Baseline descriptive characteristics are described for all trial participants, stratified by trial arm. 146 
Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, or as median with interquartile range 147 
when not normally distributed. Categorical data are presented as proportions. 148 
We conducted Linear Mixed Models (LMM) analysis for continuous outcomes and Generalized 149 
Estimating Equation (GEE) analysis for binary outcomes. The effects of the different supplementation 150 
groups compared to daily placebo intake (reference) across follow-up visits were assessed by 151 
incorporating the intake groups and follow-up visits as indicator variables in the models. In addition, 152 
to assess the effects at each follow-up timepoint, we repeated the analyses with each follow-up visit 153 
serving as a reference group for all outcomes. To account for the correlation between the repeated 154 
measures within each participant, we included a random slope and grouping-factor for participants in 155 
the LMM and GEE analyses, respectively. Due to skewed data distributions, ferritin and gastrointestinal 156 
discomfort data were log transformed. Furthermore, each model was adjusted for the baseline 157 
proportions or levels of its respective dichotomous or continuous outcome variable. In addition to the 158 
crude models, adjusted analyses were performed in which sex, age, body mass index (BMI), self-159

Deviations from the protocol 170 
Approximately 600 donors who expressed their willingness to participate in the trial did not visit a 171 
donation centre during the course of the study, as they were not routinely invited for a whole blood 172 
donation during that time. This led to inclusion of 830 participants in the trial rather than the desired 173 
1200 based on the performed power calculation 12. With only nine non-western European donors, 174 
ethnicity was not evaluated as a potential confounder due to limited ethnic diversity in our study 175 
population and consequent constraints on statistical power. Likewise, the prevalence of PICA (the 176 
consumption of non-food items) was insufficient for statistical evaluation within each supplementation 177 
group, with no more than four participants in total reporting PICA at any time point. 178

between 135 to 140 participants were assigned to each of the trial arms.  187

were similar. However, the proportion of smokers was higher in the PA, 60D, and 60A-groups.  191

DISCUSSION 241 
This randomized trial demonstrates that all examined dosages and frequencies of iron 242 
supplementation effectively reduce iron deficiency, low ferritin levels, and low hemoglobin levels in 243 
regular whole blood donors with low ferritin levels. Specifically, daily supplementation with 60 mg of 244 
elemental iron emerges as the optimal strategy, as it significantly reduces the odds of low ferritin levels 245 
without any notable differences in the odds of iron deficiency, low hemoglobin, and side effects, or 246 
treatment adherence compared to other supplementation strategies. 247 
Our findings of a reduced risk of ID, low ferritin, and low Hb following iron supplementation in regular 248 
donors align with those of previous studies 7,9,19. However, this is the first study to evaluate both iron 249 
dosage and intake frequency in relation to iron stores and donor health, while also considering side 250 
effects, intention to return to donate, and treatment adherence. Difference in dose- and intake 251 
frequency response for low ferritin levels might be related to the acute hepcidin release following iron 252 
supplementation, which inhibits iron absorption in the intestine. 8,10,20 Galetti et al.  recently 253 
demonstrated a linear relationship between ferritin and hepcidin, indicating a decrease in iron uptake 254 
from 21.9% to 10.0% as ferritin levels increased from 1 µg/L to 30 µg/L. 21 This decrease in the 255 
proportion of iron taken up with increasing ferritin levels, could explain why higher dosed iron 256 
supplements taken more frequently are more effective at addressing low ferritin levels (ferritin ≤ 30 257 
µg/L) compared to low-dosed supplements taken less frequently.  258 
Although several clinical studies have shown an association between iron supplementation-related 259 
gastrointestinal side effects and reduced treatment adherence, we observed high adherence, likely 260 
due to minimal reporting of gastrointestinal discomfort. 22–24 Previous research by Tolkien et al., 261 
demonstrated that iron supplementation in the form of ferrous sulphate led to over twice the odds of 262 
experiencing gastrointestinal side effects compared to placebo. 25 The lower levels of gastrointestinal 263 
discomfort observed in the present study may be attributed to the use of ferrous bisglycinate, an iron 264 
formulation that includes iron-bound chelates, which prevent binding to absorption-inhibiting dietary 265

ferrous bisglycinate as a low dosed iron formulation for ferritin-guided iron supplementation in donors.  269
