Comparative study of the effects 
of different iron sources on 
bioavailability and gastrointestinal 
tolerability in iron-deficient rats
Roger Mariné-Casadó1, Yaiza Tobajas1, Anna Antolín1, Teresa Negra2, Alan Connolly2,  
Juan María Alcaide-Hidalgo1 & Antoni Caimari3
Iron deficiency anaemia is a global health issue affecting millions of people. Though effective, 
conventional iron sources used for supplementation often cause gastrointestinal side effects. 
This preclinical study evaluated the efficacy and tolerability of three iron supplements—ferrous 
bisglycinate, LIPOFER™ microcapsules (Def-LFe1), and a commercially available microencapsulated 
iron pyrophosphate (Def-Fe2)—in reversing diet-induced iron deficiency in rats compared to FeSO4. 
The study included three phases: (a) First, animals underwent a 24-day iron depletion period; (b) 
then, animals were exposed to a 21-day repletion period, receiving treatments at a human-equivalent 
dose of 80 mg of elemental iron; and (c) finally, to evaluate gastrointestinal tolerability, animals were 
supplemented with each treatment for 9 weeks. All supplements reversed iron deficiency within 14 
days without adverse gastrointestinal effects. However, based on the decrease in TIBC and transferrin 
levels, coupled with the higher haemoglobin levels, Def-LFe1 demonstrated a higher absorption rate 
than Def-Fe2. In addition, the group supplemented with LIPOFER™ showed higher feed efficiency, 
especially compared to the control and FeSO4 groups. FeSO4 increased IL-6 gene expression in the 
colon, while Def-LFe1 did not. These findings highlight LIPOFER™ as a promising alternative to 
conventional iron supplementation. Further studies should be conducted to clarify these issues.

effects, Inflammation
In 2021, it was estimated that 1.9 billion people were affected by anaemia1primarily characterised by a deficiency 
in the number or quality of red blood cells, impairing the oxygen-carrying capacity of blood 2. Iron-deficiency 
anaemia (IDA) develops when there is not enough iron in the body due to increased iron demand, increased iron 
losses, and reduced absorption or intake of iron through the diet. IDA, the most common type of anaemia, can 
be a chronic condition which is often underdiagnosed due to its asymptomatic nature. When symptoms exist, 
they can present themselves as paleness of skin or conjunctiva. Other non-specific symptoms such as fatigue, 
weakness, and irritability can also be present, along with dizziness, shortness of breath and headaches. These 
symptoms collectively can have multiple consequences on human health, affecting overall quality of life and 
productivity.
Iron supplementation is the most common treatment for IDA. Iron salts such as ferrous sulfate (FeSO 4), 
gluconate, or fumarate, are the most prescribed sources of iron3. While these treatments are effective in increasing 
iron levels and alleviating IDA, they are often accompanied by side effects in the gastrointestinal tract, such as 
nausea, vomiting, constipation, and diarrhoea4. These side effects can lead to poor adherence to the treatment 
regimen, reducing its overall efficacy.
Given the challenges associated with iron salts such as FeSO4 in terms of adverse effects on health, there is a 
pressing need for new strategies to prevent IDA. Chelation and microencapsulation are techniques used to increase 
iron bioavailability and reduce the associated side effects. Microencapsulation has gained considerable attention 
as a convenient delivery system for vitamins, minerals, and other active ingredients. Microencapsulation is the 
packaging of an active ingredient, such as iron, in a protective matrix. This protective matrix prevents the iron

an alternative to FeSO4 formulation was evaluated in a preclinical model of diet-induced Fe deficiency.
Materials and methods
Treatments
The products evaluated in the present study were the following iron supplements: Ferrous sulfate (FeSO4) (Vidara, 
Spain), which contained 36.8% of elemental iron (368  mg/g); Ferrous bisglycinate (BisFe) (Vidara, Spain), 
which contained 20.3% of elemental iron (203  mg/g); microencapsulated iron pyrophosphate 1 (LIPOFER™ 
microcapsules) (Lipotec, S.A.U., Spain), which contained 9% of elemental iron (90 mg/g); microencapsulated iron 
pyrophosphate 2 (Sunactive®) (Taiyo GmbH, Germany), which contained 8.17% of elemental iron (81.7 mg/g). 
Animals were supplemented with the different treatments at the human extrapolated dose of 80 mg of elemental 
iron. The rat-equivalent dose (6.2 mg/kg bw of elemental iron) was calculated by applying the body surface area 
(BSA)-based conversion method described by Reagan-Shaw et al. 9. using species-specific Km values derived 
from the respective body mass and length of the experimental animals (0.3 kg, 20 cm) and an established adult 
human reference (60 kg, 1.60 m)9.
Animals and experimental design
The Animal Ethics Committee of the UTNS-REUS (Reus, Spain) and the Generalitat de Catalunya approved 
all the procedures. The study was carried out following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals 
and in accordance with the European provisions of Directive 86/609/EEC on the protection of animals used for 
experimental and other scientific purposes. The study is detailed in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines and 
recommendations.
60 four-week-old male Wistar rats (ENVIGO, Barcelona, Spain) were used for this study. Rats were 
individually caged in the animal facility at 22 °C with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle and were fed ad libitum a 
standard chow diet. Firstly, after 7 days of acclimation, rats were exposed to an iron depletion period. For that, all 
sixty animals were randomly distributed in 2 different groups: a Fe-deficient group (n = 50), which received a Fe-
deficient diet (ENVIGO Teklad Custom Diet TD. 80396 containing 2–6 mg Fe/kg diet, which is a modification 
of the AIN-76 A diet) for 24 days to induce iron deficiency; and a control group ( n = 10), which received a Fe-
sufficient diet (ENVIGO Teklad Global 18% Protein Rodent Diet containing 200 mg Fe/kg diet). At the end of 
the depletion period, rats fed the Fe-deficient diet were assigned to 5 groups of 10 animals according to their 
haemoglobin (Hb) levels.
Then, rats were exposed to an iron repletion period, in which animals were supplemented with the different 
products of interest at the human extrapolated dose of 80 mg of elemental iron and fed the Fe-deficient diet for 
21 days. All iron supplements were administered after a 5-hour fasting period to enhance its absorption. These 
treatments were dissolved in low-fat condensed milk and offered orally using a syringe, from which the rats 
voluntarily ingested the solution by licking. Finally, at the end of the repletion period, rats were exposed to both 
iron supplementation and a Fe-normal diet for 9 weeks to evaluate each formulation’s gastrointestinal tolerability 
profile.
Thus, animals were distributed into six experimental groups: (1) The Control + Vehicle (Con) group was 
exposed to a Fe-normal diet during the depletion and repletion periods, as well as throughout the subsequent 
9 weeks of treatment; this group received a placebo consisting of low-fat condensed milk. (2) The Fe-
Deficient + Vehicle (Def-VH) group was subjected to the Fe-deficient diet during the depletion and repletion 
phases, followed by a Fe-normal diet for the 9-week treatment period, and also received a placebo (low-fat 
condensed milk). The remaining groups (3 to 6) followed the same dietary regimen as Def-VH group, but 
received different iron-based treatments dissolved in low-fat condensed milk: (3) The Fe-Deficient + Ferrous 
Sulfate (Def-FeS) group received ferrous Sulfate; (4) The Fe-Deficient + Ferrous Bisglycinate (Def-BisFe) group 
received ferrous bisglycinate; (5) the Fe-Deficient + Microencapsulated Iron (LIPOFER™) (Def-LFe1) group 
was administered microencapsulated iron (LIPOFER™); and, lastly, (6) the Fe-Def + Iron Microencapsulated 2 
(Sunactive®) (Def-Fe2) received microencapsulated iron from Sunactive®.

Analyses during the study
Body weight, food intake and feed efficiency
Body weight and food intake were recorded once each week during the three phases of the study. Feed efficiency 
(%) was calculated as the quotient between final body weight gain in grams and the total kilocalories consumed 
during the study and is expressed as a percentage of total caloric intake.

14 and 21 days of iron supplementation, fresh whole blood samples were extracted from saphenous vein samples 
and treated with dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for the subsequent hematologic analysis. 
This analysis was performed using the Veterinary Haematology Analyzer Element HT-5 (HESKA, United States).

Faeces consistency and water content
To evaluate the consistency and water content, in the 5th and 9th week, fresh faeces were collected during a 
2-hour interval from 8:00 to 10:00 h. At the end of this period, the number of stools was recorded and weighed. 
The qualitative appearance (consistency) was determined visually following a 3-point scale: normal (3 points), 
semi-solid (2 points) and diarrhoea (1 point) according to the procedure described by Sung et al. 13. The faeces 
were then dried in an oven for 24 h at 70ºC and weighed again. The water content was determined by calculating 
the difference between the weight of fresh and dry faeces, expressing the result in %: (water content (mg) / fresh 
faeces (mg)) * 100.
Weight, number, and frequency of bowel movements evaluation
Faeces deposited during the next 22 h were collected. Their weight, number and frequency of bowel movements 
were determined from the quantification of the number of faeces made by each animal during the first 2 h and 
the following 22 h, as well as the record of its total fresh and dry weight (after being dried in an oven for 24 h at 
70 °C).
Analyses at the endpoint
After 9 weeks, rats were sacrificed under anaesthesia (pentobarbital sodium, 60  mg kg − 1 bw, Merck, 
Danvers, MA, USA) after 6 h of diurnal fasting, administered intraperitoneally. Euthanasia was completed by 
exsanguination via cardiac puncture. Serum and plasma were obtained by centrifugation and stored at -80 °C 
until analysis. Stomach, small and large intestines were rapidly removed, weighed, frozen in liquid nitrogen and 
stored at -80 °C until analysis. The liver and cecum were kept for possible analyses of interest to the company.
Iron-related parameters analysis
At the end of the study, a portion of blood was processed to obtain serum, and another portion was treated with 
dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) to analyse haemoglobin levels in fresh blood. Iron bound 
to transferrin levels and TIBC were measured in serum.
Hepcidin analysis
Circulating hepcidin levels were measured by an ELISA kit (Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) at the 
beginning of the repletion period and at the end of the study to calculate the hepcidin induction ratio.
Cytokines analysis
Twenty and fifty milligrams of duodenum and stomach, respectively, were homogenised in one mL of tissue lysate 
buffer and centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 5 min at 4ºC. Protein quantification of the homogenates was performed by 
BCA protein assay kit (Thermofisher, Massachusetts, United States). Levels of the pro-inflammatory chemokine 
TNF-α and anti-inflammatory chemokine IL-10 were analysed in the stomach and duodenum using an ELISA 
kit (Thermofisher, Massachusetts, United States) as markers of inflammation.

Histological analysis
For histology, tissue samples measuring approximately 2 cm were excised from the stomach and duodenum 
post-mortem; they were fixed in 10% formalin, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Sections measuring 5-µm 
were cut longitudinally at the midline of the paraffin-embedded duodenal samples. For the stomach, sections 
were stained with haematoxylin/eosin, whereas for the duodenum, one section was stained with haematoxylin/
eosin and another section with a Prussian blue iron staining. The number of iron foci detected in duodenal 
samples was quantified.
Statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 program. Grubbs’ test was used to detect 
outliers, which were discarded for subsequent analyses. The normality assumption was determined using the 
Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and the homoscedasticity among groups was assessed using Levene’s test. When one 
or both conditions were unmet, data was transformed to a base-10 logarithm to obtain a normal distribution.
For the parameters measured on several occasions, the ANOV A test of repeated measures (rm-ANOV A) 
was used (t, time effect; T, treatment effect; txT, time x treatment interaction effect; p < 0.05). Differences among 
the treatments were evaluated by the one-way ANOV A (1w ANOV A) test followed by Duncan’s new multiple 
range test (as a post hoc test). In contrast, differences between diets were evaluated by the Mann-Whitney test, 
as indicated in the respective figure legend. Student’s t-test was used for single statistical comparisons. Data are 
presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance level was set at bilateral 5% 
(p < 0.05).
Results
Body weight, food intake and feed efficiency
No treatment effects were reported among groups regarding weekly and final cumulative food intake (Fig.  2C 
and D). Body weight and body weight gain were reported weekly (Fig.  2A and B). During both repletion and 
side effects evaluation periods, all groups displayed a gradual increase in their body weight (t effect, p < 0.05, rm-
ANOV A) (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, in weeks 7, 9, 10, 11, 13, 14, and 17, Def-LFe1 animals exhibited significantly 
higher body weight gain than those supplemented with FeSO 4 (Fig. 2A). Def-LFe1 animals also resulted in the 
highest mean body weight gain. No significant differences were reported among the other Fe-supplemented 
groups. Figure  2E illustrates that animals supplemented with LFe1 exhibited all groups’ highest mean feed 
efficiency values. The feed efficiency values for animals supplemented with LFe1 were significantly higher than 
the Con and Def-FeS groups. This indicates an increased conversion of energy intake into body weight for the 
Def-LFe1 group of animals.
Haemoglobin parameters
After an iron depletion period of 24 days, a significant decrease in blood haemoglobin levels was reported in 
iron-deficient diet-fed animals (12.7 ± 0.2 g/dL) in comparison with control diet-fed animals (18.4 ± 0.3 g/dL) 
(p < 0.001 Mann-Whitney test) (Fig. 3A).
To assess the capacity of the different iron supplements to reverse iron deficiency, haemoglobin levels were 
monitored at 14 and 21 days of the repletion period. As shown in Fig.  3B–D, all the iron supplement-treated 
groups significantly increased haemoglobin levels at days 14 and 21 in comparison to day 0, showing a complete 
recovery of haemoglobin levels ( t, p < 0.001, rm-ANOV A) (Figs. 3B–D). However, no significant differences 
among iron-treated animals were reported on days 14 and 21 (Figs. 3B–D).
The HRE was determined at days 14 and 21 of the repletion period in all the supplemented groups. As 
shown in Fig. 4, the best repletion efficiency was reported on day 14 of iron supplementation, while a significant 
decrease in HRE levels was observed on 21 days in all the groups (Fig. 4A). No differences were reported among 
Fe-supplemented groups.
Additionally, for the analysis of the relative bioavailability value (RBV) after the repletion period, Def-Fes 
animals were used as the reference group (RBVHRE and corrected RBV∆Hb  of De-FeS group = 1).
As shown in Fig.  5, the three iron supplements tested in the present study exhibited a similar iron 
bioavailability to the reference compound (FeSO 4) at days 14 and 21, with no significant differences reported 
among the different groups.
Iron metabolism-related parameters
After 21 days of iron supplementation, iron metabolism-related parameters significantly changed between 
animals fed an iron-deficient diet and their counterparts fed a control diet (Fig.  6). Specifically, lower levels 
of serum iron (Fig.  6A) and transferrin saturation (Fig.  6B) and higher levels of transferrin (Fig.  6C), UIBC 
(Fig. 6D) and TIBC (Fig.  6E) were reported in deficient diet-fed animals when compared to control diet-fed 
ones.
As shown in Fig.  7, after 21 days of iron repletion, all iron-supplemented groups displayed a significant 
increase in serum iron levels and transferrin saturation and a sharp decrease in TIBC, transferrin and UIBC 
levels compared to Def-VH animals.

Discussion
In the present study, all the tested iron supplements were shown to fully reverse iron deficiency after 14 days 
of supplementation. However, for the FeSO 4-treated animals, several parameters, such as significantly lower 
body weight gain, lower feed efficiency, duodenal villus height to crypt depth ratio, and higher colonic IL6 gene 
expression, were reported. These would suggest potential side effects associated with FeSO 4 supplementation, 
including reduced nutrient absorption. Surprisingly, these were not reported in Def-LFe1 animals, highlighting 
the ability of LIPOFER™ to revert iron deficiency while potentially minimising iron-related side effects.
Food intake, body weight, and feed efficiency levels were monitored throughout the chronic iron 
supplementation to detect potential side effects. As mentioned, no differences were observed in food intake levels 
for each group, which indicated that calorie intake was also identical. In terms of body weight gain, as expected, 
the Con group’s body weight gain was the lowest, most likely due to the difference in the calorific content of the 
diet versus the Fe-deficient groups (3.1 kcal/g vs. 4 kcal/g, respectively). The increased conversion of energy 
intake into body weight, i.e., feed efficiency demonstrated by Def-LFe1-treated groups in comparison with the 
Def-FeS group, explains, at least in part, the higher body weight gain reported in Def- LFe1 group compared to 
their Def-FeS counterparts. These results highlight the ability of LIPOFER™ supplementation to enhance feed 
efficiency and promote growth in this animal model. Previous studies have shown that iron supplementation 
promotes growth in anaemic 21-day rats 15. Our study extends these findings by demonstrating a comparable 
weight gain effect in adult, iron-replete rats, thereby providing new evidence that the micro-encapsulated iron 
formulation LIPOFER™ can enhance body weight gain even when baseline iron stores are adequate. On the other 
hand, the lower feed efficiency reported in the animals supplemented with FeSO 4 could suggest a reduction in 
nutrient absorption induced by FeSO 4 supplementation. Similar results were observed in a preliminary study 
performed in our group comparing FeSO4 with LIPOFER™ at an equivalent human dosage of 200 mg/day for 15 
days (unpublished data). The supplementation with a high dose of FeSO4 caused a significant reduction in body 
weight gain and feed efficiency compared to the control group. This result would indicate a decreased conversion 
of energy intake into body weight gain in this group of animals, which could be interpreted as a possible adverse 
effect on nutrient absorption. Interestingly, these results were not reported in those rats supplemented with 
LIPOFER™ at high doses, again suggesting Def-LFe1 supports greater nutrient absorption.
Regarding the capacity of the different iron supplements to reverse iron deficiency, all iron supplements were 
able to fully recover haemoglobin levels on day 14. In addition, HRE was determined by the ratio of dietary 
and supplemented iron conversion into circulating haemoglobin. Considering that the significant role of iron 
in haemoglobin is oxygen binding, the lower HRE obtained at day 21 when compared to day 14 in all Fe-
supplemented groups would indicate that haemoglobin has bound all possible oxygen and, consequently, a lower 
amount of the iron-supplemented during those days was incorporated into haemoglobin. RBV of Fe from the 
tested iron supplements did not show significant differences among groups. Even though FeSO 4 is considered 
the reference compound for food fortification due to its high bioavailability, the present study reported a similar 
bioavailability among the different iron supplementations. A possible explanation for the absence of differences 
noted in the present study could be the higher doses administered of each compound (6.2 mg Fe/kg body weight, 
equivalent to a human dose of 80 mg of elemental iron). In other words, the efficacy of all products administered 
at a high dose in rapidly reversing anaemia within 14 days may account for the absence of differences in the 
bioavailability among the different iron supplements.

occurs because the liver produces more transferrin to optimise the utilisation of the limited iron supply17. In the 
present study, all iron-supplemented groups displayed a significant increase in serum iron levels and transferrin 
saturation and a sharp decrease in TIBC, transferrin and UIBC levels. Interestingly, LIPOFER™ induced a more

Hepcidin, a peptide hormone derived from the liver, is crucial in regulating systemic iron homeostasis. 
Hepcidin operates by inhibiting the flow of iron into the plasma through several mechanisms, including the 
absorption in the duodenum, the release from macrophages recycling old red blood cells, and the mobilisation 
of stored iron from hepatocytes 21. Two different mechanisms regulate the production of hepcidin: (a) it is 
increased in response to plasma and liver iron levels, serving as a feedback mechanism to maintain stable body 
iron levels, and (b) it is increased by inflammation as a defence mechanism to limit extracellular iron availability 
to microbes22. As it was reported in the present study, it has been described that in conditions of iron deficiency, 
the reduction of hepcidin production is an adaptation mechanism that facilitates dietary and pharmacological 
iron absorption23.

It is widely described that iron supplementation stimulates the production of hepcidin, which limits iron 
absorption obtained from the diet and further regulates iron storage as a mechanism to avoid iron overload 22. 
However, as mentioned above, increased hepcidin levels have also been associated with increased inflammation, 
as pro-inflammatory cytokines also regulate its production 21. Experimental evidence suggests that excessive 
iron in the lumen may also harm the intestinal mucous membrane. Despite its key role in cellular processes, free 
iron in the intestine can generate toxic free radicals that may directly impair intestinal epithelium’s integrity via 
oxidative stress. The production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), in turn, can trigger an inflammatory response 
both locally in the gut and at a systemic level 24. Consequently, inflammatory biomarkers were quantified in 
stomach and duodenal samples from all experimental groups to gain insight into this issue.
The lack of differences among iron-supplemented animals regarding inflammatory biomarkers at the level of 
stomach and duodenum would indicate that the chronic administration of an equivalent human dose of 80 mg 
of elemental iron through different iron supplements is not promoting a pro-inflammatory response in the 
animals in these specific areas. These facts are corroborated by the absence of differences in gut measurements 
(large intestine and colon weight and length), faecal characteristics assessment (faecal consistency and humidity 
and bowel movements’ frequency) and in the quantification of circulating hepcidin levels.
Considering the suggested impact on nutrient absorption induced by chronic supplementation of FeSO4, the 
analysis of the expression of genes associated with intestinal inflammation and permeability in duodenum and 
colon was suggested to provide mechanistic information of the subtle effects induced by FeSO4 and LIPOFER™ 
supplements. Claudin and occludin are considered the most important tight junction membrane proteins, 
which determine the permeability of the intestinal barrier 25,26. Related to them, zonulin 1 is also considered 
an essential regulator of tight junction assembly in epithelia 26. A decrease in the protein levels of these tight 
junctions would lead to an increased permeability in the gut27resulting in a state of poor nutrient assimilation28. 
Finally, the gene encoding the mucin 2 protein (MUC2) is involved in the production of mucin, one of the main 
components in the formation of the mucous layer of the intestinal barrier, which protects the intestine from 
external aggressions29.   Given the lack of reported intestinal disturbances in the present study, the lower mRNA 
levels of OCLN observed in Def_LFe1 animals did not appear to be relevant. A possible explanation for these 
results is that gene expression data do not always correlate with protein levels. Therefore, quantifying the protein 
levels of some of these tight junctions would help clarify this issue.

In the samples obtained from the colon, Def-LFe1 animals displayed higher MUC2 mRNA levels and lower 
IL6 mRNA than those animals treated with FeSO4. IL-6 plays a central role in the pathogenesis of inflammatory-
based disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease 30.  In addition, previous studies have reported that iron 
overloading induced by the chronic supplementation of iron can promote intestinal inflammation (increased 
IL-6) and alter the intestinal barrier (decreased Muc-2) 31. More specifically, rodent studies have shown that 
FeSO4 administration led to increased levels of inflammation-related biomarkers, such as IL6 and TNFα 32,33. 
These data come from colitis models, where pre-existing mucosal injury and heightened oxidative stress make 
these animals especially sensitive to the pro-oxidant properties of soluble ferrous salts. In the present study, the 
obtained results in the colon could suggest a slight stimulation of pro-inflammatory biomarkers production by 
the chronic FeSO4 supplementation and, consequently, a subtle negative effect on the intestinal mucosal barrier 
in this tissue, a result that was not reported in Def-LFe1 animals. However, as previously stated, these results 
should be validated through a protein expression analysis of these biomarkers in the colonic tissue.
Prior research has indicated that extended and high-dose iron supplementation regimens can harm the 
intestinal mucosa, primarily triggered by oxidative stress and inflammatory mechanisms 34. In this study, 
histological examinations on the duodenal tissue and stomach were conducted to investigate the potential 
adverse impacts of iron supplementation. Interestingly, Def-LFe1 animals exhibited a significantly higher villus 
height to crypt depth ratio than Def-FeS animals. Villus height to crypt depth ratio is considered one of the 
most significant parameters associated with nutrient absorption. An increase in this ratio is correlated with 
improved digestion and absorption of nutrients35. Interestingly, these results would be aligned with the higher 
body weight gain and feed efficiency differences reported in Def-LFe1 groups compared to Def-FeS animals, 
suggesting a reduced nutrient absorption caused by chronic supplementation of FeSO4 in comparison with those 
supplemented with LIPOFER™. However, further studies should be conducted to provide more insight into this 
issue.
The quantification of iron foci in the duodenal tissue showed a residual increase in the amount of iron foci in 
Def-LFe1 animals than in Def-FeS animals and the Def-Fe2 group, the latter also receiving microencapsulated 
iron pyrophosphate. As shown in the representative images of Prussian blue iron staining, iron foci were detected 
and quantified in the lamina propria of the intestinal mucosa, precisely at the top of the villi. Lamina propria 
is characterised by containing several immune cells, such as macrophages, eosinophils, and lymphocytes 36.

Macrophages play a pivotal role in iron trafficking by recycling the substantial amount of iron acquired through 
haemoglobin degradation, demonstrating a unique adaptability to manage and store iron quantities that might 
be toxic to other cell types 37. Interestingly, duodenal macrophages have also been described to modulate iron 
absorption and transfer to circulation by controlling transferrin availability in the lamina propria villi38. Although 
additional studies should be performed to shed more light on the relevance of these findings, a potentially higher 
iron recycling rate performed by macrophages could account for the higher number of iron foci quantified in the 
group supplemented with LIPOFER™ microcapsules compared to the groups supplemented with FeSO4 and the 
other microencapsulated ferric pyrophosphate product.
Previous in vitro studies have proposed an alternative mechanism involving the absorption of iron 
microparticles39,40. Microfold (M) cells represent a distinct subset of intestinal epithelial cells found in the gut-
associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) of the Peyer’s patches in the small intestine. These cells are recognised for 
triggering mucosal immune responses on the apical membrane of M cells, facilitating the transportation of 
microbes and insoluble particles across the epithelial cell layer from the gut lumen to the lamina propria. This 
transfer enables interactions with immune cells in the lamina propria 41,42. Despite the well-described role of 
these cells in promoting the exposure of microbial antigens to the mucosal immune system, it has been suggested 
that M-cells can also facilitate the intestinal absorption of micronised iron particles43. Thus, the higher number of 
iron foci observed in Def-LFe1 animals could be attributed to enhanced iron absorption through this alternative 
pathway by this product.
Conclusions
Considering the study’s primary results, it can be concluded that each iron source fully reverted iron deficiencies 
after 14 days of supplementation. However, based on the decrease in TIBC and transferrin levels, coupled with 
the higher haemoglobin levels, LIPOFER™ microcapsules demonstrated a higher absorption rate than the 
Sunactive® product, further highlighting the efficacy of this microencapsulation technology. Similarly, LIPOFER™ 
supplementation resulted in a higher number of iron foci, further emphasising its enhanced absorption through 
M-cells compared to the Ferrous bisglycinate.
Interestingly, Def-LFe1 supplementation also resulted in a greater feed efficiency than the other iron 
supplements. Regarding potential side effects, FeSO 4-treated animals promoted subtle increases in markers of 
intestinal inflammation and altered intestinal barrier parameters, effects that were not detected in the other iron-
supplemented groups, including Def-LFe1. This study has highlighted the ability of the LIPOFER™ technology 
to be an effective product for reversing iron deficiency while minimising any unwanted side effects frequently 
reported for other iron supplements.
