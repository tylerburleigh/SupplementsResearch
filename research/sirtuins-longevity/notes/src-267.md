## Core Findings

Resveratrol at 22 mg/kg/day administered to 1-year-old mice on a high-calorie (60% fat) diet reduced mortality by 30% at 2 years and shifted physiology toward that of standard-diet mice. Benefits included improved insulin sensitivity, reduced organ pathology (liver and pancreas), increased hepatic mitochondrial number, and preserved motor function. These results were presented as evidence that resveratrol acts as a caloric restriction mimetic via sirtuin activation.

## Key Evidence

1. **Survival**: HC + resveratrol (HCR) vs HC controls diverged at 15 months; 30% reduction in death at 24 months (statistically significant)
2. **Motor function**: Rotarod performance of HCR mice improved with age to become indistinguishable from standard diet (SD) mice; no correlation with body weight
3. **Metabolic health**: HCR mice had lower glucose and insulin levels, paralleling SD group; decreased glucose/insulin persistence after glucose challenge
4. **Organ pathology**: At 18 months, resveratrol prevented HC-induced liver enlargement, fatty liver (lipid droplet accumulation), and pancreas damage
5. **Mitochondria**: HCR livers had more mitochondria but lower mitochondrial gene activation (paradoxical finding suggesting lower turnover)
6. **Gene expression**: 127 pathways significantly altered by resveratrol, including TCA cycle, glycolysis, sterol biosynthesis; overlap with caloric restriction pathways identified

## Methodology

- **Subjects**: 1-year-old mice
- **Groups**: Standard diet (SD), High-calorie diet (HC, 60%+ fat), SD + resveratrol (SDR), HC + resveratrol (HCR)
- **Dose**: 22 mg/kg/day (higher of two doses tested; presented because effects were more prominent)
- **Duration**: Followed to at least 24 months of age
- **Measures**: Survival curves, rotarod (motor function), plasma glucose/insulin, organ histology (liver, pancreas), mitochondrial counts, RNA hybridization gene expression profiling
- **Note**: Prior lifespan extension shown in yeast, fruit flies, and fish with resveratrol

## Limitations

- This is a secondary source (wine industry summary), not the original paper. No direct access to statistical details, sample sizes, p-values, or confidence intervals.
- The study used mice on a high-calorie diet; effects on normal-diet mice were reportedly less dramatic (the lower dose results, not presented here, may illuminate this).
- The 30% survival benefit is specifically mortality reduction from high-calorie diet harm -- not lifespan extension beyond normal. This distinction is important for the sirtuin-longevity debate.
- No human data; the paper's conclusion that doses are "achievable in humans" is speculative.
- Funded summary has wine industry sponsorship, introducing potential framing bias (though the underlying research is published in Nature).
