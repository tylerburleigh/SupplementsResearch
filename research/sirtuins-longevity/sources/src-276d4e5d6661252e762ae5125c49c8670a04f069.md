Abstract
Background Recently, the beneficial effects of human umbilical cord mesenchymal stromal cell (hucMSC)-derived 
small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) in mitigating skin aging through multiple mechanisms have been widely reported. 
β-Nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) is an iconic anti-aging drug that increases NAD+ levels in the body to 
slow down, ameliorate, and prevent various phenotypes associated with aging, but its high water solubility, low 
permeability, and instability limit its clinical application. Based on this, we applied electroporation to construct NMN-
loaded hucMSC-sEVs (NMN-sEVs) to improve their stability and efficacy and to enhance their potential for translational 
application in medical aesthetics and anti-aging.
Methods D-galactose was applied to construct a mouse skin aging model, based on which comparative analyses 
of topical and nano-microneedle administration were performed to determine the optimal delivery method of sEVs 
in vivo experiments. After constructing NMN-sEVs by electroporation, high-performance liquid chromatography 
was applied to detect the loading efficiency, and the effects of NMN-sEVs on delaying skin aging were assessed by 
histological analysis. In addition, the defense effects of NMN-sEVs against cellular senescence were verified by reactive 
oxygen species assay, β-galactosidase staining, qRT-PCR, Western blot, and cellular immunofluorescence. Finally, the 
roles of NMN-sEVs in remodeling mitochondrial function and delaying cellular senescence through mitochondrial 
autophagy were assessed by mitochondrial mass, function, and autophagy level assays.

Introduction
As we age, all of the body’s organs undergo deteriora -
tion, with skin aging being particularly evident [ 1]. As 
the body’s primary superficial organ, the skin maintains 
continuous interaction with ambient surroundings while 
performing essential barrier functions against dehydra -
tion, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and pathogens [2, 3]. Skin 
aging is influenced by both internal and external factors, 
leading to a deterioration in the skin’s structural integrity 
and a reduction in its physiological functions [ 4]. Endog-
enous skin aging occurs due to gradual changes that 
lead to a buildup of cellular damage, resulting in thin -
ner skin and the appearance of fine lines [ 5]. Exogenous 
skin aging is linked to lifestyle and environmental expo -
sures that contribute to aging, with photo-aging resulting 
from UV radiation being the most prevalent factor [ 6]. 
Results Our data suggested that NMN-sEVs could improve skin aging in mice, delay cellular senescence, and restore 
cellular mitochondrial dysfunction. Notably, NMN-sEVs treatment increased intracellular NAD+ levels and SIRT3 
expression, as well as rescued the inhibition of senescence-induced mitochondrial autophagy, suggesting a role for 
NMN-sEVs in the remodeling of mitochondrial function through mitochondrial autophagy. Additionally, the use of the 
SIRT3 inhibitor 3-TYP suppressed the positive effects of NMN-sEVs on cellular senescence, mitochondrial function, and 
mitochondrial autophagy while restoring senescence-associated characteristics.
Conclusion Overall, our findings revealed a mechanism by which NMN-sEVs attenuated mitochondrial dysfunction 
and rescued cellular senescence by promoting NAD+/SIRT3 pathway-mediated mitophagy and might provide a 
promising strategy for anti-aging pharmaceuticals.

Glycosylation is another key factor in skin aging that has 
received attention in recent years in addition to UV radi -
ation [7]. D-galactose (D-gal), as a powerful glycosylation 
agent, produces advanced glycation end products (AGEs) 
in the organism and accumulates in the skin, which dis -
rupts cellular function by inducing oxidative stress and 
ultimately accelerates skin aging [8]. Therefore, D-gal has 
been extensively utilized in aging models for anti-aging 
research worldwide [9]. Notably, the skin has a cutaneous 
neuroendocrine system that can be activated by environ -
mental or endogenous stressors and produce neuroendo-
crine mediators to maintain communication between the 
skin and the brain. A study showed that after exposure 
of mouse skin to UV radiation, the cutaneous hypotha -
lamic-pituitary-adrenal axis increased post-exposure 
cortisol levels, induced a stress response in the hippo -
campus, impaired neurogenesis, and decreased synap -
tic protein expression, suggesting that the skin affected 
brain function [ 10]. In addition, as senescence occurs, 
senescent skin can transmit senescence from the skin to 
other tissues and organs through the secretion of senes -
cence-associated secretory phenotypes (SASPs), thereby 
affecting whole-body senescence [ 11]. SASP genes are 
upregulated during senescence, and senescent cells drive 
whole-body senescence through the SASP network regu -
lated by NF-κB or C/EBP β [ 12]. Others, such as miRNAs 
or the rapamycin receptor (mTOR) pathway, also play a 
role in SASP regulation [ 13]. A recent study showed that 
the presence of senescent cells in the dermis of young 
mice resulted in increased proximal and distal host tis -
sue senescence, along with increased frailty, impaired 
musculoskeletal function, and a significant decline in 
cognitive function, as well as an increase in the expres -
sion of senescence-associated markers in hippocampal 
brain regions [ 14]. Therefore, targeting skin senescence 
through topical administration of anti-aging drugs may 
contribute to the development of new anti-aging strate -
gies to delay the onset of systemic aging and age-related 
diseases. Currently, emerging anti-aging strategies tar -
get the elimination of senescent cells and the modula -
tion of SASPs [ 15– 18]. Senolytics, drugs that selectively 
eliminate senescent cells, have been applied topically to 
the skin, showing potential anti-aging effects. In addi -
tion, both metformin and rapamycin have been applied 
topically to human skin [ 19, 20]. Rapamycin significantly 
reduced P16 levels, increased collagen VII, and showed 
an overall improvement in visible skin structure [ 19]. 
However, more studies are still needed to better elucidate 
the efficacy of these anti-aging agents on skin aging as 
well as systemic aging and the role of skin aging in sys -
temic aging.
Research has demonstrated that the buildup of senes -
cent cells in the epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous 
tissue leads to skin aging [ 21]. These senescent cells stop 
proliferating but can remain metabolically active and 
induce peripheral cellular senescence through the secre -
tion of SASPs, such as inflammatory factors, chemokines, 
extracellular vesicles (EVs), matrix metalloproteinases 
(MMPs), and growth factors, and lead to persistent 
chronic inflammation (inflammaging), resulting in tis -
sue dysfunction as well as the emergence of a senescent 
phenotype [ 22– 25]. Senescent cells are also featured by 
telomere shortening, metabolic changes, mitochondrial 
dysfunction, and reduced autophagy [ 22, 26, 27]. Mito -
chondrial dysfunction represents a significant character -
istic of cellular senescence. Dysfunctional mitochondria 
result in impaired mitochondrial integrity and biogen -
esis, diminished mitochondrial membrane potential, 
elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and 
ultimately accelerated cellular senescence or individual 
aging [ 28– 32]. Similarly, mitochondrial dysfunction dis -
rupts redox homeostasis, leading to cytoplasmic NADH 
accumulation, and decreased NAD +/NADH ratios result 
in ATP exhaustion and cell cycle arrest [ 31, 33]. In addi -
tion, NAD + levels and autophagic activity decline with 
senescence, leading to impaired communication between 
mitochondria and the nucleus as well as the accumula -
tion of damaged organelles, including mitochondria, 
whereas mitochondrial elimination induced by mito -
chondrial autophagy ameliorates cellular senescence and 
inhibits the secretion of SASP factors [34– 37]. It has been 
shown that autophagy and mitochondrial quality control 
can be enhanced by NAD + supplementation, thereby 
promoting tissue rejuvenation and organismal longev -
ity. Drivers of this process are NAD +-depleting enzymes, 
such as sirtuin family deacetylases (SIRTs) [ 38]. Among 
them, SIRT3, which is present inside mitochondria, has 
been documented to promote mitochondrial autophagy 
via the mitochondrial SIRT3-PGAM5-FUNDC1 path -
way [ 39]. Therefore, supplementing NAD + precursors 
like β-nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) to increase 
NAD+ levels and thus promote mitochondrial autoph -
agy is of broad guidance in the quest to delay cellular 
senescence.
Stem cell therapies, represented by mesenchymal stro -
mal cells (MSCs), have received significant interest in 
tissue regenerative medicine research in recent years. 
MSCs can promote the repair of damage in various tis -
sues and organs, especially showing significant efficacy in 
the treatment of some clinically complex and refractory 
diseases like diabetes and spinal cord injuries [ 40, 41]. 
As an important component of MSC paracrine secre -
tion, EVs retain the inherent regenerative capabilities of 
MSCs. Furthermore, they exhibit enhanced stability and 
reduced immunogenicity, which makes them extensively 
applied in tissue regeneration following diverse forms of 
injuries. EVs are nano-sized vesicles wrapped in a lipid 
bilayer, capable of being secreted by nearly all cell types,

and are abundant in various bioactive substances. Based 
on particle size, the classifications include small EVs 
(sEVs, < 200 nm) and large EVs (lEVs, > 200 nm) [42]. Our 
early research demonstrated that human umbilical cord 
MSC (hucMSC)-derived sEVs (hucMSC-sEVs) contain -
ing 14-3-3ζ protein have the potential to accelerate ROS 
clearance, inhibit DNA damage, and suppress apoptosis 
by upregulating SIRT1 in keratinocytes, thereby attenu -
ating UV radiation- and H 2O2-induced oxidative stress 
damage [43]. In addition, hucMSC-sEVs could positively 
regulate pregnancy zone protein to inhibit the expres -
sion of MMP1, the main enzyme in the MMP family that 
degrades type I collagen, thereby preventing UV-induced 
skin photoaging [ 44]. However, the roles of natural sEVs 
in delaying skin aging are limited, and engineering modi -
fications such as anti-aging drugs can enhance the effi -
cacy of sEVs. NMN, as a precursor to NAD +, enhances 
autophagy and normalizes mitochondrial function by 
increasing NAD+ levels, thereby delaying, improving, and 
preventing various aging-related phenotypes, as well as 
age-induced metabolic disorders and age-related diseases 
[45, 46]. However, NMN has high water solubility, low 
permeability, and instability. Considering the potential of 
NMN in delaying skin aging and the need to enhance the 
therapeutic effects of sEVs, modifying sEVs with NMN is 
an effective strategy to couple the advantages of both.
In this study, we developed an NMN nano-drug 
delivery system and identified the beneficial effects of 
NMN-loaded hucMSC-sEVs (NMN-sEVs) in delaying 
skin aging. We have confirmed that NMN-sEVs could 
improve skin aging by enhancing NAD + levels to pro -
mote mitochondrial autophagy. Furthermore, as a NAD+-
dependent deacetylase, SIRT3 plays a crucial role in the 
regulation of mitochondrial function and the modulation 
of mitochondrial autophagy. In summary, our research 
examined the regulatory influences and potential mech -
anisms by which NMN-sEVs affect mitochondria and 
aging, revealing a therapeutic pathway that links NMN, 
sEVs, autophagy, mitochondrial function, and skin aging.
Materials and methods
Cell isolation and culture
With the approval of the Ethics Committee of the Fourth 
Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University, primary huc -
MSCs were obtained according to the previously estab -
lished tissue explants adherent method [ 47]. In brief, the 
umbilical cord’s vascular tissue was removed first. Then 
the remaining tissue was diced into 2  mm³ fragments 
and arranged uniformly in a culture dish (Corning). The 
petri dishes were inverted and placed in a cell culture 
incubator (Thermo Fisher) for approximately 60  min. 
After the tissue blocks adhered, minimum essential 
medium α (α-MEM, Gibco) containing 15% fetal bovine 
serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin 
(Bioind) was added for primary culture. The spent cul -
ture medium was changed every 3 days for approxi -
mately 10 days. Carefully removed the umbilical cord 
tissue when a large number of cells appeared at the edges 
of the umbilical cord tissue and grew densely in a swirl -
ing pattern. Cells were collected by trypsin digestion and 
transferred to α-MEM medium containing 10% FBS for 
further expansion, and the resulting cells were 1st gen -
eration hucMSCs.
EVs were removed from FBS by centrifugation at 4 °C, 
100,000  g for 16  h (EVs-depleted FBS). The hucMSCs 
were collected by trypsin digestion and transferred to 
α-MEM medium containing 10% FBS for cell culture. 
When the fusion of hucMSCs reached 50–70%, the cells 
were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline 
(PBS, Meilunbio) and replaced with α-MEM medium 
containing 10% EVs-depleted FBS, and then the super -
natant was collected after 48 h of incubation. The above 
steps were repeated, and the supernatants from the 
3rd to the 7th generation of cells were collected for the 
extraction of hucMSC-sEVs.
Dermal fibroblasts (DFs) were primarily isolated from 
the dorsal skin of 1–2  day-old SD rats. The dorsal skin 
of SD rats was cut and scraped to remove blood vessels 
and adipose tissue. The dermis and epidermis were gen -
tly separated after the addition of 0.25% Dispase enzyme 
(Sigma) for overnight digestion at 4 °C. The dermis was 
cut into 1–2 mm 3 pieces and evenly affixed to the bottom 
of the petri dish. Subsequently, they were cultured under 
conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO2 for approximately 7 days. 
The spent culture medium was replaced every 3 days dur-
ing this period using α-MEM containing 10% FBS and 1% 
penicillin-streptomycin to maintain growth. Generation 
3 to 5 cells were used for subsequent cell experiments. 
DFs were identified by Western blotting and cellular 
immunofluorescence using α-SMA, vimentin, and CK10.
Separation, identification, and tracking of hucMSC-sEVs
HucMSC-sEVs were extracted using differential ultra -
centrifugation [48]. The collected cell supernatants were 
centrifuged at 300 g for 10 min, 2000 g for 10 min, and 
10,000  g for 30  min at 4  °C to remove dead cells, cell 
debris, and organelles, respectively. The supernatants 
were transferred to 100 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration cen -
trifuge tubes (Millipore) and centrifuged at 2000  g for 
30  min to concentrate the sample volume. The concen -
trates were centrifuged at 100,000 g for 120 min at 4 ℃, 
and the white precipitates obtained were hucMSC-sEVs. 
The supernatants were discarded, and the precipitates 
were resuspended by adding PBS and then centrifuged 
at 100,000 g for 120 min at 4 ℃. The supernatants were 
discarded, and the precipitates were dissolved by add -
ing an appropriate amount of PBS overnight. The com -
pletely solubilized hucMSC-sEVs were sterilized by

Skin barrier recovery experiment
The degree of action of nano-microneedles on the stra -
tum corneum was evaluated by observing the closing 
speed of the pores of the stratum corneum. Mice were 
anesthetized (avertin, 1.25%, 30  µl/g, Meilunbio), and 
the hair on the back was removed and recovered for 
24 h. 100 µL of 0.4% trypan blue dye (Pricella) was first 
evenly dripped onto the dehairing back skin of the mice, 
and then the nano-microneedles were applied to the skin 
of the mice for about 20  min. The excess of the trypan 
blue dye was blotted with a dry cotton ball, and then the 
residual dye was wiped off with a cotton ball soaked in 
75% alcohol. The diffusion of trypan blue dye in the skin 
was observed at 0 min, 5 min, 10 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 
4  h, 6  h, 12  h, and 24  h after application of the nano-
microneedles, and the effect of the nano-microneedles 
on the stratum corneum was judged according to the 
time of fading of the skin staining.
Preparation of NMN-loaded hucMSC-sEVs
Using electroporation technology to prepare NMN-
sEVs. In brief, a certain amount of hucMSC-sEVs were 
mixed with NMN (> 98.0% HPLC, TCI), and the final 
concentration of hucMSC-sEVs was adjusted to 1 × 1010 
particles/mL using PBS, while the final concentration of 
NMN was adjusted to 30 mM. After mixing thoroughly, 
added the mixture solution slowly to the electrode cup. 
The following electroporation parameters were set on the 
CUY21EDIT II Super Multi-Mode Gene Electroporator 
(BEX): decay mode, pore voltage 110  V, duration 3 ms, 
rest 10 ms; driving voltage 25 V, duration 50 ms, rest 50 
ms; driving cycles 10, capacitance 940 µF. After adjusting 
the solution resistance to a suitable degree, the operation 
was performed. Upon completion of electroporation, the 
mixture was incubated under conditions of 37 °C for 1 h. 
PBS was added to dilute, and a 100 kDa MWCO ultrafil -
tration centrifuge tube was used to concentrate and wash 
away the free NMN.
The encapsulation efficiency of NMN was determined 
using ultrafiltration centrifugation [49]. The mixture after 
electroporation was placed in a 100  kDa MWCO ultra -
filtration centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 2000  g for 
30  min, with NMN-sEVs retained in the ultrafiltration 
centrifuge tube. NMN levels in the filtrate were assessed 
using HPLC to determine encapsulation rate and drug 
loading.
D-gal-induced skin senescence model and treatment
Twenty-four female ICR mice (6 weeks old, weighing 
24 g ± 2 g) were purchased and housed at the Laboratory 
Animal Center of Jiangsu University. They were kept in 
cages at a temperature of 22 ± 2  °C and given adequate 
diet and water (12 h light/dark cycle). All animal experi -
ments complied with standard guidelines for the care 
and use of laboratory animals and were approved by 
the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of 
Jiangsu University. One week prior to the formal experi -
ment, the aforementioned ICR mice were fed adaptively 
and then randomly divided into four groups of six mice 
each: Control (CTR), D-gal (D-gal), D-gal + topical + sEVs 
(sEV + TOP), and D-gal + nano-microneedles + sEVs 
(sEV + Nano-microneedle). A D-gal-induced mouse skin 
aging model was established as described previously [ 50]. 
In brief, D-gal (MedChemExpress) was dissolved in 70% 
ethanol (10 mg/mL) and 0.5 mL was topically applied to 
the shaved back skin of the mice (4 × 2  cm²) twice daily 
for 6 weeks. The shaved back skin (4 × 2 cm²) of control 
mice was coated with 0.5 mL of 70% ethanol twice daily 
for 6 weeks to exclude the influence of 70% ethanol. On 
the first day of each week, after anesthetizing mice by 
intraperitoneal injection of 1.25% avertin, hucMSC-sEVs 
(5 × 109 particles/mouse/week) were administered via 
topical or nano-microneedles once a week for 6 weeks. At 
the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized under 
anesthesia via cervical dislocation, and skin tissues were 
collected for further examination. The experimenter was 
not the designer of the experiment to exclude the influ -
ence of subjective factors on the experimental results.

mouse skin aging model and confirming the use of 
nano-microneedles as a transdermal drug delivery 
method, thirty-six 6-week-old female ICR mice were 
once again purchased and housed at the Laboratory 
Animal Center of Jiangsu University. The mice were ran -
domly divided into six groups of six mice each: Control 
(CTR), D-gal (D-gal), D-gal + nano-microneedles + PBS

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining
In brief, skin tissue sections were dewaxed, antigeni -
cally repaired, blocked, and incubated overnight with 
diluted primary antibody. Diluted secondary antibodies 
were added and incubated for 30 min. The SABC reagent 
(Boster) was then added and incubated in a humid cham-
ber for 30  min. DAB chromogenic solution was added, 
and when a brown or brownish color appeared under 
the microscope, the sections were immediately placed in 
double-distilled water and rinsed. Hematoxylin staining 
was applied for 1 min, followed by rinsing under running 
water for 15  min. After dehydration, a drop of neutral 
resin was added, and a cover slip was placed on top. The 
primary antibodies used were as follows: P53 (1:100, 
Zenbio), PCNA (1:100, CST), 53BP1 (1:100, Zenbio), and 
SIRT3 (1:100, Wanleibio).
Cellular immunofluorescence
The processed cells were fixed at room temperature with 
4% paraformaldehyde, followed by membrane permea -
bilization at room temperature, and then blocked with 
5% BSA for 2 h. The primary antibodies were incubated 
overnight under 4 °C conditions. The secondary antibod -
ies were incubated under 37  °C conditions for 30  min. 
Hoechst33342 dye (1:200, Sigma Aldrich) was used to 
stain cell nuclei. After applying an anti-fluorescence 
quencher, cell images were obtained under a microscope. 
The principal antibodies employed in this research were 
ki67 (1:100, Proteintech), P16 (1:100, Proteintech), and 
LaminB1 (1:100, Abcam).
Western blotting
Cells were lysed to extract total cellular proteins. After 
separating equal amounts of protein samples by electro -
phoresis, they were transferred to a PVDF membrane 
(Millipore). It was then closed with 5% skimmed milk for 
1 h. The membrane was then incubated with the primary 
antibodies overnight under 4  °C conditions. The mem -
brane was then incubated with the secondary antibod -
ies under 37 ℃ for 30 min. ECL (Vazyme) was applied to 
quantitatively label the proteins. Primary antibodies used 
in this study were CD9 (1:500, Proteintech), CD63 (1:500, 
Abcam), CD81 (1:500, Proteintech), HSP70 (1:500, CST), 
Alix (1:500, CST), TSG101 (1:500, Abcam), Calnexin 
(1:500, CST), β-actin (1:5000, Abclonal), CoL1A1 (1:500, 
Abclonal), P21 (1:500, Zenbio), 53BP1 (1:500, Zenbio), 
MMP-1 (1:500, Abclonal), and SIRT3 (1:500, Wanleibio).

film to prevent evaporation and incubated at 37 ℃, CO2-

light microscope and take pictures.

NMN-loaded hucMSC-sEVs modulate NAD+/SIRT3 
pathway-mediated mitochondrial autophagy to delay DFs 
aging
To further explore the specific mechanism by which 
NMN-sEVs promote mitochondrial autophagy, we 
screened aging-related genes associated with mitochon -
drial dysfunction from the Aging Atlas database (Table 
S2). We found that SIRT3, a NAD +-dependent deacety -
lase, was reported to be associated with mitochondrial 
autophagy. NAD+ levels and autophagic activity decline 
with aging, leading to impaired communication between 
mitochondria and the nucleus, as well as the accumula -
tion of damaged organelles, including mitochondria. In 
contrast, mitochondrial autophagy-induced mitochon -
drial elimination ameliorates cellular senescence and 
inhibits the secretion of SASP factors. By supplementing 
NMN, the precursor of NAD+, autophagy and mitochon-
drial quality control could be enhanced, thereby promot -
ing tissue rejuvenation and organismal lifespan extension. 
Therefore, we speculated that NMN-sEVs might delay 
cellular senescence by promoting NAD +/SIRT3 path -
way-mediated mitochondrial autophagy. To prove this 
conjecture, we found an interaction between SIRT1, a 
homologous family member of SIRT3, and NAD + by 
screening proteins interacting with NAD+ in the STITCH 
database (Fig.  5A). Therefore, we first verified the inter -
actions between SIRT3 and NAD + by molecular dock -
ing (Fig. 5B). In addition, we examined the NAD + level in 
DFs and found that the NAD + level in DFs was reduced 
after D-gal induction, while NMN-sEVs significantly 
restored the intracellular NAD + level (Fig.  5C). Cellular 
immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical staining 
showed that SIRT3 was localized on mitochondria and 
that NMN-sEVs could promote the expression of SIRT3 
more efficiently in DFs (Fig. 5D) and skin tissues (Fig. 5E) 
compared to sEVs and NMN. More importantly, Western 
blot detection of the expression of SIRT3 and mitochon -
drial autophagy-related proteins, including mTOR, LC3, 
Parkin, PINK1, and P62, and the results revealed that the 
expression of SIRT3 was increased and mitochondrial 
autophagy was activated by pretreatment with NMN-
sEVs (Fig. 5F), suggesting that NMN-sEVs might regulate 
NAD+/SIRT3 pathway-mediated mitochondrial autoph -
agy to delay aging.
To further confirm the beneficial role of SIRT3, an 
effector molecule up-regulated after NMN-sEVs inter -
vention, in delaying cellular senescence, we used 3-TYP , a 
selective inhibitor of SIRT3. DFs were treated with differ-
ent concentrations of 3-TYP , and 100 µM, which did not 
induce strong cytotoxicity, was identified as the subse -
quent concentration of 3-TYP to be used (Fig.  5G). Con-
sistent with previous findings, NMN-sEVs improved the 
proliferative viability of senescent cells, whereas 3-TYP 
treatment inhibited the improvement of cell prolifera -
tive viability by NMN-sEVs (Fig.  5H). Meanwhile, 3-TYP 
treatment attenuated NMN-sEVs-induced inhibition 
of β-galactosidase activity, suggesting that the SIRT3-
selective inhibitor, 3-TYP , inhibited the improvement of 
cellular senescence by NMN-sEVs (Fig.  5I). To further 
demonstrate that the cellular senescence-delaying effect 
of NMN-sEVs correlates with mitochondrial autophagy 
mediated by the NAD +/SIRT3 pathway, we assessed the 
effect of 3-TYP treatment on mitochondrial autophagy. 
Western blot detected the expression of mitochondrial 
autophagy-related proteins, including Parkin, PINK1, 
P62, p-mTOR, and mTOR after 3-TYP treatment, and 
the results showed that mitochondrial autophagy was 
inhibited by 3-TYP pretreatment (Fig.  5J). The mito -
chondrial ROS results showed that NMN-sEVs reduced 
mitochondrial ROS levels in senescent cells, whereas 
3-TYP treatment could again elevate the level of oxida -
tive stress in cells (Fig.  5K). Furthermore, mitochondrial 
membrane potential was elevated after NMN-sEVs inter-
vention and decreased after 3-TYP treatment (Fig.  5L). 
More importantly, mitochondrial distribution in DFs 
became dispersed and fragmented after 3-TYP treat -
ment, and co-localization of mitochondria and LC3B, as 
well as mitochondria and LAMP1, was attenuated, simi -
lar to D-gal-induced senescent DFs, suggesting that the 
activation of mitochondrial autophagy by NMN-sEVs 
was inhibited by 3-TYP (Fig.  5M). The above results con-
firmed that SIRT3 plays an important role in the activa -
tion of mitochondrial autophagy by NMN-sEVs to retard 
skin aging.

affecting the expression of a variety of inflammatory cyto-
kines, leading to inflammatory necrosis of skin cells, and 
ultimately aging or even cancer; on the other side, AGEs 
can also bind to proteins, affecting their normal struc -

skin aging is limited, and engineering EVs to enhance 
their therapeutic potential is a future trend. Therefore, we 
intend to construct an engineered EV for specific thera -
peutic use. Given the promising therapeutic potential of 
NMN in attenuating cellular senescence, restoring mito -
chondrial function, and prolonging lifespan, as well as 
its low permeability and instability in direct drug deliv -
ery, the modification of EVs with NMN presents a viable 
strategy to leverage the benefits of both. Here, we con -
structed NMN-sEVs by electroporation and verified their 
loading efficiency by HPLC. Relative to higher concentra-
tions, lower concentrations of sEVs encapsulated NMN 
more efficiently. We speculated that it might be because 
higher concentrations of sEVs are more prone to aggre -
gation under the action of the electric field rather than 
loading NMN. Regarding the application of NMN-sEVs 
in the skin, their absorption rate is limited due to the 
skin barrier. Nano-microneedles are emerging pro-per -
meation tools made of high-purity monocrystalline sili -
con with a diameter of about one-thousandth of a hair’s 
diameter, which can form open drug delivery channels 
on the skin’s surface in a short period to promote trans -
dermal penetration and absorption of topical drugs [ 61, 
62]. Moreover, the channels they form can close on their 
own in a short period, with a low possibility of infec -
tion. Compared with topical application and intrader -
mal injection, nano-microneedles are more efficient in 
drug delivery and less likely to cause skin irritation and 
inflammation. Consistently, our findings suggested that 
nano-microneedles have advantages over topical appli -
cation in effectively facilitating the entry of sEVs into the 
deeper layers of the skin and reducing skin thinning and 
collagen loss due to aging. NMN-sEVs were more effec -
tive in improving aging-induced wrinkles, skin thinning, 
reduced collagen fiber percentage, proliferation inhibi -
tion, cellular senescence, and oxidative damage than sEVs 
or NMN alone.
Characteristics of senescent cells include DNA dam -
age, metabolic changes, endoplasmic reticulum stress, 
and mitochondrial dysfunction [ 63, 64]. Mitochondria 
are crucial for modulating age-associated decline, and 
mitochondrial dysfunction induces cellular aging and 
the secretion of SASP [ 65]. In the context, we exam -
ined mitochondrial function in NMN-sEVs-treated DFs 
and found that NMN-sEVs rescued senescence-induced 
mitochondrial dysfunction, which was characterized by 
a compromised mitochondrial structure, low membrane 
potential, and high levels of ROS. We further found the 
activation of mitochondrial autophagy, the elevated level 
of NAD +, and the increase in the expression of SIRT3 
in the NMN-sEVs treatment. NAD + serves as a crucial 
coenzyme in cellular energy metabolism and may rep -
resent an adaptive mechanism in response to oxidative 
stress, participating in metabolic pathways and influ -
encing mitochondrial function [ 66]. SIRT3 is a NAD +-
dependent deacetylase located on mitochondria that 
regulates mitochondrial autophagy through metabolic 
rewiring and NAD + depletion [ 38]. In addition, SIRT3 
has the capacity to augment the activity of intrinsic 
antioxidant enzymes and inhibit the generation of ROS, 
thus safeguarding cells against oxidative injury [ 67, 68]. 
NAD+ depletion reduces the expression of SIRT3 and 
increases the acetylation level of its target proteins. As 
NMN-sEVs supplementation enhances NAD + levels, 
NMN-sEVs may activate SIRT3 expression by promot -
ing NAD+ synthesis to regulate mitochondrial autophagy. 
Therefore, we used 3-TYP to verify the regulatory effect 
of SIRT3 on mitochondrial autophagy. The findings indi -
cated that 3-TYP notably hindered the improvement of 
cellular senescence, aberrant mitochondrial function, 
and mitochondrial autophagy inhibition by NMN-sEVs 
and restored the senescence-associated phenotypic fea -
tures, confirming that SIRT3 is a key molecule in the 
regulation of mitochondrial autophagy in the process of 
senescence. In addition to mitochondria, the structure of

endoplasmic reticulum (ER) was also affected by senes -
cence. Our results indicated that D-gal induces endoplas-
mic reticulum expansion and degranulation in DFs, while 
the expanded endoplasmic reticulum in NMN-sEV group 
is significantly reduced (Figure S6). There are mitochon-
dria-ER contacts (MERCs) between the ER and mito -
chondria, and frequent exchange of substances, including 
Ca2+ and lipids, also occurs [ 69]. In addition, mitochon -
dria, ER, and MERCs are involved in ROS production 
and autophagosome assembly. As the number of cell pas-
sages increases, mitochondrial calcium uptake decreases, 
and the number of MERCs decreases in senescent cells, 
supporting the potential of MERCs as a therapeutic tar -
get for senescence that may affect cellular function and 
lifespan [ 70]. In the future, we will further investigate 
how NMN-sEVs regulate the structure and function of 
MERCs and their roles in anti-aging, providing new ideas 
and targets for anti-aging research.
There are several limitations to this study. It is essential 
to comprehend the molecular alterations in engineered 
EVs and how NMN-sEVs interact with and influence 
the behavior of senescent cells during the regeneration 
of aging skin. Here, we found that mitochondrial dys -
function increased with skin aging, including abnor -
mal mitochondrial morphology and structure, elevated 
ROS levels, mitochondrial membrane potential loss, and 
reduced levels of mitochondrial autophagy. Our data 
then showed that NMN-sEVs significantly delayed skin 
aging, such as improving skin wrinkles in aging mice in 
vivo as well as reducing oxidative stress levels in DFs in 
vitro. In particular, NMN-sEVs increased intracellular 
NAD+ levels and SIRT3 expression, as well as rescued the 
inhibition of senescence-induced mitochondrial autoph -
agy, suggesting a role for NMN-sEVs in delaying skin 
senescence through NAD +/SIRT3 activation of mito -
chondrial autophagy, which may be a potential target 
for delaying skin senescence. We demonstrated the role 
of NMN-sEVs in regulating mitochondrial autophagy 
in skin aging, but its specific regulatory mechanism still 
needs further study.
Conclusion
Our study confirmed that senescent cells show mito -
chondrial dysfunction, including abnormal mitochon -
drial morphology and structure, elevated ROS levels, 
loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, and reduced 
levels of mitochondrial autophagy. NMN-sEVs could 
rescue senescence-induced dysfunctional mitochondrial 
accumulation and inhibition of mitochondrial autoph -
agy and attenuate skin senescence through activation of 
the NAD +/SIRT3 signaling pathway, suggesting a role 
for NMN-sEVs in remodeling mitochondrial function 
through mitochondrial autophagy. Our results provide 
theoretical justification for the utilization of NMN-sEVs 
in anti-skin aging. In the future, we will investigate more 
deeply whether NMN-sEVs can be used as an anti-aging 
drug and further explore the specific mechanism by 
which NMN-sEVs regulate aging through the activation 
of mitochondrial autophagy by NAD+/SIRT3.
