Marine nutraceuticals as a source of SIRT1 and NRF2 activators for diabetes and aging-related metabolic disorders

Download PDF

Abstract
Marine ecosystems harbor a remarkable diversity of bioactive compounds with unique chemical structures and potent pharmacological activities. These marine-derived metabolites have gained increasing attention as promising therapeutic agents for metabolic disorders, particularly diabetes and aging-related diseases. This review highlights the emerging roles of marine-sourced Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) and Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2–Related Factor 2 (NRF2) activators in modulating glucose and lipid metabolism, enhancing antioxidant defense mechanisms, and ameliorating cellular senescence. We summarize the mechanistic pathways by which key marine compounds—such as fucoxanthin, bromophenols, and coral-derived steroids—modulate SIRT1 and NRF2 signaling, potentially providing synergistic effects superior to those of terrestrial or synthetic analogues. The review also addresses the challenges in pharmaceutical formulation and sustainable production, emphasizing the need for advanced biotechnological strategies and innovative delivery systems. While preclinical evidence is compelling, clinical translation remains limited by issues related to bioavailability, safety, and sustainable production. Future research should focus on clinical trials, improved formulations, and sustainable production platforms to unlock the therapeutic potential of marine natural products for diabetes and aging-related metabolic disorders.

Introduction
Marine ecosystems, renowned for their extraordinary biodiversity and chemical complexity, have long fascinated scientists as reservoirs of novel therapeutic agents [

,

,
3
]. The escalating global prevalence of chronic metabolic disorders, such as diabetes mellitus and aging-associated metabolic decline, underscores the urgent need for innovative drug candidates [
4
]. In this context, bioactive compounds derived from marine sources have emerged as promising therapeutic agents due to their capacity to target regulatory pathways fundamental to metabolic health [
5
,
6
].
The escalating global prevalence of chronic metabolic disorders, such as diabetes mellitus and aging-associated metabolic decline, underscores the urgent need for innovative drug candidates [
7
]. In this context, bioactive compounds derived from marine sources have emerged as promising therapeutic agents due to their capacity to target regulatory pathways fundamental to metabolic health. The sustainable bioproduction of these marine nutraceuticals is crucial, and concepts like "mariculture" and "blue agriculture" offer a primary avenue for their cultivation and large-scale, environmentally responsible supply [
8
]. By integrating sustainable cultivation methods like seaweed farming and offshore aquaculture, it is possible to ensure a consistent and cost-competitive supply of marine raw materials for drug development, while also providing ecological benefits such as carbon fixation. This approach frames the therapeutic potential of marine compounds within an agricultural context, highlighting the importance of reconciling therapeutic progress with environmental stewardship. This therapeutic potential lies in their ability to modulate key regulatory pathways central to metabolic health.
Among these pathways, Silent Information Regulator 2 Homolog 1 (SIRT1) [
9
] and Nuclear Factor Erythroid 2-Related Factor 2 (NRF2) have attracted considerable scientific interest [
10
]. SIRT1, a NAD + -dependent deacetylase, plays a pivotal role in the regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and cellular stress responses [
11
]. Dysregulation of SIRT1 activity is implicated in the pathogenesis of diabetes and age-related metabolic dysfunction. NRF2, a master transcription factor, orchestrates the expression of antioxidant enzymes, providing cellular protection against oxidative damage and metabolic stress [
12
,
13
]. Targeting SIRT1 and NRF2 thus represents a compelling strategy in the management of metabolic disorders. While the individual roles of SIRT1 in regulating metabolism and NRF2 in orchestrating antioxidant defense are well-established, a comprehensive review that synthesizes the emerging evidence on how marine-derived compounds dually target and exploit the synergistic crosstalk between these two pathways is currently lacking. The novelty of this study lies in its integrative perspective, consolidating fragmented findings to highlight the dual and synergistic modulation of SIRT1 and NRF2 by marine-derived bioactives. The rationality stems from addressing this overlooked intersection, which justifies the research gap by demonstrating how a unified synthesis can reveal broader therapeutic insights for combating multifactorial metabolic disorders. This gap in the literature prevents a cohesive understanding of the multi-targeted therapeutic potential of marine bioactives for complex, multifactorial diseases like diabetes and aging-related metabolic disorders.
Marine natural products offer unique structural features and pharmacological properties that often surpass those of terrestrial-derived compounds [
14
]. This review addresses the central research question: Can marine-derived bioactive compounds that activate SIRT1 and NRF2 serve as effective agents to improve metabolic health? To address this, key chemical classes of marine metabolites with SIRT1 and/or NRF2 activity are identified, along with their pharmaceutical potential and sustainable development as drug candidates for metabolic disorders.
This synthesis is based exclusively on previously published experimental studies; no new primary data are presented. A critical comparison between marine- and terrestrial-derived natural products is provided, together with an overview of the pharmaceutical challenges in drug development and practical considerations such as bioavailability, stability, drug delivery systems, and sustainability in marine drug discovery. Exemplary marine compounds—including fucoxanthin, bromophenols, and napyradiomycins—are highlighted for their documented effects on SIRT1 and NRF2 modulation, improvement of glucose homeostasis, and enhancement of cellular antioxidant defense, though their long-term efficacy and environmental impacts remain to be fully elucidated.
The review begins by summarizing the molecular roles of SIRT1 and NRF2 in metabolic regulation and their functional interplay. Recent advances in the discovery and characterization of marine-derived modulators of these pathways are presented, with particular emphasis on structural diversity, biosynthetic origins, and mechanisms of action. The advantages and limitations of marine bioactives are discussed in relation to terrestrial counterparts. Finally, the importance of sustainable sourcing and biotechnological innovation is emphasized to ensure the future viability of marine natural products as therapeutics for diabetes and aging-related metabolic disorders.
Search strategy
The literature review was conducted by systematically searching major scientific databases including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Google Scholar for relevant articles published up to May 2025. The search strategy employed combinations of the following keywords:
marine bioactive compounds, SIRT1 activators, NRF2 modulators, marine-derived metabolites, diabetes, aging-related metabolic disorders, oxidative stress, glucose metabolism, antioxidant defense, coral-derived steroids, and marine natural products.
Additional search terms included specific compound classes such as
fucoxanthin, bromophenols, napyradiomycins, and marine peptides
. Boolean operators (AND, OR) and truncation were used to broaden the search and ensure comprehensive coverage. Reference lists of selected articles and recent reviews were also screened to identify additional relevant studies. Only articles published in English were considered. Both original research articles and reviews were included to ensure a broad synthesis of mechanistic and translational evidence.
Study selection criteria
The inclusion criteria for this review encompassed peer-reviewed original research articles, reviews, and meta-analyses that focused on marine-derived bioactive compounds exhibiting demonstrated or proposed activity on the SIRT1 and/or NRF2 pathways, particularly within the context of metabolic regulation, diabetes, or aging-related disorders. Eligible studies included those employing in vitro models, in vivo animal models, and, where available, human clinical studies that investigated the effects of marine-sourced compounds on metabolic, oxidative, or cellular senescence pathways. Furthermore, studies providing evidence of molecular mechanisms or pharmacological effects—such as activation of SIRT1/NRF2, modulation of glucose metabolism, or enhancement of antioxidant responses—were included.
Conversely, publications not written in English, conference abstracts without accessible full texts, editorials, and commentaries were excluded from the review. The selection process consisted of independent screening of titles and abstracts by multiple reviewers to identify relevant articles, followed by a thorough assessment of the full texts for eligibility according to the specified criteria. Any discrepancies encountered during the selection process were resolved by consensus or, when necessary, through discussion with a third reviewer.
SIRT1 and NRF2 in metabolic regulation
The interplay of the SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways is essential to grasp the molecular intricacies of metabolic health, aging, and disease prevention. By unveiling their respective functions and interactions, the upcoming section will provide a clearer understanding of how marine-derived compounds can influence these pathways. This introduction is crucial in recognizing the molecular mechanisms underlying oxidative stress reduction, improved metabolic control, and healthspan extension.
SIRT1 pathway fundamentals
SIRT1 plays a pivotal role in regulating metabolic processes like glucose metabolism, lipid homeostasis, and insulin sensitivity [
9
]. Therefore, it becomes a therapeutic target for controlling diabetes and aging-associated metabolic disorders [
15
,
16
]. SIRT1 regulates glucose metabolism via the increase of insulin secretion, the reduction of gluconeogenesis, and the promotion of mitochondrial biogenesis. Thus, via activation of SIRT1 by deacetylating crucial transcription factors and co-activators associated with glucose metabolism, it improves insulin sensitivity and regulates glucose control, proving the importance of SIRT1 for the treatment of diabetes [
16
]. The restoration of SIRT1 in vascular cells by the molecule C3 (xyloallenoide A) can be an excellent target to prevent or alleviate cellular anti-senescence [
15
].
Another role of SIRT1 is to promote fatty acid oxidation, inhibit adipogenesis, and ameliorate metabolic syndrome. SIRT1 represents a link where metabolic and aging pathways intersect [
16
]. Several marine compounds have been identified to activate SIRT1, such as fucoxanthin and sulfated fucogalactan (SFG). Fucoxanthin, the unique marine carotenoid from brown seaweed, promotes the AMP-Activated Protein Kinase (AMPK)-SIRT1-Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma Coactivator 1-Alpha (PGC-1α) axis of murine pancreatic β-cells in vitro. Moreover, in high-fat-diet-induced and streptozotocin (STZ)-induced diabetes mice, the administration of fucoxanthin promotes insulin secretion and improves mitochondrial function [
16
]. Moreover, it was observed that a fucoxanthin diet restores renal function of db/db mice through the SIRT1/Nrf2 axis. The oral intake of fucoxanthin improved the symptoms of renal fibrosis and oxidative stress in a type 2 diabetes rodent model. Furthermore, fucoxanthin enhances glucose metabolism by improving mitochondrial function via AMPK-SIRT1-PGC-1α in vitro and in vivo by mechanisms that are not used by terrestrial bioactives [
17
]. The SFG extracted from the brown algae Laminaria japonica stimulates SIRT1 activity to promote cell viability, alleviate reactive oxygen species (ROS), and improve mitochondrial dysfunction of murine pancreatic β-cells under H
2
O
2
-induced oxidative stress. Further, SFG protects against oxidative stress-induced cell death in murine pancreatic β-cells [
16
]. Compared to terrestrial compounds, marine-derived SIRT1 activators have certain specific features, such as SFG, possessing high sulfation degrees and low molecular weight. These features might contribute to the activation of SIRT1 in these molecules, revealing potential medical and clinical applications of SFG [
16
]. Moreover, the treatment with some of the marine-derived compounds can significantly increase PGC-1α expression and promote mitochondrial biogenesis.
Marine sponges are an abundant resource that produce unique marine bioactives [
18
]. This natural resource is an attractive target for discovering SIRT1 activators with potent bioactivity. The marine sponge-derived extracts activate SIRT1 over 170%, an activity surpassing terrestrial bioactives [
19
]. Several marine sponge extracts, such as that derived from Scopalina hapalia, induce a robust SIRT1 activation along with their antioxidant activity [
19
]. These compounds are mostly made by symbiotic bacteria hosted by the sponges. Bacillus is one of the sponge-derived microbial isolates that activated SIRT1 to levels over those activated by resveratrol, the standard terrestrial SIRT1 activator [
19
]. All these results emphasize that marine symbionts, such as bacteria and sponges, are excellent resources for discovering SIRT1 activators. To apply these SIRT1 activators derived from sponges, chemical characterization and mechanistic studies are recommended for in vitro and in vivo models.
Resveratrol, a famous anti-aging natural compound, can also be isolated from marine sponges [
20
]. In the stress condition of the mussel
Mytilus coruscus
, SIRT1 expression is significantly reduced in the starved group. Although a brief starvation period elevates SIRT1 expression, the extended time of no food reduces its expression, suggesting an adaptive mechanism [
21
]. Resveratrol, a famous terrestrial bioactive that has anti-aging potential, was shown to restore SIRT1 and antioxidant enzyme activities. Further, this resveratrol, derived from marine sponges, relieves histopathological damage in the stressed mussel species.
Compared with terrestrially derived SIRT1 activators, marine-derived ones are able to modulate additional signaling pathways. Marine-derived C3 treatment could prevent vascular dysfunction in aging-induced or angiotensin II-infused cells, by restoring the SIRT1 expression, the phosphorylation status of AMPK and Akt, and their anti-senescent roles [
15
].
The high chemical diversity, special physiological role, and potent pharmacological action of marine natural products have been reported for decades. Deep-sea sponges and corals are rich in bioactives, making them a promising source of lead compounds in the ocean; however, this is not sustainable. These challenges are increasingly addressed with the emerging progress in techniques such as aquaculture, microbial fermentation, and synthetic biology to expand the production scales and provide opportunities to obtain compounds without sacrificing marine resources [
22
].
In conclusion, to fully exploit the therapeutic potential of marine-derived SIRT1 activators, further studies are still needed, and the benefits may potentially outweigh the difficulties ahead.
NRF2 signaling mechanisms
The NRF2 pathway is the main regulator of the antioxidant defense system (Fig.
1
). It governs the expression of several cytoprotective and detoxifying genes such as heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) and NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1) that scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) in various tissues [
23
]. Diabetes- and aging-related oxidative stress is the main culprit behind many metabolic diseases [
24
]. Therefore, the activation of NRF2 by pharmacological modulators of marine origin seems a promising strategy. Marine-sourced activators of NRF2 from soft coral and Streptomyces species have been shown to be efficient in improving glycemic control and inducing tissue protection by reducing ROS levels through HO-1 and NQO1 upregulation [
25
,
26
].

Mechanism of Nrf2 regulation under constitutive and oxidative stress conditions
Marine NRF2 activators exert their effects due to their novelty in molecular structure as compared to the terrestrial ones [
27
]. Their unique scaffolds, often containing halogen atoms or distinct ring systems, allow for novel interactions with biological targets. Halogenated steroids, bromophenols, and napyradiomycins, isolated from different marine sources, possess special architectures and thus display superior or multi-targeted redox modulation [
28
]. The marine natural products could be more effective in managing integrated oxidative and metabolic dysregulation than their terrestrial counterparts. This is because they often possess a multi-targeted mechanism, simultaneously modulating both pathways for a synergistic therapeutic effect. Marine NRF2 activators have been proven to have better antioxidant efficacy and, in general, present a more diverse chemical scaffold, which increases their therapeutic potential to treat diabetes and other related metabolic disorders [
26
]. Further biochemical and pharmacological exploration of marine-derived NRF2 activators could potentially unveil their potential therapeutic applications because the knowledge of their structure–activity relationships is scarce [
26
,
29
].
The functional importance of the NRF2 pathway in antioxidant defense can be further corroborated in several transgenic knockout mice. This model with suppressed antioxidant expression offers unequivocal evidence of NRF2’s role in protecting the cells from oxidative challenges [
26
]. In addition, the pharmacological evaluation of the isolated NRF2 activators indicated promising basal and inducible biological activities [
30
], suggesting that their potential treatment against chronic oxidative stress in metabolic diseases might have prolonged therapeutic effects. Furthermore, the potential alleviation of β-cell apoptosis and insulin resistance is also worth investigating in the context of chronic glucose management [
31
,
32
]. However, due to limited availability, most of the marine-derived NRF2 activators have only been screened on in vitro or acute in vivo animal models. To establish the validity of the marine NRF2-mediated treatment of metabolic diseases, the utilization of advanced in vivo systems is required for further detailed investigations on their mechanism of action and for the assessment of their translational potential.
The negative control of NRF2 activity is primarily regulated by Keap1, a cytoplasmic repressor that targets NRF2 for ubiquitination and eventual degradation under basal conditions [
33
,
34
]. Fungal metabolites from marine strains like gliorosein and terrein are potential inhibitors of the Keap1–NRF2 protein–protein interaction due to their binding to the Keap1 Kelch domain [
35
,
36
]. In molecular docking analyses of these fungal metabolites, these compounds were demonstrated to strongly bind to key amino acid residues (Arg415 and Arg483) within the Kelch β-propeller of Keap1 via hydrogen bonds [
36
]. Negative values of Gibbs free energy, as well as good force-field scores from the performed simulations [
37
], also imply a favorable and stable binding affinity of the molecules, which would allow NRF2 activation and translocation to the nucleus to stimulate transcription of antioxidant genes. One consideration should be emphasized, as marine NRF2 activators may potentially upregulate certain oncogenic or tumor growth proteins by regulating specific gene promoters. Therefore, thorough dose optimization is warranted prior to any in vivo experiments on mammalian models. The safety of these compounds will ultimately be determined after rigorous pharmacological, in vitro, and in vivo investigation of their potential toxicity.
Sustained or non-specific activation of NRF2 can have a ‘dark side’ and is linked to promoting the survival and chemoresistance of cancer cells [
38
]. While NRF2's role is cytoprotective, in certain contexts, it can also upregulate oncogenic or tumor growth proteins by regulating specific gene promoters, which can lead to undesired off-target effects. The activation of NRF2 in cancer cells can lead to enhanced proliferation, survival, and resistance to chemotherapy, which is a major challenge in cancer treatment [
39
]. Therefore, the safety of these compounds, especially in chronic administration, must be thoroughly investigated through rigorous pharmacological, in vitro, and in vivo studies to determine their potential toxicity and off-target effects. The high selectivity of the interaction between Keap1 and certain marine natural products, such as napyradiomycins, terpenoids from
Streptomyces
species, may prove to be advantageous in this regard [
40
]. This selective disruption of Keap1 binding allows for precise NRF2 activation, making these molecules attractive pharmacological tools for managing chronic diseases without the risks associated with non-selective activation.
Since some synthetic NRF2 activators may result in undesired off-target effects [
41
], the high selectivity of the interaction between Keap1 and some natural products may prove to be advantageous, especially in chronic diseases. Selective disruption of Keap1 binding with marine NRF2 activators allows for precise NRF2 activation [
42
], making the use of these molecules attractive pharmacological tools. Such is the case for bacterial metabolites such as napyradiomycins from
Streptomyces
species [
43
]. Napyradiomycins are efficient NRF2 activators since they have been shown to suppress inflammation and ROS levels and induce the antioxidant enzyme expression in in vitro models [
25
]. Napyradiomycin A1 analogs also have an extensive pharmacological activity profile. 16Z-19-Hydroxynapyradiomycin A1 exhibits dual therapeutic activity since it effectively suppresses inflammation and activates the NRF2 pathway, diminishing cellular damage caused by oxidative stress in metabolic conditions like diabetes [
25
].
Honaucin A is another marine-derived anti-inflammatory agent and NRF2 activator [
44
]. At low micromolar concentrations, it stabilizes NRF2, allowing it to translocate to the nucleus to induce gene transcription. The NRF2-regulated expression of a limited number of genes by this natural product offers a higher chance of having limited off-target effects [
45
]. The anti-inflammatory effects of honaucin A were reported in PMA-induced ear edema in mice in a dosage-dependent manner [
46
], indicating its strong in vivo potency. Thus, the anti-inflammatory and NRF2 activating properties of honaucin A could also make it a promising therapy for inflammatory diseases in humans; however, no study on the safety profile in chronic administration has been reported [
46
].
The advantage of marine NRF2 activators for potential in vivo applications lies in their halogenated centers as well as the fact that they often have an acetoxyl group and conjugated systems on the main molecular framework [
47
]. These structural characteristics greatly improve the biological efficacy and also lead to their multi-targeting activity. A variety of bromophenols, phenolic compounds extracted from sponges and marine algae, have demonstrated promising antidiabetic potency due to their dual activity of PTP1B and α-glucosidase enzyme inhibition as well as NRF2 activation [
48
]. Thus, marine-derived antidiabetic agents are better options for regulating multiple biological pathways linked to diabetes than single target-oriented drugs. However, practical aspects like sustainable production of metabolites need to be considered for large-scale drug development [
26
,
29
].
In conclusion, several pieces of evidence from preclinical studies indicate the high potential of marine NRF2 activators to effectively manage diabetes and metabolic disorders by addressing both oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction. These compelling data should provide a strong foundation for more detailed in vivo and clinical evaluations to further confirm the efficacy and safety profiles of these marine-sourced bioactive compounds. This is especially critical when considering that the majority of studies only focused on acute administration in vivo. Finally, continuous efforts in natural product bioprospecting should be implemented to expand the current list of candidate marine metabolites with NRF2-modulatory capabilities and accelerate their development as future pharmaceutical candidates for diabetes and metabolic disease treatment.
SIRT1-NRF2 cross-talk
SIRT1 and NRF2 are crucial pathways in metabolic regulation [
49
]. Marine-derived bioactive molecules have the ability to modulate both pathways and have thus been indicated as a novel treatment option for the alleviation of complicated diseases (Fig.
2
). For example, the marine-derived antioxidant fucoxanthin from the brown seaweed inhibits renal oxidative stress damage in ischemia–reperfusion and improves renal function through the dual activation of SIRT1 and NRF2 [
50
]. Fucoxanthin induces the expression of the downstream antioxidant targets HO-1 and superoxide dismutase (SOD1) [
50
]. This highlights a therapeutic connection in terms of promoting the redox state via modulating metabolic function. Simultaneous activation of SIRT1 and NRF2 by marine compounds suggests that they can offer synergistic benefits in the prevention or treatment of diabetes compared to single-pathway activators. Moreover, marine natural products hold the promise to ameliorate organ complications, such as nephropathy, which are difficult to control with terrestrial compounds. Therefore, marine-derived dual activators targeting both oxidative stress and apoptosis in metabolic tissues should be further explored for their anti-diabetes function and therapeutic advantages compared to terrestrial ones. Mechanistically, SIRT1 is capable of NRF2 deacetylation for its activation and antioxidant gene induction [
50
]. The crosstalk between SIRT1 and NRF2 is multifaceted and a key determinant of their synergistic effects. SIRT1, a NAD + -dependent deacetylase, directly deacetylates Keap1, a negative regulator of NRF2, leading to the stabilization and nuclear translocation of NRF2 [
51
]. Once in the nucleus, NRF2 binds to the Antioxidant Response Element (ARE) in the promoter regions of target genes, such as HO-1 and NQO1, to induce their transcription and enhance cellular defense against oxidative stress. Beyond this direct interaction, SIRT1 also deacetylates other key transcription factors like NF-κB and p66Shc, modulating their activity and further contributing to the overall cellular stress response [
52
]. This functional cross-talk, where SIRT1 activates NRF2 through deacetylation, highlights a powerful synergy. This synergy is particularly relevant in complicated diseases such as diabetes, where an interplay between deacetylase and redox-sensitive transcriptional networks exists. Thus, stimulation of NRF2 by SIRT1 deacetylation leads to protective cellular effects to counteract oxidative stress, suggesting that chemical optimization of marine-derived SIRT1/NRF2 dual activators should be pursued for future therapy development for metabolic syndrome and decline of functional age.

Extracts from marine sponge-associated microorganisms such as
Bacillus
and
Salinispora arenicola
are also powerful and multi-targeted activators. The extracts were able to induce 170% SIRT1 activity and more than 150% cellular antioxidant capacity [

]. Such impressive multi-targeted activity suggests that these compounds could reduce or alleviate metabolic dysfunction and oxidative stress in complicated disease states such as diabetes, as well as improve redox state, which is essential for maintaining human health. Marine sponge extracts have displayed anti-senescence and anti-inflammatory capabilities by activating both SIRT1 and NRF2 [
53

Therefore, multi-targeted agents could alleviate diseases such as diabetes and its complications via the simple use of a single component or formulation, as the dose of several medications and their pharmacokinetics may be required when a drug targeting each disease state is used in parallel. However, it remains unclear as to which bioactive compounds are responsible for the biological effects, and whether these compounds are safe in the long term.
Marine bromophenols from algae and sponges are multi-functional compounds that act on both metabolic and redox pathways [
26
]. This can be a beneficial approach compared to terrestrial natural products, which may act only on a single target. Marine-derived bromophenols are strong inhibitors of PTP1B, a regulator of glucose metabolism, α-glucosidase, an enzyme involved in starch digestion and glucose absorption, as well as modulators of redox-sensitive pathways by activating NRF2 [
29
]. Bromophenols can inhibit PTP1B in vitro at an IC
50
ranging from 0.8 µM, as well as α-glucosidase at an IC
50
as low as 0.03 µM, which is considerably low and potent, indicating they are good candidates for glycemic control [
29
]. Moreover, marine bromophenols are potent antioxidants, as they induce cellular protective systems through the upregulation of targets of NRF2, which further supports their therapeutic value. These bromophenols are composed of halogenated cores and ether linkages, which confer their unique capabilities to act on multiple targets via interacting with both metabolism and redox pathways. Their dual activity of inhibiting key metabolic enzymes like PTP1B and α-glucosidase while activating NRF2-mediated antioxidant systems exemplifies the powerful synergistic potential of marine compounds. Thus, future drug design utilizing such marine-derived bromophenols holds significant value, where scientists can mimic the functional group and/or similar scaffolds that induce multi-targeting. However, issues with bioavailability and stability still need to be improved to further study in vivo sustained efficacy.
The marine-derived compound C3 exhibits anti-senescence effects on human endothelial progenitor cells (hEPCs) [
15
]. Treatment with C3 effectively restores SIRT1 protein expression in aging hEPCs under stress by angiotensin II, in a dose-dependent manner, as well as restores cell function by reducing senescence [
15
]. Moreover, stress-induced AMPK activation was reversed and Akt phosphorylation restored with C3 treatment [
15
]. These signaling axes are all part of the functional connection of the metabolic pathway by acting on metabolic enzymes and the stress-protective response regulated by NRF2. For example, the indirect connection to the NRF2 pathway is demonstrated by increased antioxidant capacity of C3-treated hEPCs under oxidative stress [
15
]. C3's ability to restore SIRT1 expression and modulate signaling axes such as AMPK and Akt links its metabolic effects with the stress-protective response regulated by NRF2, showcasing a functional connection between these two pathways. Therefore, C3 stabilizes cells by directly modulating metabolic function via targets such as SIRT1 and Akt signaling, and indirectly via regulating oxidative stress and enhancing cellular defense. In addition, C3 treatment on aging hEPCs led to increased survival of hEPCs under stress and increased vascular function [
15
]. The authors showed that C3 ameliorates premature aging of endothelial progenitor cells in an animal model, suggesting that C3 could be useful in preventing diabetic vascular complications [
15
]. The findings from this study can guide further studies on additional marine-derived small molecules that target more signaling axes.
There are several other examples of multi-targeting activities from marine natural products by modulation of both the SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways. Aquastatin A, a PTP1B inhibitor isolated from a marine fungus, displays a strong inhibitory effect, which acts upstream of the redox-sensitive NRF2/HO-1 pathway for the induction of cellular defense by increasing antioxidant expression [
54
]. Marine actinomycete-derived compounds (e.g., pyrostatins) show multiple pharmacological properties that could reduce the need to administer several drugs at once [
55
]. Examples include: α-amylase inhibitors from marine microbial sources, which can reduce blood sugar level [
26
,
56
]; marine
streptomycetes
that produce pyrostatins that regulate the protein and mRNA levels of N-acetyl-β-D-glucosaminidase [
57
]; and one isolated from Otsuchi Bay shows significant α-amylase inhibition activity. These bioactive molecules also display a synergistic effect when used as multi-targets because they are involved in metabolic pathways through enzyme regulation, as well as the NRF2 redox-sensitive pathway. This multi-targeted approach, seen in compounds like Aquastatin A and pyrostatins, could reduce the need for administering several drugs at once, thereby simplifying treatment regimens for complex conditions like diabetes and its complications. As discussed, the combination of marine-derived NRF2/SIRT1 dual activators may produce promising cytoprotective outcomes with little or no toxicity, as marine metabolites have a naturally low toxicity [
58
]. Therefore, to enhance beneficial outcomes, the combination or structural modification of marine bioactive compounds with multi-targeting activities should be explored to promote cytoprotective action. Microbial activity is a potential source for drug discovery and multi-targeting [
59
], and marine microorganisms are likely to produce a wider range of compounds compared to their terrestrial counterparts, due to the diverse ecology of the ocean. In addition, due to their diverse habitats and ecosystems, marine microorganisms have evolved biosynthesis and adaptation mechanisms that produce bioactive metabolites [
60
]. Hence, marine natural products and especially microorganisms represent important resources of untapped potential for multi-target drug discovery, yet require extensive study to demonstrate their long-term in vivo benefits.
The intricate interaction and function of SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways modulated by marine-derived compounds suggest the opportunity to address more complex diseases and therapeutic advantages. Further research is needed to improve their pharmacological properties and in vivo studies for future clinical trials and clinical utilization.
Risks of chronic NRF2 activation
While NRF2 activation is generally viewed as protective against oxidative stress and metabolic dysfunction, chronic or unregulated activation may carry unintended risks. Prolonged NRF2 upregulation has been associated with the promotion of tumorigenesis, as NRF2 can enhance cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis under certain pathological conditions [
61
]. In cancer cells, constitutive NRF2 activity can create a selective advantage by elevating antioxidant defenses, enabling malignant cells to withstand oxidative damage, evade cell death, and thrive under stress conditions such as hypoxia or chemotherapy [
62
]. This so-called “dark side” of NRF2 highlights the dual role of the pathway as both a cytoprotective factor and, in certain contexts, a driver of oncogenic processes.
Moreover, sustained NRF2 activation may interfere with the balance of cellular redox signaling, which is essential for normal physiological processes. ROS, while often harmful at high levels, also function as critical signaling molecules in immune defense, metabolic regulation, and cellular differentiation [
63
]. Continuous suppression of ROS via NRF2-driven antioxidant pathways may disrupt these signaling networks, leading to impaired immune responses and altered metabolic homeostasis [
64
]. Such effects could compromise host defense against pathogens and blunt physiological stress responses, raising concerns about the long-term systemic consequences of chronic NRF2 stimulation through marine-derived bioactives.
Finally, the long-term safety of marine NRF2 activators requires careful consideration of their structural properties and potential for bioaccumulation. Many marine-derived metabolites, particularly halogenated compounds, may persist in tissues and exert prolonged NRF2 activation beyond the intended therapeutic window. This could increase the likelihood of off-target effects, including altered gene expression linked to detoxification pathways, drug resistance, and metabolic dysregulation. Rigorous toxicological assessments, dose–response analyses, and long-term in vivo studies are therefore critical to determine the safe therapeutic thresholds of these compounds. By addressing these safety concerns, future research can strike a balance between harnessing the protective benefits of NRF2 activation and mitigating its potential oncogenic and systemic risks.

These natural products not only expand the horizon of drug discovery but also pose unique challenges and opportunities in sustainable development and pharmaceutical formulation, ultimately advancing innovative solutions for complex diseases.

Coral and soft coral metabolites
Soft corals have emerged as a great source of bioactive metabolites that have pharmacological potential. Among these, a steroid-type antioxidant 7-dehydroerectasteroid F isolated from
Dendronephthya gigantea
can directly induce NRF2 nuclear translocation and the expression of phase II detoxification enzymes such as HO-1 [
66
]. This indicates their protective effect on oxidative injuries in neuron cell culture. Further studies in the metabolic model are needed for evidence [
66
].
With regards to structural features, it is noteworthy that both compounds feature a characteristic halogenation or acetoxylation that is very rarely observed in the steroids from terrestrial plants [
67
]. The presence of these unique halogenation and acetoxylation patterns is believed to be a key reason why many coral-derived compounds exhibit dual-modulatory effects on both SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways. This unique chemistry allows them to simultaneously regulate redox homeostasis and metabolic pathways, a property often not observed in their terrestrial counterparts [
68
]. It was reported that halogenated or acetoxylated side chains in the coral-derived steroids may be important for the antioxidant ability and prevent the H2O2-induced cell death in the diabetes mellitus model. However, further study should also focus on how different modifications of the steroid ring skeleton and attached side chain regulate the biological activities [
69
]. The investigation of the ecological pressure driving the accumulation of halogenated and acetoxylated molecules may be an important point in order to maximize the pharmacological effects [
66
,
70
].
Moreover, soft coral metabolites showed different upstream signals for NRF2 activation, thus promoting their responses to metabolic regulation like diabetes and aging as compared to plant-derived therapeutics. Therefore, to have more information about the upstream of NRF2 activation, we need further studies [
66
,
71
]. The dual mechanism of steroid-type antioxidant with direct scavenging of ROS and induction of NRF2/HO-1 axis is also considered as a strategy to enhance the chance for a multitargeted therapeutic development. This idea should be examined through the studies of treating the oxidative damage induced by chronic diseases [
66
,
72
,
73
].
Coral reef, as one of the most biodiverse marine ecosystems, is now recognized as a rich source of structurally novel secondary metabolites that may not exist in any other source [
74
]. In general, the structural novelty of coral metabolites is related to the introduction of halogen or acetoxy motifs, which are not so prevalent in terrestrial organisms. Coral-derived metabolites were reported to have superior ability to activate the NRF2-mediated antioxidant defense to combat metabolic dysregulation due to unique structural diversity [
75
,
76
]. All these facts prove that these metabolites evolved for survival in extreme environments. The correlation between ecological factors and chemical adaptations might serve as an important reference in the optimization of drug applications [
70
].
The pharmacological potentials of coral metabolites for the induction of NRF2 signaling and thus activation of cellular antioxidant ability were identified and were superior to those of conventional antioxidant therapeutics [
77
,
78
]. Conventional natural antioxidants, such as resveratrol, α-lipoic acid, etc., that originated from terrestrial organisms usually exhibit only one property in different therapeutic applications, such as anti-inflammation, anti-oxidation, glucose homeostasis maintenance, etc. [
79
,
80
]. Further investigations are required to assess whether coral metabolites also exhibit other regulatory properties, such as anti-inflammation and anti-apoptotic effects [
66
,
70
].
Emerging preclinical studies indicate that coral-derived antioxidants not only regulate redox homeostasis but also modulate glucose and lipid metabolism, providing a novel therapeutic opportunity for treating diabetes and degenerative diseases [
81
]. Further, the superiority of coral-derived compounds over terrestrial compounds remains to be substantiated using mechanism-based studies and experimental models of human diseases [
81
]. The superiority of coral metabolites is often attributed to their unique structural scaffolds that allow them to elevate cellular levels of antioxidant enzymes via NRF2 activation and enhance metabolic pathways through SIRT1 modulation, thereby providing a powerful, multifunctional therapeutic effect [
82
].
Coral metabolites are good leads to develop novel NRF2 activators as therapeutics for multiple disease conditions [
70
]. Analogs of coral-derived compounds are also potentially designed in order to have more effective and/or safer therapeutics [
66
]. Coral-derived bioactives can elevate the cellular levels of antioxidant enzyme expression, providing cytoprotection against ROS and metabolic injuries [
83
]. This also validates the proposed pathway interactions via modulation of SIRT1 and NRF2. These observations pave the way for translational studies in clinical trials to establish their human efficacy [
83
].
It was reported that coral-derived bioactives could attenuate neuronal and vascular injury, not only by decreasing ROS production and accumulation, but also through the regulation of gene transcription. Unlike conventional therapeutics that modulate a single pathway in the body, this feature allows for the development of multifunctional therapeutic candidates to cope with complex human diseases. Detailed pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic studies should be investigated for future development [
66
,
83
].
In addition, coral-derived bioactives could also enhance metabolic pathways to improve cellular conditions by modulation of SIRT1. Combining them to develop synergistic therapies targeting both NRF2 and SIRT1 will achieve more efficacious results in alleviating multifactorial diseases, such as diabetes and age-related complications, in which both oxidative stress and metabolic disturbances are often involved [
66
,
83
].
In addition, metabolites from
Dendronephthya gigantea
displayed a neuroprotective role on cellular oxidative injury. It indicated that compounds originating from coral could effectively prevent the pathological responses caused by oxidative injuries, which led to abnormal homeostasis in metabolism [
66
].
Coral tissues could harbor several microbial symbionts, which produce many novel secondary metabolites that exhibit novel molecular scaffolds and thus exert different biological activities in contrast with their host [
15
]. The incorporation of microbial secondary metabolites into the chemical library of coral tissues could expand the chemical diversity and the chemical space for biological evaluation in order to increase the probability of discovery of novel therapeutics [
15
,
70
].
It has also been reported that microbial symbionts in marine invertebrates could synthesize secondary metabolites with SIRT1 activation. This allows for the modulation of oxidative responses to the injury of metabolic dysfunction caused by diseases, and to have a therapeutic effect [
15
,
70
]. The structural novelty of coral-associated microbial-derived metabolites has stimulated broad interest in their application to human health [
70
]. Some coral-associated microorganisms play a role in the biosynthesis of some metabolites from coral, such as polyketides and brominated compounds. Understanding the biosynthetic pathway of these secondary metabolites and cloning of the corresponding genes would be an essential approach to produce structurally diverse compounds in microorganisms by synthetic or systems biology [
70
]. In the screening for bioactivity by using coral tissues with associated microbes, some metabolites could lead to oxidative and metabolic signaling pathways to regulate oxidative and metabolic abnormalities [
84
], such as ROS production or glucose homeostasis [
85
]. Metabolites from coral tissue and its associated microbes were identified to influence the cell cycle and, thus, could possess anti-tumor activity. Coral tissue extract can effectively inhibit the proliferation of certain cancer cells [
19
].
Coral-associated microbial symbionts are also gaining interest in their cultivation and exploitation for drug discovery and production [
70
]. As it is difficult to obtain and identify coral symbionts, culture-based methods are considered promising tools to produce them for novel drug candidates in an economically sustainable way to reduce the over-exploitation of coral resources [
70
]. Combining secondary metabolites from host and microbial symbionts provides a rich repertoire of therapeutic scaffolds with high chemical and biological diversity that may not be available from any other organism [
15
,
70
].
While the unique halogenated and acetoxylated structures of coral metabolites contribute to their potent bioactivity and are superior to conventional terrestrial antioxidants, there remains a significant knowledge gap regarding their long-term safety and potential for toxicity. The pharmacological potentials for the induction of NRF2 signaling are promising, but further investigations are required to assess whether coral metabolites also exhibit other regulatory properties, such as anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects. Detailed studies are needed to evaluate the long-term effects of these halogenated compounds on mammalian models and to determine their safety profiles, especially in the context of chronic administration for multifactorial diseases.
Overall, coral and soft coral metabolites, along with their microbial symbiont-derived compounds, represent a structurally unique and pharmacologically promising class of SIRT1 and NRF2 modulators. Preclinical studies provide validated evidence of their antioxidant and metabolic benefits in cell-based and limited animal models, particularly highlighting the role of halogenated and acetoxylated steroids in regulating oxidative stress and glucose metabolism. However, most findings remain at the experimental stage, with limited data on pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and long-term safety in mammalian systems. While mechanistic studies suggest multifunctional effects, including potential anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic properties, these require further validation in clinically relevant models. Thus, coral-derived bioactives should currently be regarded as strong therapeutic leads with preliminary validation in vitro and in vivo, but their clinical efficacy and safety remain to be substantiated through systematic pharmacological studies and well-designed human trials.

Marine sponges are a promising source of unique metabolites for modulating SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways in managing metabolic disorders (see Table

) (Fig.

,

Bromophenols and polybrominated diphenyl ethers from marine sponges show higher specificity and potency as SIRT1 and NRF2 activators in drug discovery compared to terrestrial ones [

,

These structural features, particularly the halogenation and phenolic ether linkages, are crucial for their multi-targeting activity, enabling them to modulate both SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways more potently and with higher specificity than many terrestrial compounds. Chemical structural elucidation will be useful for understanding structure–activity relationships.

Moreover, the identification of novel scaffolds enables structure-based drug design, potentially generating new analogues with improved stability, potency, and specificity in the modulation of metabolic disorders characterized by oxidative stress and glucose dysregulation. As a remarkable source of unprecedented and diverse chemistries, marine sponges are irreplaceable resources for therapeutic targets associated with metabolic diseases [
104

Scopalina hapalia

Ethyl acetate extract of isolates from marine sponge
Scopalina hapalia

In addition to SIRT1 and NRF2 activation activities, marine sponge metabolite crude extracts also display certain cytoprotective and preventative effects in cellular models. At 200 μg/mL, SH-09-EA-LM exhibits elastase inhibitory activities of 32.5%, and SH-75-EA-LM, at 20 μg/mL, exhibits tyrosinase inhibitory activities of 58.33% [

The extract from this microorganism not only enhances cell survival via NRF2/ARE expression but also possesses enzyme inhibitory activities that further protect and enhance the integrity of cells and tissues in metabolically active models [

A number of marine sponge-derived metabolites have demonstrated PTP1B inhibition for the therapeutic potential to increase insulin sensitivity. In this manner, certain halogenated metabolites from sponges inhibit PTP1B (IC

Additionally, multiple therapeutic targets simultaneously modulated are more likely to have long-term, protective effects with lower risks of drug resistance in chronic diabetes [

Beyond the activation and inhibitory activities, many marine sponge metabolites also show therapeutic activities against endothelial cellular senescence. C3 is a novel activator of SIRT1 that regulates critical signaling pathways in cellular models of endothelial progenitor cells upon cellular stress [

Even though marine sponges represent an extremely exciting source of compounds for drug therapy discovery, there are significant challenges to using these natural products for this purpose. Such challenges include the risk to sustainability, limited natural abundance, compound instability, and scalability. Many of the marine sponge metabolites that display potent activities toward promising therapeutic targets are only present in trace amounts in nature. Thus, if marine sponges from the wild are harvested directly, they are not only being depleted, thus affecting the biodiversity in the marine environment, but there are also limitations in the amount of these bioactive metabolites that can be isolated from natural sources. In recent years, there has been progress with some of the sponge-derived metabolites as sources of new drug leads because of the utilization of microbial fermentation and synthetic biology techniques [
70
].
As such, fermentation of marine sponge-associated microorganisms has been developed to produce bioactive sponge-derived metabolites [
19
]. Furthermore, the biosynthetic pathways and metabolic reactions for these microbial metabolites have been explored, providing the opportunity to modify and synthesize these molecules through metabolic engineering [
70
].
A limiting factor toward developing marine sponge metabolites for drug therapy comes from the instability of these metabolites in storage. Because these metabolites are often extremely potent and active, they must retain their structural integrity and activity during storage for development in drug therapies [
19
,
70
]. Additionally, if the marine sponge-derived metabolites are to be effective toward potential disease targets, they have to be supplied in adequate amounts. In response, marine sponges and marine sponge-associated microorganisms have been tested to determine their potential yields of metabolites using fermentation [
19
].
In order for marine sponges to be developed in drug discovery, there must be techniques to enhance their stability during storage and their supply in an ecological, sustainable, and economical manner. This is accomplished through innovative techniques in formulation and synthetic biology. The supply and stability problems are not just restricted to marine sponge drug development but also in terrestrial natural products for drug therapies. Innovative approaches include the employment of excipients to enhance the compound stability during storage or the use of encapsulation techniques [
19
,
70
].
Innovative molecular technologies in formulation have to be utilized in order to ensure that the biologically active metabolites that are synthesized and/or isolated and purified in the lab are not degraded in time [
19
,
70
]. As stated before, because these marine sponge-derived metabolites are so potent, it is critical that during storage they do not degrade and, therefore, retain their drug activities. This also involves an examination of what types of storage and in which types of solvent/drug formulations would enhance the stability of these compounds over time. By examining innovative methods to enhance compound stability and supply, marine sponges, as sources of these potentially therapeutically active metabolites, will be readily available [
19
,
70
].
Additionally, in order to overcome supply chain limitations, marine sponge-derived metabolites must also be produced in a manner that maintains biodiversity and protects the environment in an ecologically sustainable approach. This will ensure that as this particular sector of drug therapy increases, there is no damage to the marine ecosystem [
70
].
Marine sponge-associated microorganisms display other potential cytoprotective and beneficial actions in cellular models. At 200 μg/mL, SH-09-EA-LM displays elastase inhibitory activity, and at 20 μg/mL, SH-75-EA-LM inhibits tyrosinase [
19
]. The elastase enzyme mediates several physiological functions through degradation and cleavage of extracellular matrix proteins and is also involved in the inflammatory process through leukocyte recruitment. This contributes to cell growth, survival, and proliferation. Tyrosinase plays important roles in melanogenesis and melanocytes. Inhibiting elastase and tyrosinase also benefits metabolically active systems due to their roles in inflammation and in cell survival. Both SH-09-EA-LM and SH-75-EA-LM also display antioxidant activation activity through NRF2 gene and protein expression and upregulation [
19
].
Many of the known sponge metabolites function through the activation of antioxidant gene and protein expression. Specifically, these compounds exert protective and inhibitory effects through NRF2 gene and protein expression upregulation. The mechanisms in marine sponge natural product extracts and isolated natural products that prevent oxidative stress and cellular senescence need to be further examined to improve the design of compounds for the treatment of chronic and acute disease [
105
].
Despite the impressive multi-targeting activity of marine sponge-derived metabolites, particularly halogenated bromophenols and polybrominated diphenyl ethers, a critical knowledge gap exists concerning their long-term safety and bioavailability. While preclinical evidence shows they can effectively inhibit metabolic enzymes and activate SIRT1/NRF2 pathways, there is a pressing need for more in-depth mechanistic studies to examine their bioavailability and toxicity. The presence of halogenated centers can sometimes lead to unexpected off-target effects or long-term accumulation, which must be thoroughly investigated to allow for potential therapeutic applications.
In summary, marine sponge metabolites display anti-diabetic and metabolic disorder therapeutic activities by dually targeting activation and inhibition. These metabolites activate SIRT1 and NRF2 to enhance metabolic homeostasis and prevent/treat diabetic complications such as vascular cellular senescence. These metabolites achieve these effects by activating SIRT1 to enhance metabolic homeostasis and NRF2 to combat oxidative stress, which together help prevent and treat diabetic complications such as vascular cellular senescence. The ability of marine sponge metabolites to dually target these pathways offers a significant advantage over many terrestrial products that often act on a single target. In addition to these activities, marine sponge metabolite extracts inhibit several key metabolic enzymes that contribute to glucose uptake (PTP1B and α-glucosidase) and prevent cellular damage in metabolism (elastase and tyrosinase). The multitude of activities mediated in both cellular and tissue-specific models by both marine sponge metabolite extracts and isolated metabolites allows for the design of potentially highly effective compounds to treat various symptoms associated with metabolic diseases.

Glucose homeostasis regulation
Marine-derived substances exert their blood glucose homeostasis in different ways. Fucoidan and alginate, both being polysaccharides isolated from brown algae, are highly effective in enhancing insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake in muscle and reducing gluconeogenesis in the liver. Diabetic mice, after being treated with fucoidan, were found to have increased insulin sensitivity and lower fasting blood glucose levels [
105
,
113
,
114
,
115
]. Additionally, alginate supplementation can improve postprandial blood glucose levels in healthy people. In contrast to terrestrial polysaccharides, marine-derived polysaccharides are more effective in modulating glucose metabolism due to their unique sulfation pattern and macromolecular structures [
105
,
116
]. However, further studies are warranted to investigate the role of these marine-derived polysaccharides in glucose metabolism in humans.
Phlorotannins, a family of polyphenols mainly found in brown algae, are able to inhibit α-glucosidase and α-amylase simultaneously and thus decrease postprandial blood glucose. They are also capable of activating the SIRT1/NRF2 pathways simultaneously [
105
], which terrestrial plant polyphenols rarely achieve. These are potentially valuable interventions for preventing hyperglycemia and regulating glucose metabolism. However, their efficacy relies heavily on bioavailability, scale production, and pharmacokinetics. More research should be undertaken to evaluate their potential in dietary supplementation and treatment options in humans [
117
].
Marine sponges and coral have produced many novel marine metabolites. They have a specific structure, such as halogenated and steroidal features, which enables them to inhibit certain metabolic enzymes [
118
]. For instance, a selective protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) inhibitor, isolated from a sponge and coral, improves insulin sensitivity and glucose homeostasis in mice through targeting SIRT1 [
118
]. However, further studies are warranted to confirm their safety and potential in treating diseases.
Peptides, being the important functional components in red algae and fish protein hydrolysates, are another type of marine substance that can improve blood glucose levels, decrease the occurrence of diabetes, and can be used as functional food ingredients. Bioactive peptides improve insulin sensitivity and glucose uptake in muscle tissues [
105
,
119
]. They affect blood glucose levels through glucose transporter activity and insulin signaling pathways, significantly reducing postprandial blood glucose and improving glucose metabolism. The efficacy of peptides largely depends on the production methods and extraction scale; thus, further investigation should be carried out to examine their effects on glucose metabolism [
120
,
121
].
Moreover, a small-molecule substance des-O-methyllasiodiplodin (DML) was isolated from a deep-sea-derived fungus. Orally administered DML could significantly lower blood glucose levels and glycosylated hemoglobin, as well as reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines and macrophage activation [
122
]. This suggests that DML might regulate glucose homeostasis by preventing the inflammation-related insulin resistance pathway. However, its regulatory role and signal transduction should be investigated further.
Although many marine-derived substances, including halogenated compounds from sponges and corals, show promise in regulating glucose homeostasis, their potential application is hindered by a lack of long-term safety data. The unique structural features of these compounds, while conferring potent efficacy, may also contribute to instability or non-bioavailability, which presents a significant obstacle for sustained use. Therefore, more efforts are needed to identify and refine their biological role in glucose metabolism and, critically, to improve their stability, solubility, bioavailability, and sustainability before they can be widely used as therapeutic agents.
Marine-derived substances, as stated above, have specific regulatory roles in different pathways in the regulation of blood glucose homeostasis, from blood glucose transport, insulin signaling, to anti-inflammation [
116
,
123
]. These marine substances show multiple effects, and their potential application is quite intriguing. However, their potential still has some obstacles. In general, most of these substances are unstable or non-bioavailable. Thus, more efforts are needed to identify and refine their biological role in glucose metabolism and improve their stability, solubility, bioavailability, and sustainability, which would greatly improve the use of these promising marine substances [
105
].
Oxidative stress protection
Marine-derived compounds have great potential for ameliorating oxidative stress by NRF2-mediated activation and subsequent increase of the endogenous antioxidant levels [
124
,
125
]. Importantly, many of these compounds also interact with SIRT1, which exerts antioxidant effects by deacetylating transcription factors such as FOXO and PGC-1α, thereby enhancing mitochondrial antioxidant defenses and cellular resilience. The dual activation of NRF2 and SIRT1 creates a synergistic mechanism to combat oxidative damage. Astaxanthin, phlorotannins, and fucoidan appear to be more effective in preventing oxidative stress-induced cell damage [
126
,
127
]. Astaxanthin, in fact, mediates the modulation of both NRF2 and NF-κB to break the cycle of inflammation and oxidative damage in diabetes and aging animal models. Additionally, astaxanthin has been reported to enhance SIRT1 activity, leading to improved mitochondrial biogenesis and reduced ROS generation [
128
], which complements its NRF2-dependent antioxidant effects. This is critical for the alleviation of chronic oxidative stress, which is one of the major causative factors for the metabolic dysfunction in diabetes [
129
]. More studies should investigate the long-term consequences of this double modulation in relation to the equilibrium between the inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways.
The chemical structure of astaxanthin, a marine xanthophyll, is well suited to trigger redox-sensitive transcription factors such as NRF2 and NF-κB, allowing it to regulate antioxidant gene transcription, reduce pro-inflammatory mediators, and alleviate cellular stress linked with aging and diabetes [
129
]. By also enhancing SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of FOXO transcription factors, astaxanthin promotes the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as MnSOD and catalase [
128
], providing a broader defense against oxidative insults. The antioxidant and anti-inflammatory responses appear to occur simultaneously, a phenomenon not usually observed with terrestrial antioxidants [
129
]. Though these in vitro results suggest the potential to be exploited, future investigations should focus on its bioavailability, as well as potential metabolic crosstalk.
Due to the highly polymerized and sulfated structure of marine phlorotannins, their radical scavenging ability and the modulation of signaling cascades such as NRF2 and MAPKs are superior compared to those derived from the terrestrial counterparts [
130
,
131
]. Recent evidence also suggests that phlorotannins can activate SIRT1, thereby coupling direct ROS scavenging with long-term improvements in cellular stress resistance [
132
]. The coordinated induction of NRF2-driven antioxidant enzymes and SIRT1-regulated mitochondrial defenses makes phlorotannins particularly promising in protecting pancreatic β-cells from dysfunction. This enhances the bioactivity to counter β-cell dysfunction in diabetes and the incidence of other diabetic-associated outcomes [
130
,
131
]. Therefore, investigations on phlorotannins will need to be scaled up to evaluate the potential translation of laboratory and pre-clinical findings into clinical therapeutics. Bioavailability and clinical scalability should also be examined to address potential challenges when translating from in vitro to in vivo systems.

The combination of these bioactivities could provide protection against the harmful oxidative stress effects resulting from chronic inflammation in diabetic pathology [

,

Aging and neurodegenerative disorders
Marine sponges and corals represent a valuable reservoir of bioactive compounds with promising effects beyond glucose regulation and oxidative stress protection. In particular, several metabolites isolated from these organisms exhibit neuroprotective and anti-aging potential through the activation of SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways, which are central to maintaining cellular homeostasis during aging and metabolic decline.
For instance, diterpenoids such as gracilin A, isolated from marine sponges, have been shown to promote NRF2 activation and inhibit tau hyperphosphorylation, a hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease pathology [
148
]. By mitigating tau aggregation and oxidative damage, gracilin derivatives offer a potential strategy to attenuate neurodegeneration associated with both aging and diabetes. Similarly, bryostatin-1, a macrolide lactone from Bugula neritina, enhances NRF2 signaling and reduces advanced glycation end-product (AGE)-mediated stress, thereby alleviating vascular and neuronal complications linked to metabolic dysfunction and cognitive decline [
82
,
148
].
Coral-derived steroids also demonstrate neuroprotective actions by inducing HO-1 expression via NRF2 activation, reducing oxidative vulnerability, and preserving neuronal function under conditions of hydrogen peroxide-induced stress [
66
]. These findings suggest that coral metabolites could protect against neuronal apoptosis and memory loss, making them attractive scaffolds for preventing aging-related disorders.
Moreover, certain sponge-derived metabolites have been reported to restore SIRT1 activity and modulate AMPK/Akt pathways, thereby enhancing mitochondrial function and reducing premature vascular senescence in aging models [
15
]. This dual regulation of metabolic and stress-response pathways may be particularly valuable in combating neurovascular decline, which is a shared mechanism between diabetes, cardiovascular aging, and neurodegenerative diseases.
Development challenges
Approaching the practical issues in the development of marine-derived bioactive compounds, two major barriers need to be overcome: one in the pharmaceutical formulation field and one concerning the sustainable production of these marine molecules. It is important to tackle the practical aspects of developing these compounds for metabolic and age-related diseases because they present major barriers to marine pharmacology as a whole.
Pharmaceutical formulation
The pharmaceutical formulation of marine-derived bioactive compounds poses several challenges to the efficacy and safety of these novel therapeutic agents (Fig.
6
) [
152
]. Marine-derived bioactive compounds frequently exhibit poor aqueous solubility, which limits their dissolution and absorption in the gastrointestinal tract [
153
,
154
]. In addition, many of these compounds are chemically unstable under gastric conditions, leading to rapid degradation and reduced systemic bioavailability. The intricate chemical structures and inherent instability of marine molecules, such as the unique sulfation patterns in marine polysaccharides and the large molecular weights of marine polyphenols, can compromise their biological activities [
155
]. In addition, many marine polysaccharides can degrade under normal processing and storage conditions, resulting in a loss of activity due to the damage to critical functional groups. Therefore, formulation methods, such as lyophilization to remove water at low temperatures or encapsulation, can be applied to protect marine bioactives from thermal, oxidative, and hydrolytic degradation. These approaches help to preserve and enhance the therapeutic activity of marine compounds and ensure their effective use [
155
].

One of the major barriers to the clinical translation of marine-derived bioactives has been poor oral bioavailability and rapid metabolism, which may result from poor absorption from the digestive tract or extensive enzymatic degradation. In general, natural products like phlorotannins and fucoidan, as well as marine-derived peptides, have a tendency to undergo metabolic breakdown or are easily eliminated [
105
]. Nanoformulations have been found to enhance the bioavailability and prevent the enzymatic breakdown of natural compounds. Liposomes, lipid-based nanoparticles, and polymeric carriers can provide protection and sustained release in preclinical models of diabetes and oxidative stress [
105
].
Another significant challenge in bridging the gap between preclinical discovery and clinical application is the regulatory distinction between a "nutraceutical" or "functional food" and a "pharmaceutical". While many marine-derived compounds, such as fucoidan and phlorotannins, are currently marketed as nutraceuticals, this status requires a lower level of evidence for safety and efficacy than a pharmaceutical drug. To be approved as a drug, these compounds must undergo rigorous and extensive clinical trials to prove their safety, long-term efficacy, and specific therapeutic mechanisms in human subjects. The high costs, complex logistical challenges, and strict regulatory standards of these trials are major limiting factors that contribute to the scarcity of clinical data for marine bioactives in the treatment of metabolic disorders [
1
].
The pharmaceutical formulation of marine natural compounds must also account for the extensive chemical diversity found in marine organisms, as this represents one of the most remarkable aspects of marine natural products. Marine life generates molecules with a unique degree of variation in chemical structures and functional groups, resulting in an array of biological activities [
118
,
156
]. Some structural features, such as halogenation, are commonly found in marine compounds like the bromophenols and halogenated steroids, which can be extracted from sponges and corals. These structural characteristics present multiple difficulties in their purification. For example, the presence of similar analogs in marine extracts can result in a lack of purity. Additionally, biological activity can differ from batch to batch. Enzyme-assisted extraction (EAE) has recently been reported as an efficient process to isolate and purify high-purity marine bioactive compounds. This technology may also reduce batch variability, improve the bioactivity of products, and minimize energy consumption. Furthermore, biotransformation via marine-derived enzymes can generate stable derivatives with suitable bioactivity for large-scale manufacturing [
118
,
156
].
Several unique mechanisms involved in the biotransformation, distribution, and excretion pathways of marine-derived compounds differ from those found in terrestrial compounds. In general, these pathways influence the level of bioavailability and efficacy of marine bioactives, as well as their overall pharmacological action [
118
,
156
]. Recent studies have demonstrated that several marine-derived small molecules possess targeted tissue specificity. For example, some marine-derived cancer drugs are known to have increased drug exposure to tumor cells while simultaneously decreasing drug exposure to healthy cells [
157
].
Pharmacological effectiveness and specificity are highly dependent on chemical structure, which is why there is substantial interest in modifying the structure of natural products to improve biological activity. For example, enhanced bromination has been found to improve the efficacy of marine-derived bromophenols. Elongated alkyl chains in protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B inhibitors have also shown improved efficacy [
158
]. However, changes in chemical structure can result in undesirable effects like cytotoxicity or diminished activity, and therefore must be carefully balanced [
118
,
156
,
158
].
Advanced biotechnological approaches have shown promising solutions for many challenges surrounding the development of novel marine pharmaceuticals. These techniques provide a variety of methods for increasing the yield, purity, and stability of marine-derived lead compounds. For instance, enzyme-assisted extraction, microbial fermentation, and synthetic biology may address ecological constraints, such as species rarity and extraction difficulties, to develop viable drug candidates with retained functional integrity and bioactivity [
118
,
156
,
158
]. Enzyme-assisted extraction has been found to improve the bioactivity by maintaining the critical structural motifs responsible for the activation of the SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways in sulfated polysaccharides and marine polyphenols. As such, the combined utilization of EAE with modern formulation technologies, such as nanoencapsulation, may overcome many of the previous roadblocks to the development of effective metabolic therapeutics [
118
,
156
,
158
].
While the therapeutic promise of marine bioactive compounds such as fucoxanthin, bromophenols, napyradiomycins, and marine peptides is well documented in preclinical studies, clinical trials remain severely limited—especially for pure, isolated compounds (Table
2
). In particular, fucoxanthin has been the subject of a few small-scale trials, largely in the form of brown seaweed extracts rather than as purified compounds. For example, Asai et al. (2008) confirmed the presence of fucoxanthinol in plasma at concentrations of approximately 0.8 nM following daily wakame consumption containing 6.1 mg of fucoxanthin [
159

160
]. Hashimoto et al. (2012) and Mikami & Hosokawa (2013) provided pharmacokinetic data from kombu extract dosing, demonstrating reliable detection of fucoxanthinol in human serum [
161
,
162
], while Hitoe & Shimoda (2017) showed modest reductions in body weight, BMI, and visceral fat in a four-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled fucoxanthin trial (1–3 mg/day) in Japanese adults with BMI over 25 kg/m
2
[
163
]. Although these findings suggest promising metabolic effects, no trials have yet tested fucoxanthin alone or as a cancer therapy, and data on diabetic or aging-related endpoints remain scarce.
In contrast, for other compound classes like bromophenols, napyradiomycins, and marine peptides, clinical evidence is virtually nonexistent. A recent mini-review on marine brown algae confirmed only a handful of clinical trials involving whole-algae supplementation, with no studies identified that assessed isolated marine bromophenols for anti-diabetic or antioxidant outcomes [
164
]. Similarly, reviews summarizing marine compound research in metabolic disorders underscore a rich preclinical literature but note that translational and clinical validation is largely absent [
26
,
165
]. Searches of ClinicalTrials.gov produce no registered trials specifically investigating pure bromophenols, napyradiomycins, or marine peptides for metabolic indications such as diabetes, oxidative stress, or age-related metabolic dysfunction. This stark gap underscores the urgent need for well-designed human studies to validate the safety, pharmacokinetics, and efficacy of these marine-derived metabolites in metabolic health settings. In addition to technical barriers, regulatory and ethical concerns regarding the large-scale harvesting of marine resources also present challenges. Overexploitation of ecologically sensitive species raises sustainability issues and often triggers stricter regulatory oversight, emphasizing the need for responsible sourcing and sustainable bioproduction strategies in drug development.
Improving pharmaceutical formulation and mitigating toxicity in marine nutraceuticals requires a multipronged approach that integrates technological innovation with careful safety evaluation. Novel drug delivery systems such as nanoencapsulation, liposomes, and polymeric carriers not only enhance stability and bioavailability but also allow for controlled release and targeted delivery, thereby reducing systemic toxicity [
166
]. Structural optimization and biotransformation can generate more stable derivatives with improved pharmacological activity while minimizing off-target effects. In parallel, advanced in vitro toxicity screening models and computational tools, including molecular docking and ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) profiling, can be used to predict and refine the safety profiles of marine compounds before costly clinical trials [
167
]. Combining these formulation strategies with sustainable bioproduction techniques, such as enzyme-assisted extraction and microbial fermentation, offers a pathway to scalable and reproducible products that maintain efficacy while ensuring patient safety. Ultimately, aligning pharmaceutical technology with rigorous toxicological assessment will be essential for translating marine bioactives from preclinical promise to safe and effective therapeutic options.
Marine-derived bioactives have received substantial attention as potential therapies for diabetes and other metabolic disorders due to their chemical diversity and pharmacological activity. However, there are many challenges that must be overcome to deliver these compounds effectively. Developing stable and scalable formulations is critical to allow marine bioactive compounds to deliver on their promise in the clinical management of diabetes. Taken together, these advances illustrate that while marine-derived bioactives hold substantial promise as therapies for diabetes and other metabolic disorders, significant challenges remain in ensuring their stability, safety, and scalability. Addressing pharmaceutical formulation-related barriers and systematically evaluating toxicity will be critical steps in enabling marine compounds to fulfill their potential in the clinical management of metabolic diseases.
Sustainable production
The sustainable production of marine bioactive compounds is an important challenge in an attempt to reconcile therapeutic progress and environmental stewardship (Fig.
7
). Large-scale offshore aquaculture and seaweed farming are the most economical solutions for ensuring a steady production of marine bioactive compounds. Kelp yields approximately 30–60 tons of fresh biomass per 3 acre (~ 1.2 ha) per year—equivalent to roughly 25–50 dry tons per hectare annually—on par with or exceeding terrestrial yields such as potatoes and much higher than staple grains [
168
]. By contrast, average conventional wheat yields globally are around 3–4 metric tons per hectare (with typical ranges of 1.9–8 t/ha), and soybean yields average about 2.4–3.2 t/ha. Thus, kelp production can deliver 8–15 times more biomass per hectare than these land crops, underscoring its efficiency for biomass-based applications like the extraction of bioactive compounds [
169
]. Production cost ranges from $200 to $300 per dry ton of algal biomass, which is highly cost-competitive with land-based agriculture resources [
116
]. Under optimized growth conditions, the price may even decrease to less than $100 per ton. In addition, these methods can ensure a more consistent supply of marine raw materials for the consistent supply of novel drug candidates [
116
]. In order to ensure that these methods remain environmentally advantageous, the ecosystem services provided by these approaches need to be monitored and studied more extensively.

Kelp forest, growing for several centimeters per day, offers the best option to ensure the constant supply of marine bioactive compounds all year round. The annual productivity of the kelp forest is generally around 8 to 10 kg of dry biomass per square meter [
116
]. The highly rapid growth of kelp indicates that the production of biomass can be replenished at a constant rate. Moreover, kelp functions as a potent blue‑carbon sink. Macroalgae globally sequester on the order of 200 million metric tons of CO₂ per year, with kelp forests alone in regions such as Australia estimated to absorb 1.3–2.8 teragrams (i.e., million metric tons) annually, accounting for 27–34% of all “blue carbon” sequestered by coastal ecosystems [
170
]. While this does not directly translate to per‑ton values, it implies that each ton of kelp biomass effectively captures close to one ton of CO₂, given typical carbon content and in situ uptake and export pathways. As a result, kelp mariculture offers a unique synergy: high‐yield biomass for pharmaceutical bioactives without the need for arable land, coupled with meaningful carbon removal, positioning it as a sustainable, climate‑positive production platform. Because the growth of kelp, in contrast to several other marine resources, is seasonally dependent, it becomes one of the leading contributors to the fixation of atmospheric CO
2
. Nevertheless, the scalability of this system requires increased investment in cost-efficient cultivation techniques. Modeling studies identified that a total of 18 production parameters could be optimized to improve growth, yield, and CO

sequestration rates for sustainable seaweed cultivation [

].
Unregulated harvesting of marine organisms such as sponges, corals, and rare algae poses significant risks to biodiversity and ecosystem integrity. Unlike macroalgae, many of these benthic invertebrates grow slowly and have limited natural abundance, meaning that large-scale extraction for nutraceutical purposes can result in long-term depletion of populations and irreversible damage to reef ecosystems. Overharvesting may also disrupt ecological functions, as corals and sponges provide essential habitat, nutrient cycling, and filtration services in marine environments. For example, sponge communities play a critical role in benthic–pelagic coupling, while corals are foundation species that sustain reef biodiversity [
171
]. If these organisms are exploited unsustainably, the ecological costs may outweigh the therapeutic benefits of the derived compounds, highlighting the importance of prioritizing non-destructive sampling, aquaculture, and synthetic biology as alternatives.
Moreover, the carbon footprint and ecological trade-offs of marine bioprospecting must also be critically assessed. While seaweed mariculture can act as a blue-carbon sink, the extraction of delicate or rare marine invertebrates may contribute to habitat degradation and localized carbon release. Coral harvesting, for instance, can accelerate reef erosion, reduce shoreline protection, and compromise the ocean’s natural carbon sequestration capacity [
172
]. Balancing the need for bioactive discovery with environmental protection, therefore, requires strict adherence to conservation guidelines, marine spatial planning, and the integration of ecosystem service valuation into policy frameworks. By embedding environmental impact assessments into nutraceutical development pipelines, researchers and industry stakeholders can ensure that the expansion of marine bioactive production aligns with global biodiversity and climate goals.
In response to the sustainability challenges associated with marine bioactive production, biotechnological techniques like enzyme-assisted extraction and microbial fermentation are employed. Enzyme-assisted extraction is carried out by employing certain hydrolytic enzymes that will break down algal cell walls, releasing target metabolites like phlorotannins and sulfated polysaccharides [
173
]. This strategy can potentially provide high product yields without having to rely on traditional solvent extraction methods [
173
]. Furthermore, some microbial fermentation systems offer a reliable platform to grow marine bioactive compounds in a controlled environment, leading to the synthesis of products like mycosporine-like amino acids and polyketides [
145
]. Although these techniques may not be readily scalable for the industrial production of marine bioactives due to technical difficulties and costs, they are certainly efficient methods for improving production consistency and reliability. Beyond conventional methods, biotechnological platforms such as marine microbial fermentation and synthetic biology provide innovative alternatives for sustainable compound production [
174
,
175
]. These approaches enable the heterologous expression of complex marine metabolites in engineered microbial hosts, reducing reliance on large-scale biomass harvesting while ensuring a controlled, reproducible supply of bioactives.
Additionally, the use of certain innovative biotechnological tools can further contribute to achieving sustainability for the production of marine bioactives. For example, automated systems have been implemented to improve biomass cultivation by reducing labor, optimizing yields, and monitoring environmental impacts [
116
]. Automated biomass production can ensure a constant, scalable supply with fewer workers needed to manage large culture tanks in order to achieve higher yield and a smaller ecological footprint. Although the cost of automated equipment can be high for small laboratories or farms, the implementation of advanced automated tools is essential for streamlining marine-based processes in a sustainable and reproducible way.
To ensure that the discovery of marine bioactives does not lead to the exploitation of marine ecosystems, stringent ethical and legal management regulations are required. The international framework for this is primarily governed by the Nagoya Protocol, which provides a transparent legal basis for the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the utilization of genetic resources. The protocol is a supplementary agreement to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) and sets rules for accessing genetic resources and traditional knowledge, requiring prior informed consent from the country of origin and mutually agreed terms for benefit-sharing [
176

In many instances, ethically and legally controlled marine bioprospecting has no significant ecological effects on marine ecosystems. Additionally, involving and collaborating with indigenous cultures and local communities can add to the benefits gained from marine bioactive production by incorporating traditional knowledge into management strategies. These collaborations are essential for improving resource management and fostering a more equitable and sustainable approach to marine drug discovery.

Limitations
Although the majority of studies reviewed suggest promising dual activation of SIRT1 and NRF2 by marine-derived metabolites, it is important to acknowledge that the evidence is not always consistent. For instance, while halogenated sponge-derived metabolites demonstrate potent bioactivity, concerns remain regarding their bioavailability, long-term toxicity, and potential off-target effects. Similarly, coral-derived halogenated compounds, despite strong antioxidant potential, present uncertainties regarding chronic safety and possible pro-oxidant effects under prolonged exposure. Furthermore, compounds such as astaxanthin, which modulate both NRF2 and NF-κB, highlight the risk of paradoxical outcomes if pathway crosstalk is not tightly regulated. These discrepancies underscore the need for cautious interpretation of current findings and for future studies that directly compare outcomes across different experimental systems, with particular emphasis on resolving mechanistic inconsistencies and validating results in clinically relevant models.
While the therapeutic potential of marine-derived bioactive compounds as SIRT1 and NRF2 activators is broadly acknowledged, their translation into practical applications remains constrained by a lack of specific information on dosage ranges, formulations, and source species. For example, although bromophenols and fucoxanthin consistently demonstrate potent in vitro activity at micromolar concentrations, their bioavailability in vivo is often low, and optimal dosing regimens are not yet established. Likewise, sponge- and coral-derived compounds may vary significantly in potency depending on species origin and extraction methods, making it difficult to generalize findings across studies. Moreover, only a limited number of studies have explored advanced delivery systems (e.g., nanoparticle encapsulation, liposomal formulations) that could improve pharmacokinetic stability and therapeutic efficacy. These knowledge gaps underscore the need for systematic dose–response analyses, species-specific comparisons, and formulation optimization in future research to bridge the gap between preclinical promise and clinical applicability.

In addition to these scientific and technical challenges, broader safety and ethical concerns must also be considered. Chronic NRF2 activation has been associated with oncogenic risks, as tumor cells may exploit sustained antioxidant signaling to evade apoptosis, enhance proliferation, and resist chemotherapy. The structural complexity of many halogenated marine metabolites also raises the possibility of bioaccumulation and long-term off-target effects, yet few comprehensive toxicological evaluations have been performed. Equally important are the ethical issues surrounding bioprospecting and marine biodiversity conservation. Unsustainable harvesting of slow-growing organisms such as corals and sponges can lead to irreversible ecological damage, while questions of benefit-sharing, intellectual property, and respect for indigenous knowledge remain pressing under frameworks like the Nagoya Protocol. Ensuring rigorous pharmacological assessments, sustainable sourcing through aquaculture or microbial biosynthesis, and adherence to ethical standards is therefore essential to responsibly advance marine bioactives from discovery to application.
A further limitation lies in the significant gap between promising laboratory findings and their successful translation into human therapies. Most current evidence is derived from in vitro models and animal studies, which, while valuable, do not fully replicate the complexity of human physiology and disease progression. Differences in metabolism, immune responses, and genetic variability can lead to divergent outcomes when marine-derived bioactives are tested in clinical settings. To overcome these challenges, a multipronged strategy is needed: conducting well-designed clinical trials to establish safety and efficacy in humans, employing advanced pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic modeling to predict therapeutic windows, and integrating nanotechnology-based delivery systems to enhance bioavailability. Additionally, the use of organ-on-chip technologies and human-derived cell models may provide more predictive insights into clinical outcomes. Bridging these translational gaps will be essential for moving marine bioactives from bench to bedside in a safe and effective manner.
Key summary
Marine-derived bioactive compounds represent a promising frontier in the dual modulation of SIRT1 and NRF2 pathways, offering therapeutic potential for oxidative stress-related diseases and metabolic dysfunctions. However, the current body of evidence is predominantly based on in vitro and animal models, with limited clinical validation. Challenges remain in terms of bioavailability, dosage optimization, species-specific variability, and the long-term safety of these compounds. Moreover, potential risks associated with chronic NRF2 activation, off-target effects, and ecological sustainability of marine harvesting warrant careful consideration.
To fully realize the translational potential of these compounds, future research should prioritize well-designed clinical trials that evaluate both efficacy and safety in human populations. These efforts must be supported by systematic dose–response studies, advanced pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic modeling, and the exploration of innovative delivery systems such as nanoparticles and liposomal formulations to improve bioavailability. Additionally, integrating emerging tools such as organ-on-chip technologies and human-derived cell models could provide more predictive insights into therapeutic outcomes. On the ecological and ethical front, sustainable sourcing through aquaculture, microbial biosynthesis, and synthetic biology must be further developed to reduce reliance on wild harvesting, while adhering to international frameworks like the Nagoya Protocol to ensure equitable benefit-sharing.
By combining rigorous scientific investigation with ethical and sustainable practices, the therapeutic promise of marine-derived SIRT1 and NRF2 activators can be responsibly advanced from bench to bedside. Such an integrated approach will not only strengthen the scientific foundation of this field but also enhance its long-term impact on human health and environmental stewardship.
