## Core Findings

The JAS 2017 guidelines address homocysteine as a potential risk factor for ASCVD (Section 12-ix, "Other Risk Factors and Markers to Be Considered") but notably do NOT elevate it to the status of a primary risk factor requiring routine screening or intervention. The guidelines acknowledge epidemiological associations between elevated homocysteine and CAD/stroke/PAD but emphasize that interventional trials with vitamin supplementation failed to reduce cardiovascular events.

## Key Evidence on Homocysteine

### 1. Homocysteine as Risk Marker (lines 2255-2269)
- Elevated blood homocysteine is reported as a risk factor for CAD, stroke, and PAD (refs 287-289)
- In elderly individuals aged >=85 years without CVD history, high homocysteine increased relative risk for MI (ref 290)
- Recent analyses suggest homocysteine may be a stronger marker than CRP (ref 291)
- **Critical limitation:** Vitamin supplementation to lower homocysteine did NOT suppress cardiovascular events (refs 292, 293)
- No association between ASCVD and hereditary genetic mutations causing elevated homocysteine (ref 294)

### 2. MTHFR 677C>T and Asian Populations (lines 2274-2286)
- In a meta-analysis of premature CAD risk, homocysteine effect was not seen in all subjects
- Significant risk increase was observed in:
  - Subjects with homocysteine >= 15 umol/L
  - Asians with the MTHFR 677C>T polymorphism (ref 295)
- The suggested mechanism: relatively lower folic acid intake by Asians compared to other races
- This implies a need to study hyperhomocysteinemia as an independent risk factor specifically in Japan

### 3. Genetic Evidence (lines 2286-2282)
- GWAS in individuals of European descent found that homocysteine concentration did not contribute to CAD risk in Caucasians (ref 296)
- Various genetic polymorphisms affecting homocysteine concentration were evaluated

### 4. Position Among Other Risk Factors (lines 1704-1711)
- Homocysteine is mentioned alongside Lp(a) and small dense LDL as risk factors "that should be considered" and are "genetically regulated"
- These are categorized under "Other Risk Factors and Markers to Be Considered" (Section 12), separate from established risk factors
- The guidelines note these may be genuine risk factors OR merely markers for ASCVD

## Folate/B-Vitamin Recommendations

The guidelines contain **no specific recommendations for folate or B-vitamin supplementation** for ASCVD prevention. Dietary recommendations focus on:
- Traditional Japanese dietary pattern (fish, vegetables, seaweed, soy products)
- No mention of folate-rich foods as specific ASCVD prevention strategy
- No recommendation to supplement folic acid or vitamin B12 for homocysteine lowering

## Methodology

- Evidence levels: E-Ia (meta-analysis of cohort studies) to E-III (descriptive studies)
- Recommendation levels: A (strong) or B (weak)
- Systematic review based on MINDS method, literature through end of 2015
- Homocysteine section lacks a formal recommendation level, suggesting insufficient evidence for a clinical recommendation

## Limitations

- Homocysteine content is limited to approximately 30 lines within a 9,000+ line guideline
- No specific CQ (Clinical Question) was formulated for homocysteine
- No recommendation level assigned to homocysteine management
- Japanese guideline perspective may limit generalizability to non-Asian populations
- Guidelines do not address methylfolate specifically

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (homocysteine as CVD risk factor):** Moderate relevance -- acknowledges association but emphasizes negative interventional trials
- **Q3 (dietary factors and homocysteine):** Indirectly relevant -- notes lower folic acid intake in Asians as background for MTHFR-related risk
- **Q4 (B-vitamin supplementation for homocysteine):** Directly relevant -- states vitamin supplementation did not reduce events despite lowering homocysteine

## Claims to Verify

- "therapies for lowering the concentration of homocysteine through vitamin supplementation did not result in the suppression of events (292, 293)" -- critical claim that B-vitamin supplementation is ineffective for CVD prevention; worth checking the specific RCTs cited
- "homocysteine is a stronger marker than is CRP (291)" -- single reference supporting this comparative claim
