# src-015: Practitioner Know How - The Methylfolate Myths

**Source:** https://www.mthfrsupport.com.au/2014/08/practitioner-know-methylfolate-myths
**Type:** Practitioner blog post (MTHFR Support Australia)
**Date:** August 1, 2014
**Quality:** ok (substantive content, but not peer-reviewed)

## Core Findings

This practitioner-facing blog post warns that prescribing methylfolate based solely on a positive MTHFR mutation result -- without assessing the full methylation cycle -- can cause over-methylation and serious adverse reactions. The author (a clinician at MTHFR Support Australia) reports seeing increasing numbers of patients with adverse reactions from other practitioners who prescribed high-dose methylfolate immediately upon finding an MTHFR mutation.

## Key Evidence

### Over-Methylation Symptoms / Side Effects of Methylfolate
The article lists the following side effects (lines 29-47):
- Insomnia
- Muscle and joint pain
- Rash
- Severe anxiety and irritability
- Palpitations
- Nausea and vomiting
- Headaches, migraines
- Decrease in mood, including severe depression and suicidal thoughts
- Increase in allergic symptoms and reactions

### Clinical Cautions
- **Full methylation cycle assessment needed:** Discovering an MTHFR mutation should trigger assessment of peripheral systems and genes surrounding MTHFR, not immediate methylfolate supplementation (lines 25-26).
- **Pre-existing conditions can be amplified:** Patients with allergies, inflammation, poor detoxification, hormonal imbalances, or mood/neurotransmitter imbalances may have these issues worsened by methylfolate if not addressed first (lines 53-54).
- **Gentle supplementation may suffice for some:** For otherwise healthy patients with few conditions, gentle methylfolate may be all that is needed (lines 51-52).

### Recommended Testing Before Supplementation
- B12, RBC folate, zinc, copper, hormonal profiles, homocysteine, and genetic testing (e.g., 23andMe) (line 55-56).

## Methodology

This is a clinical observation/opinion piece from a practitioner, not a systematic study. No sample sizes, controlled trials, or quantitative data are provided. The claims are based on the author's clinical experience.

## Limitations

- **Not peer-reviewed:** This is a blog post on a commercial clinic website that also promotes paid practitioner training webinars.
- **No quantitative data:** No sample sizes, effect sizes, or statistical evidence provided.
- **Potential conflict of interest:** The post promotes a paid practitioner training series on MTHFR/methylation.
- **Anecdotal evidence:** Claims are based on clinical observations, not controlled studies.
- **Single practitioner perspective:** Represents the experience of one clinical practice.

## Relevance to Q5 (Risks/Harms)

Directly relevant to Q5. Provides a practitioner's list of methylfolate side effects and the clinical reasoning for why over-methylation occurs when methylfolate is prescribed without full methylation cycle assessment. However, the evidence strength is weak because it is anecdotal and unpublished. It can be used to contextualize what clinicians report observing, but should not be cited as primary evidence for specific side effect rates or severities.

## Claims to Verify

- The list of side effects (insomnia, anxiety, palpitations, depression, suicidal thoughts, etc.) cannot be verified from this source alone. These should be cross-referenced with peer-reviewed literature on methylfolate adverse events.
- The assertion that "majority" of patients referred from other practitioners had "serious adverse reactions" is unsubstantiated and should be treated as anecdotal.
