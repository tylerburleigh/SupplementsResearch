# src-487: Lewis, Ebrahim, Davey Smith (2005) - BMJ

## Full Citation
Lewis SJ, Ebrahim S, Davey Smith G. Meta-analysis of MTHFR 677C->T polymorphism and coronary heart disease: does totality of evidence support causal role for homocysteine and preventive potential of folate? BMJ. 2005 Nov 5;331(7524):1053. doi: 10.1136/bmj.38611.658947.55

## Core Findings

This meta-analysis of 80 studies (26,000 cases, 31,813 controls) found a modest overall association between MTHFR 677TT genotype and CHD (OR 1.14, 95% CI 1.05-1.24), but this was driven by heterogeneity across geographical regions. In folate-sufficient regions (Europe, North America, Australia), no meaningful association existed (ORs 0.93-1.08). The authors conclude that the evidence does not support a causal role for homocysteine in CHD in high-income/folate-fortified populations, casting doubt on the rationale for folic acid supplementation for CVD prevention.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Overall MTHFR-CHD Association
- Overall random effects OR: 1.14 (95% CI 1.05-1.24), TT vs CC genotype
- 80 studies, 81 data points, 26,000 cases and 31,813 controls
- 24 additional studies identified beyond previous meta-analyses (Wald et al, Klerk et al)
- Strong between-study heterogeneity: P < 0.001, I-squared = 38.4%

### Regional Stratification (Critical Finding)

Heterogeneity largely resolved after regional stratification.

### Homocysteine and Meta-regression
- Overall difference in homocysteine (CC minus TT): 2.24 micromol/L (95% CI 1.55-2.94)
- 20 studies provided homocysteine data by genotype
- Meta-regression: beta = 0.103, P = 0.02 (homocysteine difference associated with CHD risk)
- Difference ranged from -0.8 to 11 micromol/L across studies
- North American homocysteine difference: 1.35 micromol/L (small, consistent with fortification)
- Middle East homocysteine difference: 8.80 micromol/L (large, consistent with low folate intake)

### Publication Bias / Small Study Bias
- Egger test P = 0.03 overall, indicating small study bias
- This contrasts with Wald et al's Egger test P = 0.55
- Largest Japanese study: null result (OR 0.99, 0.74-1.32)
- Largest Middle Eastern study: not incompatible with null
- Authors suggest positive studies from Middle East and Japan could represent publication bias

## Methodology

- **Design**: Systematic review and meta-analysis using mendelian randomization principles
- **Search**: Medline and Embase, January 2001 to August 2004, updating previous meta-analyses; no language restrictions
- **Inclusion**: Case-control and prospective studies of MTHFR 677C->T and myocardial infarction or coronary artery occlusion
- **Analysis**: Random effects models; TT vs CC genotype comparison; stratified by geographical region; I-squared for heterogeneity; meta-regression by homocysteine difference
- **Software**: Stata version 8
- **Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium**: 5 studies showed deviation (P < 0.05); exclusion made minimal difference

## Limitations

1. Publication bias likely causes overestimation of effects despite comprehensive search
2. No individual-level data; could not account for uncertainty in homocysteine differences in meta-regression
3. Homocysteine data not limited to healthy participants; disease status may cloud true genotype effect
4. Too few Japanese and Middle Eastern studies to formally test for small study bias
5. Observational genetic epidemiology lacks mechanisms for robust systematic reviews (compared to RCTs)

## Relevance to Research Questions

### Q1: MTHFR polymorphism, homocysteine, and cardiovascular outcomes
**Highly relevant.** This is one of the largest and most-cited meta-analyses of MTHFR 677C->T and CHD. It demonstrates that the MTHFR-CHD association is geographically heterogeneous and essentially null in folate-sufficient populations. This directly challenges the mendelian randomization inference that homocysteine is causally related to CHD.

### Q4: Folate supplementation and cardiovascular outcomes
**Highly relevant.** The paper explicitly addresses whether folic acid supplementation would prevent CHD. The authors argue that in regions where folate intake is already adequate (Europe, North America, Australia), there is no evidence that raising folate would reduce CHD risk. They question the inclusion of folic acid in the "Polypill" strategy. The regional variation is consistent with the hypothesis that folate only matters where intake is low, but the authors note publication bias as an alternative explanation.

## Claims to Verify

1. **Sample sizes**: "26,000 cases and 31,813 controls" - these are round numbers for cases but specific for controls. The abstract says "26 000 cases and 31 183 controls" while the results text says "26 000 cases and 31 813 controls." This discrepancy (31,183 vs 31,813) should be noted. The table summary uses 31,813.

2. **Wald et al Egger test P = 0.55**: This is cited from Wald et al's paper. Worth verifying in the original if possible.

3. **The largest Japanese study OR 0.99 (0.74-1.32)**: This is attributed to Shioji et al 2004 (ref 16). The claim that this large study gave a null result is load-bearing for the publication bias argument.

4. **"80 studies" vs "81 data points"**: The paper says 81 data points from 80 studies, meaning one study contributed two data points. This is minor but worth noting for precision.

## Quality Assessment

- **Source quality**: Full-text peer-reviewed BMJ paper with detailed methods, results tables, and discussion
- **Study quality**: Comprehensive meta-analysis with appropriate methods (random effects, heterogeneity assessment, publication bias testing, regional stratification)
- **Key strength**: Large sample size, geographical stratification, explicit attention to publication bias
- **Key limitation**: Cannot fully resolve whether regional variation reflects true folate-mediated effects or publication bias
