# src-494: Homocysteine and Dementia: An International Consensus Statement

**Citation:** Smith AD, Refsum H, Bottiglieri T, et al. (2018). Journal of Alzheimer's Disease, 62(2):561-570. DOI: 10.3233/JAD-171042. ~352 citations.

**Source quality:** Full text available from ResearchGate (SAGE Publications open access, CC BY-NC 4.0). The PDF extraction is word-by-word separated (one word per line) but contains the complete paper including abstract, introduction, all Bradford Hill criteria sections, Table 1 (meta-analyses), Table 2 (trial criteria), clinical trial summaries, and conclusions.

## Core Findings

An international panel of 9 experts (from Oxford, Oslo, Baylor, CSIRO, Karolinska, Cardiff, Rutgers, Tufts, Saarland) reviewed 20 years of literature and concluded, using Bradford Hill criteria for causation, that elevated plasma total homocysteine (tHcy) is a **modifiable risk factor** for cognitive decline, dementia, and Alzheimer's disease in older persons. The evidence satisfies all nine Bradford Hill criteria: strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, analogy, and experiment.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Strength of Association (Table 1)
- Seven meta-analyses of prospective cohort studies all show significant association between elevated tHcy and dementia/AD
- Pooled risk estimates range from RR 1.15 to 2.5
- Xu et al. meta-analysis: RR 1.15 (CI 1.02-1.27) for AD in 13 cohort studies on 6,310 subjects (Grade 1 evidence)
- Beydoun et al.: RR 1.93 (CI 1.50-2.49) for incident AD in 5 cohorts on 4,412 subjects
- Van Dam et al.: RR 2.5 (CI 1.38-4.56) for AD in 3 prospective studies on 2,569 subjects

### Mendelian Randomization Evidence
- Hu et al. meta-analysis: 34 studies on 9,397 subjects
- MTHFR C677T TT vs CC alleles: OR 1.37 (CI 1.15-1.63) for AD
- Per 1 SD increase in ln(tHcy): OR 3.37 (CI 1.9-5.95)
- Association stronger in Asian/mixed populations than Caucasians

### Population Attributable Risk (PAR%)
- PAR estimates range from 4.3% to 31% depending on the meta-analysis
- Based on prevalence of hyperhomocysteinemia of 25-30%
- Beydoun et al.: PAR 18.9-21.8%; Shen et al.: PAR 16.1-18.8%; Nie et al.: PAR 11.7-13.7%
- 12-31% of dementia/AD cases could potentially be prevented by lowering tHcy

### Biological Gradient / Threshold
- Nearly 5-fold higher dementia incidence in those with tHcy >15.0 vs <10.1 umol/L (Italian study)
- Concentration-dependent cognitive decline in OPTIMA over 10.0-18.0 umol/L range
- Threshold at ~11.1 umol/L for medial temporal lobe atrophy rate
- Threshold at 11 umol/L for spatial copying impairment (Normative Aging Study)

### Clinical Trial Evidence

**FACIT Trial** (n=818, Netherlands):
- Normal elderly with tHcy 13-26 umol/L
- 0.8 mg folic acid/day for 3 years; 26% tHcy reduction
- Slowed decline in information processing speed
- Performance equivalent to someone 4.7 years younger for memory, 1.7 years younger for sensorimotor speed

**AD Cooperative Study** (n=340, USA):
- Probable AD patients; 5 mg folic acid + 1 mg B12 + 25 mg B6 for 18 months
- 26% tHcy reduction (9.2 to 6.78 umol/L)
- No overall benefit, but subgroup analysis: patients with higher baseline MMSE or CDR 0.5 showed significant benefit

**VITACOG Trial** (n=168 with MCI):
- 0.8 mg folic acid + 0.5 mg B12 + 20 mg B6 for 2 years
- 30.2% tHcy reduction; 29.6% slowing of whole brain atrophy
- Top quartile (tHcy >13 umol/L): 53% slowing of brain atrophy
- Regional AD-specific atrophy slowed by 88.5%
- Bayesian DAG: B vitamins -> lower tHcy -> slow brain atrophy -> slow cognitive decline
- Cognitive benefits only in those with tHcy above median (11.3 umol/L)
- Omega-3 fatty acid status modulated B vitamin response

### Mechanisms
- Vascular mechanisms, regional brain atrophy, neurofibrillary tangle and amyloid plaque formation, neuronal death, epigenetic mechanisms
- Raised tHcy remained associated with cognitive impairment after adjusting for B vitamin status
- Multiple biological pathways likely involved simultaneously

## Methodology

Expert consensus statement (not a systematic review) applying Bradford Hill's nine criteria for causation. Based on review of literature from the preceding 20 years, including seven meta-analyses of prospective studies and three key clinical trials (FACIT, ADEGS, VITACOG).

## Limitations

- Not a systematic review; represents expert panel conclusions
- Many meta-analyses included overlapping cohorts
- Most B vitamin clinical trials had flawed design (76% of 20,431 participants in largest meta-analysis lacked baseline cognitive measures)
- Specificity criterion is not met (tHcy associated with many vascular outcomes beyond dementia)
- Many negative trials excluded subjects with high tHcy or failed to show cognitive decline in placebo group
- Post-hoc subgroup analyses in some trials (VITACOG omega-3 interaction)

## Relevance to Research Question (Q6: Homocysteine and dementia)

This is a **key source** for Q6. It provides the most comprehensive synthesis of evidence for the causal role of elevated homocysteine in dementia, applying a formal causal framework (Bradford Hill criteria). It summarizes all major meta-analyses and clinical trials up to 2018, provides PAR estimates showing substantial public health impact, and establishes a clinically relevant tHcy threshold (~11-13 umol/L). The finding that B vitamin treatment (including folate) slows brain atrophy and cognitive decline specifically in those with elevated tHcy directly supports the mechanistic link between folate/homocysteine metabolism and dementia risk.

## Claims to Verify

- The FACIT trial "4.7 years younger" for memory claim is striking but appears to come from the original FACIT publication (Durga et al.); would benefit from cross-checking against the primary source.
- The 88.5% slowing of regional AD-specific atrophy in VITACOG is a post-hoc voxel-based analysis and should be interpreted cautiously.
- The PAR estimates depend heavily on assumed prevalence of hyperhomocysteinemia (25-30%) and may vary by population.
