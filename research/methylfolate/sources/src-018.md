Types of Methylfolate: Which Form is Best?

Not all methylfolate supplements are created equal. The form of methylfolate used in a supplement can significantly impact its stability, absorption, and effectiveness. Here’s what you need to know about the main forms:

Quatrefolic (Glucosamine Salt)

Quatrefolic is a fourth-generation folate that binds L-5-MTHF to glucosamine (non-shellfish derived). Research suggests it may have higher initial bioavailability than calcium salt forms, meaning it becomes available in the bloodstream faster. However, some stability studies indicate it may degrade more quickly over time compared to crystalline calcium salt forms. Many popular brands including Jarrow Formulas, NOW Foods, and Seeking Health use Quatrefolic.

Metafolin (Calcium Salt – Type I Crystalline)

Metafolin was the first patented form of L-methylfolate, developed by Merck. It uses a crystalline calcium salt structure that provides excellent long-term stability. Brands like Pure Encapsulations and Solgar typically use Metafolin. The crystalline structure helps maintain potency throughout the product’s shelf life.

Generic L-5-MTHF Calcium Salt

Some supplements use generic calcium salt forms of methylfolate. While these can still be effective, quality and stability may vary more between manufacturers. Look for products that specify they use the (6S) or “L” isomer, which is the biologically active form.

Which Form Should You Choose?

For most people, both Quatrefolic and Metafolin are excellent choices. The differences in bioavailability are relatively minor, and both forms have demonstrated effectiveness in clinical research. More important than the specific patented form is choosing a reputable brand with third-party testing and proper storage practices.

Buying Guide: What to Look For in a Methylfolate Supplement

Quality Indicators

Patented Ingredient Forms:
Look for supplements using branded methylfolate forms like Quatrefolic, Metafolin, or Magnafolate. These patented ingredients come with quality guarantees and research backing that generic forms may lack.

Third-Party Testing:
Reputable brands conduct independent testing for purity, potency, and contaminants. Look for products that display testing certifications or offer Certificates of Analysis.

Correct Isomer:
Ensure the label specifies L-methylfolate, (6S)-5-MTHF, or L-5-MTHF. The “L” or “6S” designation indicates the biologically active form. Products listing just “5-MTHF” without clarification may contain inactive forms.

Clean Formulation:
Minimal inactive ingredients reduce the risk of reactions and indicate quality manufacturing. Avoid products with artificial colors, unnecessary fillers, or common allergens unless you know you tolerate them.

Certifications That Matter

GMP (Good Manufacturing Practices):
Indicates the facility follows FDA-recognized quality standards for supplement manufacturing.

NSF International:
Independent testing organization that verifies products contain what they claim and are free from contaminants.

NSF Certified for Sport:
Especially important for athletes; confirms products are free from banned substances.

Non-GMO Project Verified:
Third-party verification that products are free from genetically modified ingredients.

Red Flags to Avoid

Products listing only “folate” or “folic acid” without specifying methylfolate

Unusually low prices that suggest inferior ingredients

Missing or vague information about ingredient sources

No third-party testing claims or certifications

Products containing both folic acid and methylfolate (unnecessary and potentially problematic)

Outrageous health claims or promises of disease treatment

How to Take Methylfolate

Recommended Dosage

The Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for folate in adults is 400 mcg DFE daily, increasing to 600 mcg DFE during pregnancy. However, methylfolate supplements often provide higher amounts because they may be used to address specific health concerns beyond basic nutritional needs.

For general supplementation, 400-1,000 mcg of methylfolate daily is typically appropriate. Higher doses (1-15 mg) should only be used under healthcare provider guidance based on individual testing and health needs.

Best Time to Take Methylfolate

Methylfolate can generally be taken at any time of day with or without food. However, some individuals find that taking methylfolate in the morning works best, as it may affect energy levels. Several brands recommend avoiding methylfolate within 5 hours of bedtime, as some people report sleep disturbances when taking it late in the day.

Usage Tips for Different Forms

Capsules and Tablets:
Take with a full glass of water. May be taken with food if stomach upset occurs.

Lozenges:
Allow to dissolve slowly under the tongue for sublingual absorption. Do not chew or swallow whole.

Liquid Drops:
Follow product-specific directions; typically taken directly under the tongue or mixed with a small amount of water.

Duration and Cycling

Methylfolate is generally safe for long-term daily use, as it is the natural form of folate used by the body. Unlike some supplements, there is typically no need to cycle methylfolate. However, if you experience side effects, your healthcare provider may recommend adjusting the dose or taking breaks.

It may take several weeks to notice the effects of methylfolate supplementation, particularly for mood-related benefits. Consistent daily use is more important than specific timing.

Potential Benefits of Methylfolate

Research suggests methylfolate may support several aspects of health. Here’s what the science indicates:

Methylation Support

Methylfolate is essential for the methylation cycle—a biochemical process that occurs in every cell of the body. Proper methylation is involved in DNA synthesis and repair, neurotransmitter production, detoxification processes, and gene expression regulation. Individuals with MTHFR gene variants may have reduced ability to convert folic acid to methylfolate, making direct supplementation potentially beneficial.

Cardiovascular Health

Folate helps metabolize homocysteine, an amino acid that, when elevated, has been associated with cardiovascular concerns. Studies have shown that methylfolate supplementation may help maintain healthy homocysteine levels already within normal range, potentially supporting heart health. A study published in research literature found that 400 mcg of Quatrefolic, combined with vitamins B6 and B12, was more effective at reducing homocysteine than high-dose folic acid alone.

Mood and Cognitive Function

Methylfolate crosses the blood-brain barrier and is involved in the production of neurotransmitters including serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine. Some research suggests that methylfolate supplementation may support mood balance, particularly in individuals with certain genetic variations. High-dose methylfolate (7.5-15 mg) has been studied as an adjunct to conventional treatments for mood concerns, though this use should only be pursued under healthcare provider supervision.

Prenatal Health

Adequate folate is critical during pregnancy for proper fetal development, particularly neural tube formation in early pregnancy. While folic acid has been the traditional recommendation, methylfolate may be preferable for women with MTHFR variants or those who want to avoid unconverted folic acid. Pregnant women should consult their healthcare provider before choosing a folate form and dosage.

Safety Considerations

Potential Side Effects

While methylfolate is generally well-tolerated, some individuals may experience side effects, particularly when starting supplementation or taking higher doses:

Irritability or mood changes

Headaches

Difficulty sleeping (insomnia)

Nausea or digestive discomfort

Muscle aches or joint pain

Skin rashes (rare)

Anxiety or restlessness

These effects often indicate the dose is too high or that methylfolate is being processed too quickly. Reducing the dose or taking methylfolate with other B vitamins (particularly B12, B6, and riboflavin) may help.

Who Should Avoid or Use Caution

Individuals with bipolar disorder:
High-dose methylfolate may trigger manic episodes in some individuals; use only under psychiatric supervision.

Those taking anticonvulsants:
Methylfolate may interact with certain anti-seizure medications; consult your healthcare provider.

People on methotrexate for cancer:
5-MTHF supplementation is not recommended during methotrexate cancer therapy as it may interfere with treatment effectiveness. (Note: This does not apply to methotrexate used for autoimmune conditions.)

Individuals with vitamin B12 deficiency:
High-dose folate may mask symptoms of B12 deficiency, potentially allowing neurological damage to progress undetected. Always ensure adequate B12 status before taking high-dose methylfolate.

Drug Interactions

Methylfolate may interact with several medications including certain anti-seizure drugs (phenobarbital, phenytoin, primidone), methotrexate, and some antibiotics. Always inform your healthcare provider and pharmacist about all supplements you take.

When to Consult a Healthcare Provider

Before starting methylfolate if you take any prescription medications

If you’re pregnant, planning pregnancy, or breastfeeding

If you have a history of mood disorders

Before using doses above 1 mg daily

If you experience persistent side effects

If you want to use methylfolate for a specific health condition

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between folic acid and methylfolate?

Folic acid is the synthetic, oxidized form of folate commonly used in fortified foods and basic supplements. It must be converted through multiple enzymatic steps in the body to become usable methylfolate. L-methylfolate (5-MTHF) is the biologically active form that requires no conversion and can be used immediately by the body. This is particularly important for individuals with MTHFR gene variants who may have reduced ability to convert folic acid.

How do I know if I have an MTHFR mutation?

MTHFR gene variants can be identified through genetic testing, either through healthcare providers or direct-to-consumer testing services. Common variants include C677T and A1298C. However, having an MTHFR variant doesn’t necessarily mean you need supplementation—many people with these variants maintain adequate folate status through diet alone. Testing homocysteine levels can provide functional insight into whether supplementation may be beneficial.

Can I take methylfolate while pregnant?

Methylfolate can be an appropriate choice during pregnancy, and some prenatal vitamins now include it instead of or in addition to folic acid. However, the standard recommendation for pregnancy remains 400-800 mcg of folate daily (from any source). Consult your healthcare provider before choosing between folic acid and methylfolate during pregnancy.

How long does it take to notice benefits from methylfolate?

Some individuals notice effects within days to weeks, particularly in terms of energy levels. However, full benefits for mood support or homocysteine reduction may take 4-12 weeks of consistent supplementation. Patience and consistent dosing are important.

Is it possible to take too much methylfolate?

Yes. While methylfolate doesn’t have an established tolerable upper intake level like folic acid (which is 1,000 mcg daily for adults), high doses can cause side effects in some individuals. More is not always better with methylfolate—starting low and increasing gradually is the safest approach.

Should I take methylfolate with B12?

Methylfolate and B12 work together in the methylation cycle, and many practitioners recommend taking them together. This is particularly important with higher doses of methylfolate, as adequate B12 is needed to complete the metabolic cycle. If you’re taking methylfolate alone, ensure your diet or a separate supplement provides adequate B12.

What’s the difference between Quatrefolic and Metafolin?

Both are patented, high-quality forms of L-methylfolate. Quatrefolic uses a glucosamine salt, while Metafolin uses a calcium salt. Research suggests Quatrefolic may have slightly faster initial absorption, while Metafolin may have better long-term stability. In practical terms, both are effective choices, and the difference is relatively minor for most users.

Can methylfolate help with depression?

Some research suggests methylfolate may support mood, particularly when used as an adjunct to conventional treatments. High-dose methylfolate (7.5-15 mg) has been studied in this context. However, methylfolate supplements are not treatments for depression, and anyone experiencing depression should work with a healthcare provider. Never replace prescribed medications with supplements without medical guidance.

Why does methylfolate give some people anxiety or irritability?

Some individuals experience “overmethylation” symptoms when taking methylfolate, especially at higher doses. This may manifest as anxiety, irritability, or restlessness. This can happen when methylfolate is taken without adequate B12, or when the dose is too high for an individual’s needs. Reducing the dose or adding cofactors like B12 and niacin often helps.

Is methylfolate safe for children?

Methylfolate can be appropriate for children, but dosing should be determined by a pediatric healthcare provider based on the child’s age, weight, and specific needs. Some children’s multivitamins now include small amounts of methylfolate.
