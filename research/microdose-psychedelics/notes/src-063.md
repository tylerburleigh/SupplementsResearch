# src-063: Calder & Hasler (2023) - Towards an Understanding of Psychedelic-Induced Neuroplasticity

**Citation:** Calder AE, Hasler G. Neuropsychopharmacology (2023) 48:104-112. doi:10.1038/s41386-022-01389-z
**Type:** Review article | **Citations:** 240 | **Quality:** ok (full text available via raw text extraction)

## Core Findings

This comprehensive review synthesizes evidence that classic psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, DMT, DOI) enhance neuroplasticity primarily through 5-HT2A receptor-dependent mechanisms, with the strongest effects in cortical regions (especially PFC). The neuroplasticity window opens within 1-6 hours and structural changes persist for at least one month. However, the evidence base is heavily skewed toward animal/in vitro studies, with human evidence limited to peripheral BDNF (inconsistent results) and neuroimaging of connectivity changes. The review identifies important compound-specific and region-specific differences that complicate simple generalizations.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Mechanisms of Neuroplasticity
- **Primary pathway:** 5-HT2A receptor activation on layer 5/6 pyramidal neurons leads to extracellular glutamate increase, AMPA receptor stimulation, triggering BDNF-TrkB-mTOR positive feedback loop sustaining dendritic growth
- **Ketanserin (5-HT2A antagonist):** Low doses partially block, high doses completely block psychedelic-induced neuroplasticity; 5-HT2A knockout mice show no plasticity enhancement
- **Alternative receptors:** DMT neurogenesis involves sigma-1 receptors; 5-MeO-DMT effects may involve 5-HT1A receptors (1000-fold higher affinity for 5-HT1A vs 5-HT2A)

### Regional Specificity
- **Neocortex/PFC:** Robust effects -- upregulated plasticity genes, increased synaptogenesis, spinogenesis, dendritic growth
- **Hippocampus:** Modest or inconsistent effects; some studies show opposite pattern (reduced glutamate after psilocybin). Likely due to higher 5-HT1A:5-HT2A receptor ratio
- **Subcortical regions:** Limited evidence; c-fos upregulation seen in claustrum, locus ceruleus, lateral habenula, thalamus, amygdala
- **Mesolimbic pathway:** Low 5-HT2A receptor expression means limited direct plasticity effects; anti-addictive effects may operate via PFC projections

### Dose-Response Findings
- **Animal studies:** Dose-dependent effects on gene expression (LSD 0.2-1 mg/kg, psilocybin 4 mg/kg required). Sub-hallucinogenic 1 mg/kg DMT increased functional plasticity in rat cortical slices
- **Human microdosing data:** 5-20 microg LSD produced significant short-term BDNF increases, but 25-200 microg LSD only showed effects at 200 microg in some studies; other studies found no change at all
- **Chronic dosing:** Animal studies show enhanced learning with chronic LSD, but chronic DMT may cause dendritic spine retraction; chronic LSD also upregulated schizophrenia-related genes

### Timeline of Neuroplasticity
- Gene expression changes (IEGs): within 1-2 hours
- BDNF mRNA upregulation: 2-3 hours
- Morphological changes (dendritogenesis, synaptogenesis): starting at 6 hours, peak at 24-72 hours
- Rate of spine formation returns to baseline: ~5 days
- New structures persist: at least 1 month
- Chronic dosing (LSD every other day for 1 month): BDNF upregulation in medial PFC persisting 4 weeks after cessation

### Neurogenesis (Compound-Specific)
- LSD and DOI: no effect on adult neurogenesis in rats
- Psilocybin: slight reduction in mice
- DMT: increased neurogenesis (sigma-1 receptor dependent)
- 5-MeO-DMT: increased neurogenesis (possibly 5-HT1A mediated)

## Methodology

Systematic narrative review covering cellular/molecular, morphological, and functional levels of neuroplasticity. Synthesized evidence from animal studies (rats, mice, pigs, in vitro neuronal cultures) and human studies (peripheral BDNF measurements, PET imaging, fMRI connectivity). Organized around specific questions: whether psychedelics enhance plasticity, mechanisms, brain regions, doses, duration, and consequences.

## Limitations

1. **Translational gap is the central limitation:** Animal evidence is moderately strong; human evidence remains inconclusive, especially for peripheral BDNF
2. **Peripheral BDNF is a flawed biomarker:** Psychoplastogens may increase peripheral BDNF without increasing brain BDNF; platelets store/release BDNF independently; BDNF may not correlate with cortical plasticity measures
3. **Lack of causal inference:** Few studies have directly linked neuroplastic changes to behavioral outcomes; most evidence is correlational
4. **Chronic dosing studies in animals** have not consistently differentiated microdoses from hallucinogenic doses
5. **Few direct human measures of structural plasticity** -- SV2A PET and LTP-induction protocols recommended for future work

## Relevance to Research Questions

### Q2: Neuroplasticity mechanisms at sub-hallucinogenic doses
**Highly relevant.** This is the most directly relevant source for Q2. Provides:
- Mechanistic pathway (5-HT2A -> glutamate -> AMPA -> BDNF-TrkB-mTOR feedback loop)
- Direct discussion of microdose-level dosing and neuroplasticity
- Human microdose BDNF data (5-20 microg LSD)
- Animal dose-response relationships showing sub-hallucinogenic doses can promote plasticity
- Important caveat that chronic microdosing effects may differ from single doses and may carry risks

### Q4: Translational gap between preclinical and clinical evidence
**Highly relevant.** The review explicitly maps the gap:
- Animal studies: moderately strong evidence for gene expression, synaptic growth, dendritic changes
- Human studies: inconclusive peripheral BDNF, limited neuroimaging of connectivity
- Identifies methodological limitations of peripheral BDNF as translational bridge
- Recommends specific improved methods (paired associative stimulation, SV2A PET)
- Notes compound-specific and region-specific effects that complicate cross-species extrapolation

## Claims to Verify

- "5-20 microg LSD produced significant short-term enhancements in plasma BDNF" -- this relies on a single study (Hutten et al. 2020); should verify the sample size and effect size in the original
- "1 mg/kg DMT increased functional plasticity" -- from Ly et al. 2018; verify whether this is truly sub-hallucinogenic in rats
- "Chronic DMT dosing may cause retraction of dendritic spines" -- single citation (ref 115); important negative finding that should be verified
- "4 mg/kg psilocybin required to induce neuroplasticity-related gene expression changes" -- from Jefsen et al. 2020; verify dose range and species
