# src-208: Great Expectations (Aday et al. 2022, Psychopharmacology)

## Core Findings

This review article comprehensively documents the methodological challenges facing psychedelic clinical trials, with a primary focus on expectancy effects and masking (blinding) failures. The authors argue that psychedelic trials face compounded challenges from both psychotherapy and pharmacology research traditions, plus unique problems including cultural "hype" and the pronounced subjective effects that make functional unmasking nearly inevitable. They provide detailed, practical recommendations for improving methodological rigor across all stages of trial design.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Blinding/Masking Failures
- Active placebo masking success rates in psychedelic trials are typically **less than 25%** or unreported (line ~674)
- A systematic review found masking was not maintained in **20 out of 23** "double-blind" psychotropic drug studies (Fisher and Greenberg 1993; line ~631)
- Only **59%** of psychiatry trial reports in 2017-2018 included adequate reporting of masking outcomes (Juul et al. 2020; line ~626)
- In the famous "Good Friday Experiment," all participants correctly ascertained their treatment condition despite the use of niacin as an active placebo (line ~662)
- Even with incomplete disclosure and multi-substance consent designs, participants accurately identify the experimental condition **~70-85%** of the time (line ~751)

### Expectancy Effects
- Meta-analysis (N=12,722): positive outcome expectancy associated with better treatment results, Cohen's d = 0.36 (Constantino et al. 2018; line ~266)
- In placebo analgesia studies, outcome expectancies accounted for up to **81% of variance** in post-treatment pain ratings (Fillingim and Price 2005; line ~273)
- Negative expectations completely negated the analgesic effect of remifentanil (Bingel et al. 2011; line ~286)
- Waitlist controls may artificially inflate effect sizes due to nocebo effects; psilocybin depression trial waitlisted participants reported higher anxiety at end of waitlist period (Davis et al. 2021; line ~312)

### Microdosing-Specific Evidence
- Kaertner et al. (2021): positive expectations at baseline predicted subsequent increases in wellbeing **irrespective of whether** a participant received a psychedelic or inert placebo (line ~426)
- Szigeti et al. (2021): large-scale, placebo-controlled microdosing study found **comparable improvements** in mood and cognition in drug and placebo conditions (line ~430)
- Van Elk et al. (2021): after controlling for baseline expectancies, **no difference** between psilocybin and placebo on measures of awe (line ~433)
- These findings are directly relevant to microdosing research, suggesting expectancy effects may fully account for observed microdosing benefits

### "Michael Pollan Effect" / Hype
- The authors coin/adopt the term "Michael Pollan Effect" for heightened positive expectations about psychedelic efficacy stemming from media coverage (line ~138, ~459)
- Pervasive positive messaging may lead to amplified expectations compared with other treatments
- Some participants express wanting to not "ruin the research" by failing to improve (line ~470)

## Methodology

This is a narrative review article, not a primary empirical study. The authors synthesize evidence from psychotherapy, pharmacology, and psychedelic-specific research literatures. They draw on systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and individual clinical trials to construct their argument. The paper provides recommendations organized by trial lifecycle stage: study development/design, participant recruitment/selection, outcomes/endpoints, study procedures, and analysis plans.

## Recommendations Summary

1. **Study design**: Use between-subjects designs with single dosing sessions; consider 3-arm designs (active treatment, active placebo, inactive control) to disentangle placebo effects; sequential parallel designs with placebo run-in to exclude placebo responders
2. **Recruitment**: Recruit psychedelic-naive participants; impose exclusion criteria on prior use
3. **Incomplete disclosure**: Withhold number of study arms and exact doses from participants and staff; list side effects of all potential study drugs together
4. **Active placebos**: Substances with hallucinatory effects (ketamine, DXM, high-dose THC) combined with drug-naive participants; pair with ultra-low-dose psychedelic
5. **Measurement**: Use validated expectancy measures (Stanford Expectations of Treatment Scale, Credibility and Expectancy Questionnaire); measure expectations at baseline AND post-dosing session
6. **Analysis**: Use expectancy and perceived treatment arm as covariates; compare against placebo benchmarks; power calculations based on known placebo effect sizes
7. **Alternative approaches**: Pragmatic clinical trials, natural experiments using population-level data

## Limitations

- This is a review article with no original data; recommendations are based on expert judgment rather than empirical validation
- The specific active placebo recommendations (ketamine, DXM, THC) have not been systematically tested in psychedelic efficacy trials
- The "Michael Pollan Effect" is a descriptive concept without formal quantification
- Some recommendations (e.g., incomplete disclosure, psychedelic-naive participants) have practical and ethical tradeoffs the authors acknowledge but do not fully resolve
- The review focuses primarily on high-dose (macrodosing) trials; microdosing-specific recommendations are limited

## Relevance to Research Questions

**Q3 (Methodological limitations - blinding, expectancy, active placebos):** This is one of the most comprehensive and authoritative sources on this topic. It systematically documents blinding failures across psychedelic trials, quantifies the magnitude of expectancy effects, reviews active placebo strategies and their limited success, and provides detailed recommendations. The paper directly cites evidence that only 1 of 23 double-blind psychotropic studies maintained adequate masking, and that psychedelic trial masking rates are typically below 25%.

**Q5 (Evidence challenging the benefit narrative):** The review provides strong support for the position that expectancy effects may drive much of the observed benefit in psychedelic trials, particularly for microdosing. It cites three key microdosing studies (Kaertner et al. 2021, Szigeti et al. 2021, van Elk et al. 2021) where expectancy/placebo effects fully accounted for apparent drug effects. It also highlights how waitlist control designs inflate apparent treatment effects.

## Claims to Verify

- The "less than 25%" masking success rate (line ~674) is attributed to multiple studies; the exact percentage should be verified against the cited primary sources (Bershad et al. 2019; Carbonaro et al. 2018; Griffiths et al. 2006) as this is a critical quantitative claim.
- The 20/23 figure for failed masking (line ~631) comes from Fisher and Greenberg 1993; this is an older review and the finding should be cross-referenced with more recent data.
- The 81% variance figure from Fillingim and Price 2005 (line ~273) appears extreme and should be verified in context -- it likely refers to a specific study or subset rather than all placebo analgesia research.
