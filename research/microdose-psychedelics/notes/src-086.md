# src-086: The Costs and Benefits of Psychedelics on Cognition and Mood

**Authors:** Ceyda Sayali & Frederick S. Barrett
**Venue:** Neuron (2023)
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.neuron.2022.12.031
**Type:** Review/Perspective article
**Source quality note:** Metadata marked "degraded" due to PDF extraction format issues, but content is substantive and complete.

## Core Findings

This review frames psychedelics as having a paradoxical therapeutic profile: acute cognitive disruption that paradoxically leads to lasting cognitive and emotional improvement. The central thesis is that psychedelics temporarily "loosen the mind's usual control systems," enabling a shift from mental rigidity (entrenched negative thought patterns) toward psychological and cognitive flexibility. This is supported by neuroplasticity changes (increased brain-derived growth factors, altered functional connectivity) that persist up to one month after a single dose.

## Key Arguments: Costs vs. Benefits

**Costs (acute):**
- Impaired cognitive control: slower responses, more errors on tasks requiring focus, working memory, and rule-switching
- Increased distractibility; difficulty articulating experiences ("ineffability")
- Higher doses can induce challenging/negative emotions through diminished self-control
- Perceptual distortions (hallucinations, warped space) that may be distressing

**Benefits (longer-term):**
- 75% response rate and 58% remission rate at 12-month follow-up for MDD after psilocybin (Gukasyan et al., 2022)
- Enhanced cognitive flexibility: fewer errors on rule-switching tasks 1 month post-treatment in depression (Doss et al., 2021)
- Psychological flexibility mediates reductions in anxiety and depression (Close et al., 2020)
- Emotional bias toward positivity: reduced sensitivity to negative stimuli under LSD (Preller et al., 2016)
- Altered brain network communication and decreased mood-linked brain activity persisting up to 1 month (Barrett et al., 2020)

## Cognitive and Mood Effects (Q1)

The review distinguishes acute vs. chronic effects clearly:
- **Acute:** Cognitive impairment across executive function, working memory, attentional control. Emotional amplification with positivity bias but potential for challenging experiences at higher doses.
- **Chronic (post-acute):** Improved cognitive flexibility, enhanced psychological flexibility, sustained mood improvement. Mystical-type experiences (30-40% in Pahnke's Good Friday experiment) associated with lasting positive change.

Notably, psilocybin was comparable but not superior to escitalopram in a head-to-head trial (Carhart-Harris et al., 2021). Conventional antidepressant response/remission rates are cited as 58%/46% (DeRubeis et al., 2005).

## Neuroplasticity Discussion

- Psychedelics increase brain substances that promote growth and adaptation (BDNF implied; Holze et al., 2021; Hutten et al., 2020)
- Changes in brain network communication observed after psilocybin (Barrett et al., 2020; Stoliker et al., 2022; McCulloch et al., 2021)
- These networks are involved in thought management, attention, and decision-making
- Neuroplasticity is presented as the mechanism bridging acute disruption to lasting therapeutic change

## Methodological Considerations (Q3)

- The review notes the dose-response complexity: higher doses increase both therapeutic potential and risk of adverse emotional experiences
- Self-report and subjective measures remain central to assessing mystical and emotional experiences
- The paradoxical relationship between acute impairment and long-term benefit complicates standard pharmacological assessment frameworks
- Comparisons with alcohol are used to highlight that psychedelics uniquely produce enduring cognitive benefits, not just transient changes

## Translational Gap (Q4)

- "The underlying mechanisms of psychedelics' therapeutic effects remain largely unknown" is stated explicitly at the outset
- The theoretical framework (mental rigidity vs. flexibility) is metaphorical ("well-trodden path in a forest") rather than mechanistically precise
- The review calls for understanding how single administrations produce lasting changes -- this remains an open question
- Integration with psychotherapy is suggested as critical, but the specific mechanisms of synergy are not specified

## Limitations

- Review/perspective article -- no new primary data
- Heavy reliance on metaphor and analogy for theoretical framework
- Does not systematically address microdosing (focus is on full-dose therapeutic use)
- No discussion of adverse events, exclusion criteria, or contraindications in detail
- Comparison data with conventional treatments is limited to two studies

## Claims to Verify

- "75% response rate" and "58% remission at 12 months" from Gukasyan et al. (2022) -- these are high; verify against the original study's completer vs. intention-to-treat analysis
- "Conventional treatment response/remission rates: 58%/46%" from DeRubeis et al. (2005) -- verify this is the correct comparison frame (the original study compared CBT vs. medication, not all conventional treatments)
- The alcohol comparison (Benedek et al., 2017) is cited as showing temporary creative boost without lasting benefit -- verify this characterization
