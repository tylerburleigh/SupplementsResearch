Abstract

Vascular aging is a major risk factor for cardiovascular
diseases
(CVDs) in the older individuals. Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG),
the primary active compound in green tea, exhibits cardiovascular
protective effects. However, its effect and the underlying mechanism
of the same on vascular aging remain unclear. Herein, doxorubicin
(DOX) was employed to develop an endothelial cell senescence model.
In vitro
results indicated that EGCG alleviates endothelial
cell senescence by promoting autophagy and inhibiting ferroptosis,
supported by Western blot analysis.
In vivo
animal
studies revealed a significant decrease in aortic senescence-associated
β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) positive staining and pulse
wave velocity (PWV) in the EGCG group compared with that in the control
group (
p
< 0.001), indicating that EGCG can significantly
attenuate vascular aging in mice. Multiple lines of evidence suggest
that EGCG alleviates vascular aging by activating sirtuin 1 (SIRT1),
which promotes autophagy and inhibits ferroptosis.

1. Introduction

Aging has emerged as a
global public health concern, characterized
by decreasing cellular proliferation, increasing cell death, organ
dysfunction, and heightened risk of age-related diseases such as cardiovascular
diseases (CVDs) and neurodegenerative disorders.

,

CVDs
remain the leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. Vascular aging is a significant risk factor for
CVDs.

,

Age is the most critical risk factor for
vascular aging, the key features of which include vascular remodeling,
endothelial dysfunction, and a progressive loss of vascular function.

,

The gradual decline in vascular function during aging is a major
contributor to the high prevalence of CVDs in the older population.

,

In addition to chronological aging, pathological and biochemical
changes, such as increased oxidative stress and inflammation, accelerate
cellular aging.

,

Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms
underlying vascular aging and identifying effective therapeutic agents
are paramount for intervention and treatment of vascular aging.

Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) is an NAD
+
-dependent histone deacetylase
that regulates various cellular processes, including metabolism, apoptosis
inhibition, mitochondrial biogenesis, antioxidative responses, ferroptosis
resistance, and autophagy induction. Studies
have reported that SIRT1 expression negatively correlates with age.
The downregulation of SIRT1 has been shown to promote vascular senescence
and inflammatory responses, whereas its overexpression confers protective
effects against these pathological processes.

−

Therefore,
SIRT1 is considered a potential protective factor against vascular
aging, and direct modulators of its expression may serve as viable
therapeutic candidates for this condition.

Dietary intervention
is a safe, broad-spectrum, and low-cost strategy
for delaying aging and preventing age-related diseases.

,

The intake of plant polyphenols has been significantly associated
with improved health indices in experimental animal studies and epidemiological
research.

,

Epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), a major
polyphenolic compound in green tea, can be supplemented through diet
and supports cardiovascular health by modulating various pathways.

,

Therefore, elucidating the effects of EGCG on vascular aging and
identifying its molecular targets can considerably delay the progression
of vascular aging-related CVDs. Furthermore, EGCG consumption has
been reported to extend the lifespan of rats. However, the effects of EGCG on SIRT1 expression in vascular cells
and its role and underlying mechanisms in vascular aging remain inadequately
explored.

This study establishes a doxorubicin (DOX)-induced
vascular aging
model to investigate the effects of EGCG on vascular aging and its
specific molecular mechanisms, focusing on SIRT1 and its downstream
pathways related to ferroptosis and autophagy. The findings are expected
to provide theoretical support for the development of EGCG as a potential
intervention against vascular aging.
2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Cell Culture

Human umbilical vein
endothelial cells (HUVECs) were purchased from Saiye (Suzhou) Biotechnology
Co., Ltd. and cultured in endothelial cell medium (ScienCell, San
Diego, CA, USA) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum and 1% endothelial
cell growth supplement at 37 °C with 5% CO
2
. DOX stock
solution (2 mM) was prepared in sterile phosphate-buffered saline
(PBS) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) and stored at −20 °C
for subsequent assays. EGCG and EX527 (a SIRT1-specific inhibitor)
were purchased from Macklin (China). As shown in
Figure

A, the
in vitro
experimental cellular groups were defined as follows: the control
(Ctrl) group served as the untreated control, the DOX group represented
cells treated with 2 μM DOX to establish a senescence model
as described in previous studies, the
EGCG group comprised cells treated with 10–90 μM EGCG,
the EGCG+DOX group included cells pretreated with EGCG for 24 h followed
by 2 μM DOX treatment, and the EGCG+DOX+EX527 group involved
cells pretreated with 50 μM EGCG and 10 μM EX527 for 24
h before 2 μM DOX treatment; the EGCG+DOX+siRNA group and EGCG+DOX+siNC
group underwent SIRT1-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) or scrambled
negative control siRNA (siNC) transfection and subsequent validation
of SIRT1 knockdown efficiency prior to EGCG+DOX treatment. The corresponding
samples were collected for further experiments.

2.2. Animal Modeling and Grouping

15-Month-old
and 9-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were purchased from Beijing Weishang
Lide Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). They were housed under
standard laboratory conditions with unrestricted access to food and
water in a well-ventilated room. The environment was maintained at
25 °C ± 1 °C with a relative humidity of 60% ±
5% and a 12 h light/dark cycle. As shown in
Figure

A, the mice were divided into three groups
(
n
= 6): Young, Aged, and Aged+EGCG. The Young group
consisted of the 9-week-old male C57BL/6 mice, while the 15-month-old
male C57BL/6 mice were randomly grouped into the Aged group or Aged+EGCG
group. EGCG was dissolved in sterile saline and administered through
oral gavage at a dose of 50 mg/kg once daily for 16 weeks. The Young
and Aged groups were given an equivalent volume of sterile saline
by gavage once daily. All animal experimental protocols were conducted
in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals
and approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of the General
Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army (Beijing, China) (approval
no. S2022-522-01).
2.3. Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability
was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Biosharp, China).
The cells were seeded into 96-well plates and treated for 24 h. Following
treatment, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and
the plates were incubated at 37 °C in a CO
2
incubator
for an additional 4 h. Absorbance wavelength at 450 nm was measured
using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M5, Molecular Devices, USA).
2.4. Senescence-Associated β-Galactosidase
Staining

The accumulation of senescence-associated β-galactosidase
(SA-β-gal) was detected using an SA-β-gal staining kit
(C0602, Beyotime, China). After treatment, freshly isolated aortas
without adventitia and HUVECs were immediately fixed, washed thrice
with PBS, and stained with the SA-β-gal staining solution according
to the manufacturer’s instructions. Images of the stained cells
were captured using an inverted microscope (Olympus, Japan). The staining
area (green signal) was quantified using IPWIN60 software (Media Cybernetics,
Inc.).
2.5. SIRT1 Activity Assay

The activity
of SIRT1 was measured using a commercial kit (ab156065, Abcam, USA).
After the treatment was completed, the cell culture supernatants were
collected according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and
the activity of SIRT1 was quantitatively determined following the
manufacturer’s protocol.
2.6. Transfection of siRNA

SIRT1-specific
siRNA (Genechem, Shanghai, China) and scrambled negative control siRNA
(siNC) were transfected into HUVECs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen,
USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells
were seeded in 6-well plates and transfected with 50 nM siRNA when
reaching 70–80% confluence. After 6 h of transfection, the
medium was replaced with fresh complete medium.
2.7. Cellular Reactive Oxygen Species Measurement

The cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h.
The 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA)
probe (Beyotime, China) and dihydroethidium (DHE) probe (Servicebio,
China) were diluted to a final concentration of 10 μM using
serum-free medium at a 1:1000 ratio. After removing the cell culture
medium, the diluted probes were added, and the cells were incubated
in a CO
2
incubator at 37 °C for 30 min. Following
incubation, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed thrice
with PBS. Then, 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining
solution (Servicebio, China) was added, and the cells were incubated
for an additional 30 min. Images were captured using a fluorescence
microscope (Olympus, Japan).
2.8. Cellular Superoxide Dismutase and Malondialdehyde
Detection

The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was measured
using an MDA assay kit (Servicebio, China), and the superoxide dismutase
(SOD) activity was detected using an SOD assay kit (Servicebio, China).
All assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols.
Additionally, MDA and SOD concentrations were calculated by comparing
the samples’ absorbance values to the corresponding standard
curves.
2.9. Cellular Ferrous Iron Detection

The
intracellular ferrous iron levels were measured using a FerroOrange
fluorescence-based kit (Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s
instructions. Cells were stained as per the kit protocol, and images
were captured and analyzed with a fluorescence microscope.
2.10. JC-1 Assay for Mitochondrial Membrane Potential

Mitochondrial membrane potential was detected using a JC-1 assay
kit (Servicebio, China). After corresponding cell treatments, staining
was performed according to the kit instructions, followed by imaging
with a fluorescence microscope.
2.11. BODIPY 581/591 C11 Staining for Lipid Peroxidation

Lipid peroxidation was assessed using a BODIPY 581/591 C11 staining
kit (Beyotime, China). Cells were processed as per the manufacturer’s
protocol, and images were captured via fluorescence microscopy.
2.12. Immunofluorescence Analysis

HUVECs
were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China) and permeabilized
with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Servicebio, China). After blocking with 5%
normal goat serum (Servicebio, China) for 1 h, the cells were incubated
overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-SIRT1 antibody (GB11171–100,
Servicebio, China). The cells were then treated with Cy3-conjugated
goat antirabbit IgG (GB21303, Servicebio, China) at a 1:250 dilution
and incubated at room temperature for 40 min. Nuclei were counterstained
with DAPI (Servicebio, China). After immunofluorescence (IF) staining,
images were captured using a fluorescence microscope.

Mouse
aorta tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, China)
at 4 °C overnight and embedded in optimal cutting temperature
compound (GP1007, Servicebio, China). The embedded tissues were cryosectioned
for staining. The sections were air-dried for 2 h, washed thrice with
PBS, and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Servicebio, China).
Furthermore, they were blocked with 1% bovine serum albumin in PBS
for 15 min. The fixed tissue sections were incubated overnight at
4 °C with rabbit anti-SIRT1 antibody (GB11171-100, Servicebio,
China), followed by incubation with Cy3-conjugated goat antirabbit
IgG (GB21303, Servicebio, China) at a 1:250 dilution for 2 h at room
temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio, China).
Next, images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus,
Japan).
2.13. Histopathological Analysis

Tissues
were dissected from the mouse aorta and fixed in 4% buffered formalin.
Paraffin-embedded blocks were prepared, and 5 μm-thick sections
were cut. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s
trichrome staining were performed following standard protocols. Additionally, six random fields from each section
were selected for microscopic observation. The thickness of the vascular
wall and the percentage of collagen deposition area (blue staining)
were measured using IPWIN60 software (Media Cybernetics, Inc.).
2.14. Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay

The mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium
pentobarbital (50 mg/kg). Blood samples were collected into heparinized
tubes and centrifuged at 3000–4000 rpm for 10 min to obtain
the serum, which was then stored at −80 °C. Additionally,
SOD and MDA levels were quantified using corresponding assay kits
(Servicebio, China). Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6
(IL-6), and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) levels were measured
using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (UpingBio, China).
2.15. Western Blot Analysis

Western blot
analysis was performed as previously described. Total protein samples were extracted from cells using a
protein extraction kit (BL671A, Biosharp, China). Protein concentrations
were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (G2026, Servicebio,
China). The protein samples were adjusted to equal concentrations
with 5× loading buffer (G2034, Servicebio, China) and heated
at 95 °C for 5 min. Equal amounts of protein lysates were separated
using sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis
(10%–12% w/v) according to the molecular weight of the target
proteins. The primary antibodies included P16, P21, P53, Nrf2, HO-1,
P62, Beclin-1, LC3B, and SIRT1 (Abcam, USA) and β-actin (Servicebio,
China). After three washes with Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20,
the membranes were incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature with goat
anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (GB23303, Servicebio, China). Immunoreactive
bands were detected using an automated chemiluminescence/multicolor
fluorescence imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA) and ECL reagent (G2020,
Servicebio, China). In addition, densitometric analysis of the bands
was performed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).
2.16. Pulse Wave Velocity Measurement

The mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane and positioned securely
on a temperature-controlled heating pad to maintain a stable core
body temperature of 37 °C. The aortic arch was located using
the Vevo3000 VisualSonics ultrasound system (VisualSonics Inc., Toronto,
Canada), and blood flow signals from it were recorded.
2.17. Statistical Analyses

Statistical
analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad
Software, USA). Data were evaluated using an unpaired Student’s
t
-test for comparisons between two groups and one-way or
two-way analysis of variance for multiple groups, followed by Tukey’s
post hoc test for comparisons of means among three or more groups.
All experiments were repeated at least thrice, and the results were
expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean.
p
value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
3. Results

3.1. EGCG Alleviates DOX-Induced Senescence in
HUVECs

The CCK-8 assay revealed that EGCG at concentrations
of 10–50 μM did not significantly affect the viability
of HUVECs compared with the Ctrl group, whereas EGCG at 70 and 90
μM significantly inhibited cell viability (
p
< 0.001;
Figure

B). Based on these results, EGCG concentrations of 10, 30, and 50
μM were selected for further investigation of its antisenescence
effects. SA-β-gal staining demonstrated that the SA-β-gal-positive
cell rate was significantly higher in the DOX group compared to that
in the Ctrl group (
p
< 0.001), indicating the
successful establishment of the cellular senescence model. Alternatively,
the SA-β-gal-positive cell rate was significantly reduced in
the DOX+EGCG group compared to that in the DOX group (
p
< 0.05), showing a concentration-dependent decrease (
Figure

C,D). Subsequent
cotreatment of HUVECs with DOX and EGCG at concentrations of 10, 30,
and 50 μM revealed that EGCG improved cell viability in a concentration-dependent
manner (
Figure

E).
Furthermore, Western blot analysis of senescence-associated proteins
supported these findings, with the expression of the senescence markers
P16, P21, and P53 being significantly lower in the DOX+EGCG group
compared to that in the DOX group (
p
< 0.001),
and this reduction was concentration-dependent (
Figure

F–I). Consequently, 50 μM EGCG,
which exhibited the most pronounced effects, was selected for the
subsequent experiments.
3.2. Antisenescence Effect of EGCG on HUVECs Is
Mediated by the Upregulation of SIRT1

As shown in
Figure

A,B, SIRT1 expression
was significantly decreased in the DOX group compared to the Ctrl
group (
p
< 0.001), whereas EGCG treatment in the
EGCG+DOX group markedly increased SIRT1 expression (
p
< 0.001), indicating that EGCG partially reversed DOX-induced
SIRT1 downregulation. Consistent with Western blot results, immunofluorescence
staining confirmed a significant increase in SIRT1 expression in the
EGCG+DOX group (
Figure

C,D). To validate the specificity of SIRT1 modulation, we employed
both pharmacological and genetic approaches. EX527, a well-characterized
and selective SIRT1 inhibitor,

−

was used in combination with siRNA-mediated SIRT1 knockdown. As
shown in
Figure

E,
SIRT1 activity was significantly reduced in the EGCG+DOX+EX527 and
EGCG+DOX+siRNA groups compared to the EGCG+DOX group (
p
< 0.001). Western blot analysis (
Figure

F,G) further revealed decreased SIRT1 protein
levels in these groups (
p
< 0.01), corroborated
by immunofluorescence staining (
Figure

H,J).

3.4. EGCG Inhibits Ferroptosis by Upregulating
SIRT1

We first evaluated reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels
across all experimental groups. As shown in
Figure

A–C, the DOX group exhibited a significant
increase in ROS production compared to the Ctrl group (
p
< 0.001), whereas the DOX+EGCG group showed a marked reduction
in ROS levels relative to the DOX group (
p
< 0.001).
Ferrous iron staining (
Figure

D,E) revealed that intracellular ferrous iron levels were
significantly elevated in the DOX group compared to Ctrl (
p
< 0.001), an effect that was reversed by EGCG treatment
(
p
< 0.001). JC-1 staining (
Figure

F,G) indicated a significant decline in mitochondrial
membrane potential in the DOX group (
p
< 0.001),
which was notably restored in the DOX+EGCG group (
p
< 0.001). BODIPY staining (
Figure

H,I) demonstrated that DOX treatment increased lipid
peroxidation levels (
p
< 0.001), whereas EGCG
cotreatment significantly mitigated this effect (
p
< 0.001). Additionally, SOD activity and MDA content assays (
Figure

J,K) showed that
DOX treatment decreased SOD activity and increased MDA levels (
p
< 0.01), effects that were reversed by EGCG (
p
< 0.01).

4. Discussion

Vascular aging is a key
contributor to CVDs

,

as it exacerbates structural
and functional vulnerability.

,

Herein, we found that
EGCG alleviates vascular aging by upregulating
SIRT1 expression in endothelial cells, inhibiting ferroptosis by promoting
Nrf2 and its downstream proteins, such as HO-1, FTH1, and GPX4, and
enhancing autophagy by increasing LC3I and LC3II expression levels.

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of cell death, primarily
mediated by the action of ferrous iron or lipoxygenases.

,

Key features of ferroptosis include lipid peroxidation and iron
accumulation, resulting in decreased GPX4 activity and increased ROS
levels, culminating in cell death.

,

Ferroptosis
accelerates vascular aging, and targeting ferroptotic signaling in
age-related CVDs is a crucial strategy to counteract this process.

,

SIRT1 plays a crucial role in regulating ferroptosis by activating
Nrf2. Upon stimulation, SIRT1 activates Nrf2, which subsequently translocates
to the nucleus and promotes the expression of antioxidant proteins
such as GPX4, HO-1, and FTH1, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis.

−

Herein, we observed that in DOX-induced senescent HUVECs, the levels
of ferrous iron, lipid peroxidation, and ROS significantly increased,
while the expression levels of ferroptosis-related proteins such as
FTH1, GPX4, Nrf2, and HO-1 markedly decreased. These findings indicate
the occurrence of ferroptosis in senescent vascular endothelial cells,
characterized by increased lipid peroxidation and iron accumulation,
consistent with the key features of ferroptosis described earlier.
Upon EGCG treatment, ferrous iron, lipid peroxidation, and ROS levels
significantly decreased, and the expression levels of FTH1, GPX4,
Nrf2, and HO-1 were significantly restored, suggesting the inhibition
of ferroptosis. Moreover, some studies have reported a close association
between ferroptosis and cellular senescence, highlighting that inhibiting
ferroptosis can ameliorate aging. Our
findings indicate a close link between the antisenescence effects
of EGCG on vascular endothelial cells and its ability to inhibit ferroptosis.

In addition to ferroptosis, the reduction of autophagy plays a
critical role in the progression of vascular aging. A decline in autophagy
accelerates vascular senescence, whereas enhanced autophagy slows
this process. Herein, we observed a significant
decrease in autophagy levels in DOX-induced senescent HUVECs, while
EGCG treatment significantly restored these cells’ autophagy
levels. Numerous studies have shown that EGCG promotes autophagy by
protecting vascular endothelial cells from oxidative stress, reducing lipid accumulation in endothelial cells, and other mechanisms through autophagy activation.
Our study also supports this conclusion, demonstrating that EGCG promotes
autophagy in the regulation of vascular aging. Consistent with our
findings, EGCG enhances autophagy levels in adipose and intestinal
tissues of aging mice.

SIRTs are
a family of proteins with NAD
+
-dependent deacetylase
or ADP-ribosyltransferase activity. These
proteins regulate various cellular functions and stress responses
and are implicated in preventing age-related diseases, including CVDs,
diabetes, and cancer. SIRT1 is highly
expressed in endothelial cells and plays a critical role in angiogenesis
and the maintenance of vascular function. It is highly sensitive to the cellular redox state, and its expression
is directly associated with the maintenance of endothelial function
and aging. A reduction in SIRT1 expression leads to increased P53
acetylation, resulting in a stress-induced premature senescence-like
phenotype in endothelial cells. Conversely,
SIRT1 overexpression reverses oxidative stress-induced premature senescence,
underscoring the direct involvement of SIRT1 in endothelial aging.

,

Compared with the findings of other studies, our findings align
with those of previous research that highlights the role of SIRT1
in mitigating oxidative stress by reducing ROS production and activating
antioxidant defenses, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis. In addition to regulating ferroptosis, SIRT1 can directly
modulate autophagy by deacetylating key components of autophagy-related
proteins.

,

Our findings also support this view. Herein,
we found that the protective effects of EGCG on DOX-induced senescent
HUVECs involved increased SIRT1 expression, ferroptosis inhibition,
and autophagy promotion. When SIRT1 expression was specifically blocked,
the antiaging effects of EGCG were reversed, along with its anti-ferroptosis
and pro-autophagy activities. These findings are consistent with those
of previous reports that SIRT1 activation can counteract endothelial
aging and suggest that EGCG exerts its protective effects through
a similar mechanism. Our research offers an integrated view by showing
that SIRT1 regulates both ferroptosis and autophagy, unlike some studies
that focus on just one of these processes. This dual regulatory role
of SIRT1 underscores its importance in maintaining endothelial cell
homeostasis and delaying vascular aging.

This study has several
limitations. First, it focused only on endothelial
cells
in vitro
, without addressing vascular smooth
muscle cells. Second, the
in vivo
experiments were
conducted exclusively on mice, which may not fully represent the conditions
in humans. Lastly, our research was limited to investigating the roles
of SIRT1, ferroptosis, and autophagy. Future studies should explore
other mechanisms by which EGCG may counteract vascular aging to gain
a more comprehensive understanding of its therapeutic potential.
5. Conclusion

Our study indicates that
EGCG reduces aging in endothelial cells
and the aorta of mice, potentially through mechanisms such as SIRT1,
ferroptosis, and autophagy. EGCG demonstrates significant potential
in mitigating vascular aging and may serve as a novel intervention
for preventing and treating associated diseases, offering innovative
strategies and perspectives for clinical therapies.
