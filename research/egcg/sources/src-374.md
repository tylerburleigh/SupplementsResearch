This cohort study investigates if genetic predisposition to hypertension is associated with cardiovascular disease risk beyond measured blood pressure.
Key Points

Question

Does genetic predisposition to hypertension inform cardiovascular disease risk beyond measured blood pressure?
Findings

In a UK Biobank study of 331 078 participants, genetically predicted blood pressure was associated with cardiovascular risk in addition to measured blood pressure and antihypertensive medication prescription. Each SD increase in genetically predicted systolic blood pressure was independently associated with excess cardiovascular disease risk by 1.13-fold in normal blood pressure, 1.04-fold in untreated hypertension, and 1.06-fold in treated hypertension groups.
Meaning

Blood pressure polygenic risk scores may complement measured blood pressure to achieve earlier and targeted cardiovascular disease prevention.
Abstract

Importance

Hypertension remains the major cardiovascular disease risk factor globally, but variability in measured blood pressure may result in suboptimal management. Whether genetic contributors to elevated blood pressure may complementarily inform cardiovascular disease risk assessment is unknown.
Objective

To examine incident cardiovascular disease by blood pressure polygenic risk score independent of measured blood pressures and antihypertensive medication prescriptions.
Design, Setting, and Participants

The cohort study (UK Biobank) recruited UK residents aged 40 to 69 years between March 2006 and August 2010. Participants without a prior physician diagnosis of cardiovascular disease, including myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure, were included. Excluded were individuals with mismatch between self-reported and genotypically inferred sex, sex aneuploidy, missing genotype rates of 1% or greater, and excess genotypic heterozygosity. Data analyses were performed from September 25, 2021, to July 21, 2022.
Exposures

Measured blood pressure and externally derived blood pressure polygenic risk score stratified by hypertension diagnosis and management, which included normal blood pressure (<130/80 mm Hg without antihypertensives), untreated hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥130 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mm Hg without antihypertensives), and treated hypertension (current antihypertensives prescriptions).
Main Outcomes and Measures

Composite of first incident myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, or cardiovascular-related death.
Results

Of the 331 078 study participants included (mean [SD] age at enrollment, 56.9 [8.1] years; 178 824 female [54.0%]), 83 094 (25.1%) had normal blood pressure, 197 597 (59.7%) had untreated hypertension, and 50 387 (15.2%) had treated hypertension. Over a median (IQR) follow-up of 11.1 (10.4-11.8) years, the primary outcome occurred in 15 293 participants. Among those with normal blood pressure, untreated hypertension, and treated hypertension, each SD increase in measured systolic blood pressure was associated with hazard ratios of 1.08 (95% CI, 0.93-1.25), 1.20 (95% CI, 1.16-1.23), and 1.16 (95% CI, 1.11-1.20), respectively, for the primary outcome. Among these same categories, each SD increase in genetically predicted systolic blood pressure was associated with increased hazard ratios of 1.13 (95% CI, 1.05-1.20), 1.04 (95% CI, 1.01-1.07), and 1.06 (95% CI, 1.02-1.10), respectively, for the primary outcome independent of measured blood pressures and other covariates. Findings were similar for measured and genetically predicted diastolic blood pressure.
Conclusions and Relevance

Blood pressure polygenic risk score may augment identification of individuals at heightened cardiovascular risk, including those with both normal blood pressure and hypertension. Whether it may also guide antihypertensive initiation or intensification requires further study.
Introduction

In the past decade, lifestyle modification and pharmacologic advances have substantially improved the management of hypertension.
1
Nevertheless, hypertension remains the leading cardiovascular (CVD) risk factor globally,
1
accounting for half of its population attributable risk worldwide.
2

Blood pressure (BP) is a complex trait related to socioeconomic, environmental, clinical, genetic, and contextual factors with marked intraindividual variability.
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
BP measures for a given individual commonly vary by technique, equipment, and assessor; even with repeat measurement within the same outpatient practice, variability persists.
7
Patients often also have distinct BP measures outside of the office. Sustained BP elevations are strongly predictive of heightened CVD risk. Nevertheless, such unique measurement variability poses important clinical challenges for optimal management.
8

Recent studies indicate that CVD-associated genetic alleles are associated with CVD with a larger magnitude than explained by corresponding measured risk factors.
9
,
10
For example, blood cholesterol–associated genetic alleles are associated with greater CVD effects than anticipated by laboratory measures alone.
11
,
12
,
13
BP-associated genetic alleles predict early-onset hypertension as well as incident CVD.
14
However, the extent to which they are clinically informative beyond measured BP and recognized hypertension status is not well understood. Here, we examined whether BP polygenic risk score (PRS) is associated with CVD independent of measured BPs and antihypertensive medication in middle-aged adults without prior CVD.
Methods

Data Source and Study Population

The UK Biobank study has approval from the North West Multicenter Research Ethics Committee. UK Biobank data are available to researchers by application (
https://www.ukbiobank.ac.uk/
). Secondary use of data under UK Biobank application 7089 was approved by the Massachusetts General Hospital institutional review board. The UK Biobank is a prospective cohort study of approximately 500 000 individuals aged 40 to 69 years at baseline recruited from the general UK population. Between March 2006 and August 2010, participants underwent physical examination; provided blood, urine, and saliva samples; and completed questionnaires on sociodemographics, lifestyle, and health history. Health care utilization was collected through linkage to electronic health-related records.
15

For the present analysis, we considered 494 504 participants without a prior physician diagnosis of CVD, including myocardial infarction, stroke, or heart failure. We excluded 1451 participants with mismatch between self-reported and genotypically inferred sex, sex aneuploidy, missing genotype rates of 1% or greater, and excess genotypic heterozygosity. We also excluded 65 665 related individuals (kinship index >0.088) using the KING software (King Software Systems).
16
Lastly, 96 310 participants with incomplete measurements of key covariates were excluded. All participants provided electronically signed informed consent, and the present study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (
STROBE
) reporting guidelines.
Construction of PRS

Details on central quality control and imputation of genotypic data have been previously described.
15
Briefly, genotypic data were obtained using either UK Biobank Axiom or UK BiLEVE Axiom arrays (Affymetrix Research Service Laboratory). The Haplotype Reference Consortium and the merged UK10K+1000 Genomes were used as reference panels for imputation with preference for the Haplotype Reference Consortium panel, where single nucleotide variations (SNVs) were present in both panels. To identify population structure of the UK Biobank cohort, principal component analysis was performed using fastPCA
17
based on a pruned set of 147 604 SNVs among unrelated individuals.
18

PRSs for BPs were derived from genome-wide association study results of the Million Veterans Program (91.5% male sex, 69.1% non-Hispanic White participants) based on 1 120 696 variants.
19
Genotyping array design and data quality control in the Million Veteran Program have previously been described.
20
For primary analysis, we applied the Bayesian regression-based PRS with continuous shrinkage priors (PRS-CS) framework,
21
which places the continuous shrinkage priors on SNV effect sizes, thus allowing for marker-specific adaptive shrinkage. PRS-CS accommodates varied underlying genetic architectures, is computationally efficient, and is flexible in multivariable modeling of local linkage disequilibrium patterns.
21
We validated the prediction accuracy of global shrinkage parameter of 1, 1 × 10
−2
, 1 × 10
−4
, and 1 × 10
−6
for systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP), separately, in an independent Mass General Brigham Biobank data set.
22
Markov chain Monte Carlo iteration was set to 1000, burn-in iteration to 500, and thinning factor to 5. Linkage disequilibrium reference panels were constructed using the European 1000 Genomes Project phase 3 samples. Posterior SNV effect size estimates for each global shrinkage parameter were calculated for each chromosome. After concatenation, individual-level SBP and DBP PRSs were generated using the sum modifier using PLINK version 1.9. The global shrinkage parameter of 1 × 10
−2
was chosen for both SBP and DBP PRSs based on the highest
R
2
.

For sensitivity analysis, PRSs were calculated based on linkage disequilibrium-informed pruning and
P
value thresholding method using a reference panel of 503 European samples from 1000 Genomes phase 3, version 5.
23
For each BP trait, genetic variants associated with BP at genome-wide significance
P
< 5 × 10
−8
were identified based on the aforementioned discovery data set.
19
We summed the number of alleles after multiplying with the discovery effect size between the SNV and BP. If a locus contained multiple correlated variants, independent SNVs were selected based on the highest reported
P
value based on the linkage disequilibrium clumping procedure at
R
2
less than 0.01 using PLINK, version 1.9.
24
,
25
BP Measurement and Antihypertensive Medication

After 5 minutes of seated rest, BP was measured on 2 consecutive occasions with a 1-minute interval using Omron 705 IT electronic BP monitor (OMRON Healthcare); the mean of the first and the second automated readings was adopted for the data analysis whenever available.
26
Antihypertensive medications were classified into calcium channel blockers, β-blockers, angiotensin receptor blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, potassium-sparing diuretics, and thiazide-related diuretics. The number of antihypertensive agents prescribed was tabulated.

Based on the measured BP levels and antihypertensive intake, participants were further stratified into normal BP, untreated hypertension, and treated hypertension groups. For the primary analysis, hypertension was defined as SBP of 130 mm Hg or greater, DBP of 80 mm Hg or greater, or prescription of an antihypertensive medication in accordance with the 2017 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association classification.
27
For sensitivity analysis, hypertension was defined as SBP of 140 mm Hg or greater, DBP of 90 mm Hg or greater, or prescription of an antihypertensive medication.
28
Demographics and Behavioral and Clinical Risk Covariates

eTable 1 in the
Supplement
summarizes Unique Data Identifier of key risk factors in the UK Biobank. Racial and ethnic background was self-identified from fixed categories of African, African (Black and White), Asian, Bangladeshi, British (White), Caribbean, Caribbean (Black and White), Chinese, Indian, Irish, Pakistani, White and Asian, White and Black African, White and Black Caribbean, other Asian, other Black, other White, other multiracial, or other/unknown. Single-inverse normalized Townsend Deprivation Index
29
was assigned based on automobile ownership, household overcrowding, household owner occupation, and employment.

Cigarette smoking and frequency of moderate to vigorous physical activity were self-reported. Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Blood biochemistry, including total, low-density, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterols, triglycerides, and glucose, were assayed within 24 hours of sample collection. Diabetes was defined as glycated hemoglobin A
1c
level of 6.5% or greater (to convert to proportion of total hemoglobin, multiply by 0.01) or prior physician diagnosis.
Outcomes

The primary outcome was a first composite CVD event, defined as myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, or CVD-related death, ascertained by linkage to Hospital Episode Statistics data. Disease outcome definitions were based on the
International Classification of Diseases, Ninth
and
Tenth Revision (ICD-10)
(eTable 2 in the
Supplement
). CVD-related death was defined as mortality with
ICD-10
codes of I00 to I99 as either primary or secondary cause of death. If a participant had more than 1 event during follow-up, the first event was counted as satisfying the outcome. In secondary analyses, we separately assessed the first event of each component of the primary outcome.
Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were reported as mean (SD), median (IQR), or count (percentage). We tested the associations of SBP and DBP PRS within each of the hypertension groupings (ie, normal BP, untreated hypertension, and treated hypertension) with measured SBP and DBP as continuous traits using multivariable linear regression models. Analyses were adjusted for age,
2
sex, self-reported race and ethnicity, and the first 10 principal components of ancestry. Next, we assessed the continuous association of each SD increase in SBP PRS increase with first CVD event, adjusting for sex, age, race and ethnicity, body mass index, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, diabetes, statin prescription, smoking, physical activity, Townsend Deprivation Index, genotyping batch, the first 10 principal components, measured BPs, and number of antihypertensive medication classes. We also calculated predicted (1) odds ratios (ORs) and (2) probabilities of CVD event by age 75 years for each BP PRS percentile with the median PRS of untreated hypertension group as the reference. To quantify the SBP PRS percentile cutoff for normal BP where average predicted CVD probability does not significantly differ from that of untreated hypertension, an analysis of variance test was performed. The BP PRS percentile cutoff of normal BP group that did not significantly differ from the average risk of untreated hypertension group was determined by the
F
statistic. Separately, the same approach was applied to calculate PRS percentile of untreated hypertension that overlapped with the average risk of treated hypertension.

Additionally, each BP PRS was categorized into 5 bins of less than 2.5, 2.5 to less than 20, 20 to less than 80, 80 to less than 97.5, and 97.5 or greater percentiles. The incidence rates of CVD events were calculated for each PRS by hypertension group. The cumulative incidence rates of CVD events were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for CVD events associated with each PRS by hypertension group were calculated using Cox proportional hazards models. We confirmed the proportional hazards assumptions by visual inspection of log-minus-log plot and Schoenfeld residuals. The end of the observation period was defined as the date of first CVD event, last follow-up, or July 22, 2021, whichever came first.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at
P
< .05. All analyses were performed from September 25, 2021, to July 21, 2022, using R, version 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).
Results

Baseline Characteristics

The study included 331 078 participants (mean [SD] age, 56.9 [8.1] years; 178 824 female [54.0%]; 152 254 male [46.0%]) without prior CVD at baseline (
Table 1
and eFigure 1 in the
Supplement
). Participants from the following race and ethnicity categories were included: 7137 Black (2.2%), 1118 Chinese (0.3%), 6442 South Asian (2.0%), 312 023 White (94.2%), and 4358 other (including multiracial; 1.3%). The mean (SD) SBP and DBP were 137.7 (18.6) mm Hg and 82.2 (10.1) mm Hg, respectively. ). Excluded participants with missing covariate measurements had similar SBP and modestly lower DBP (included, 82.2 mm Hg vs excluded, 82.1 mm Hg) (eTable 3 in the
Supplement

Discussion

In a large prospective cohort of middle-aged adults, we observed greater associations of BP PRS relative to, and independent of, measured BP for CVD event risk among those with and without hypertension. BP genetic risk identified 1 in 10 with normal measured BP who had comparable CVD risk as those with untreated hypertension; similarly, 1 in 4 with untreated hypertension carried comparable CVD risk as those prescribed antihypertensives.

These observations permit several conclusions regarding BP-associated CVD risk and its management. First, genetic predisposition to elevated BP was associated with independent and generally greater CVD risks compared with single in-office BP measures in middle age. These observations for BP are aligned with genetic and longitudinal analyses of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and smoking, highlighting the accelerated atherogenicity attributed to cumulative exposures of causal atherogenic factors.
30
,
31
,
32
Recent work demonstrated that even modest abnormalities in childhood risk factors, including BP, carry additive risks beyond risk factors ascertained in middle age.
33
Practical challenges remain linking pediatric with adult records, often in distinct health systems as well as inconsistent risk factor ascertainment in childhood. However, we observed a role for BP PRS even among normotensive adults as well. Furthermore, a BP PRS is obtainable at any age with increasingly widespread and available technologies. As a stepwise increase in atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk begins from very low BP levels,
34
our results highlight the synergistic potential of BP PRS to achieve primordial prevention in a normotensive subgroup. Specifically, BP PRS may augment screening frequency and facilitate earlier actionability.

Second, BP PRS may offer an opportunity for primordial CVD prevention. Prior work showed that BP PRS can identify individuals at risk for incident hypertension.
14
Although specific therapies are currently not recommended for normotensive individuals in current guidelines, we observed 1 in 10 individuals carry a BP genetic risk for CVD equivalent to diagnosed hypertension. Furthermore, such heightened risk was not identified by measured BP among normotensive individuals. Given the ability to ascertain a BP PRS early in life, nonpharmacologic strategies previously shown to reduce both the risks for incident hypertension and CVD
5
,
35
,
36
may be more efficiently applied by BP PRS given the low prevalence of abnormal BP early in life.

Third, BP PRS may augment current strategies to time the initiation of antihypertensives as well as to intensify regimens. Current guidelines recommend the use of measured BP and estimated CVD risks for the initiation of risk-modifying therapies.
26
,
27
However, we found that 1 in 4 individuals with hypertension who are not prescribed antihypertensives carry a similar CVD risk as those prescribed antihypertensives from a BP PRS. Given challenges in BP measurement variability posing practical challenges in the initiation of antihypertensives, a BP PRS may help to identify individuals for whom antihypertensives should be considered. Surprisingly, a BP PRS also identified residual CVD risk among those with treated hypertension. Such observations may support the use of BP PRS for more aggressive BP targets among those for whom antihypertensives are warranted. Minimal risk overlap was observed between normotensive and treated hypertensive individuals, indicating that a low BP PRS may not be suitable for deprescribing antihypertensives. Given the differential predictiveness of BP PRS across hypertension and treatment status, considering antihypertensive regimen and adherence may enable PRS to better predict BP traits and to augment the current guideline-directed therapy.

Limitations

Despite the systemic characterization of exposures and outcomes in the present data set, our results should be interpreted in the context of potential limitations. First, we relied on a single-occasion, onsite examination BP measure. Prior literature supports the use of repeated
37
and ambulatory BPs
38
for more comprehensive characterization of cumulative BP burden. Future studies warrant whether BP PRS associates with longitudinal BP patterns and importantly demonstrates incremental prognostic value beyond longitudinal BP in improving cardiovascular risk reclassification. Second, the majority of the UK Biobank participants are of White British ancestry. Importantly, we observed consistent and similar observations among non-White participants albeit with reduced effects. Expanding discovery genome-wide association studies in non-European populations and improving computational algorithms to consider admixed populations may further yield unbiased multiancestry summary statistics toward more equitable clinical utility.
39
Furthermore, current findings may have limited external validity, as UK Biobank participants are known to be older, more likely to be female, to reside in less socioeconomically deprived areas, and to be subjected to healthy volunteer selection bias compared with the general UK population.
40
Third, both misclassification bias and residual confounding are possible owing to a lack of medication adherence data. Lastly, it remains uncertain how best to optimally integrate genomic data into risk stratification and clinical practice.
41
Health care professional and patient education on the implications and limitations of genetic information may facilitate optimal treatment decisions with existing risk factors and lifestyle and minimize potential risks from its disclosure.
41
Conclusions

In conclusion, in this cohort study of UK Biobank participants, middle-aged adults with high BP genetic risks had associated significantly higher risks for subsequent CVD events regardless of measured BP or antihypertensive treatment. A BP PRS was independently associated with CVD risk for those without and with hypertension. Whether incorporation of BP PRS into public health surveillance and clinical care will enable earlier identification and corresponding risk mitigation of patients via BP management requires prospective studies.
