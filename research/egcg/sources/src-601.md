Abstract 1 
Objectives: Tea consumption has been considered beneficial to human health because tea 2 
contains phytochemicals such as polyphenols and theaflavins.  We conducted a systematic 3 
review and meta-analysis on the association between tea consumption and mortality from all  4 
causes, cardiovascular disease ( CVD), and cancer to provide a quantitative assessment of 5 
current evidence. 6 
Methods: The PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus databases were searched t hrough April 7 
2024 to identify eligible studies. Random effects models were used to combine study -specific 8 
effect estimates (ESs).  9 
Results: A total of 38 prospective cohort data sets (from 27 papers) with 1,956,549 participants 10 
were included in this meta-analysis. The pooled ESs of the highest versus lowest categories of 11 
tea consumption were 0.90 (95% CI, 0.86-0.95) for all -cause mortality, 0.86 (95% CI, 0.79-12 
0.94) for CVD mortality, and 0.90 (95% CI, 0.78-1.03) for  cancer mortality. In the dose-13 
response analysis, a nonlinear association was observed. The greatest risk reductions were 14 
observed for the consumption of 2 cups/day for all -cause mortality ( ES, 0.91; 95% CI, 0.88-15 
0.94) and 1.5 cups/day for cancer mortality (ES, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.89-0.96), whereas additional 16 
consumption did not show a further reduction in the risk of death. A plateau was observed for 17 
CVD mortality at moderate consumption levels (1.5 –3 cups/day), but a sustained reduction in 18 
mortality risk was observed at higher intake levels. 19 
Conclusions: Moderate tea consumption (e.g., 1.5–2 cups/day) was associated with lower all-20 
cause, CVD, and cancer mortality compared to no tea consumption.  Further well -designed 21 
prospective studies are needed for a definitive conclusion. 22

Introduction 26 
Tea has been one of the most commonly consumed beverages worldwide for centuries [1]. Tea 27 
is a rich source of fla vonoids such as catechins, theaflavins, anthocyanins, and flavonols [2]. 28 
Flavonoids are known for their potential to reduce the risk of many non-communicable diseases, 29 
including cardiovascular disease (CVD) and cancer, due to their anti -inflammatory and 30 
antioxidant properties that reduce oxidative stress [3, 4]. In addition, a meta-analysis involving 31 
386,610 participants showed that high flavonoid intake was associated with a lower risk of 32 
mortality from CVD and all causes [5].  33 
 Because tea is widely consumed globally, its effect on public health can be major even 34 
if its effect is minor on an individual level. Many observational studies have explored the 35 
associations between tea consumption and the risks of all-cause and cause -specific mortality 36 
[6-32]. Some studies found an inverse [7, 17, 21-24, 27, 28, 30] or positive [9, 10] association 37 
between tea consumption and the risk of mortality, whereas others did not find a significant 38 
association [6, 8, 11-16, 18-20, 25, 26, 29, 31, 32]. To date, several meta-analyses have been 39 
conducted to examine the association between tea consumption and risk of mortality, but they 40 
included studies in which all participants had a specific disease  [33, 34] or did not provide 41 
results for cancer mortality [34, 35]. Since then, many large cohort studies have been published, 42 
analyzing data from the UK Biobank (n= 498,158) and Asia Cohort Consortium (n=528,504) , 43 
[27-32]. Recent large cohort studies have indicated that significant associations exist between 44 
tea consumption and mortality [27, 28, 30]. Given the results published to date, we speculated 45 
about the possible association between tea consumption and mortality if a meta -analysis were 46 
conducted on the general population, excluding as many studies as possible that targeted only 47 
patients. 48 
 Therefore, we performed a systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohort 49 
studies to provide the latest evidence on the associations between tea consumption and the risk 50

Material and methods 55 
Data sources and searches 56 
A systematic literature search  was performed using the  PubMed, ISI Web of Science , and 57 
Elsevier Scopus databases to identify eligible studies published in English through April 2024. 58 
We used t he following keywords  in the search : “( tea OR beverage OR beverages) AND 59 
(mortality OR death OR fatal).” A manual search that reviewed the included articles' reference 60 
lists was also conducted to identify additional eligible studies.  This meta -analysis was 61 
registered on PROSPERO (CRD42023400484). 62 
 63 
Study selection 64 
Studies were included in the current meta-analysis according to the following criteria: (1) the 65 
study had a prospective observational design; (2) the exposure of interest was tea consumption; 66 
(3) the outcome of interest was mortality from all causes, CVD, and cancer; (4) the researchers 67 
reported effect estimates (relative risks [RRs] or hazard ratios [HRs]) with the corresponding 68 
95% confidence intervals (CI s) for each tea-intake group. If 2 different papers had been 69 
published on the same cohort  data sets, the study with a more extended follow -up period or 70 
more participants was included in the meta -analysis. If 2 papers following the same cohort 71 
reported deaths from different causes, both were included in the meta-analysis [19, 32]. Studies 72 
in which the participants consisted only of patients with a specific disease were excluded. 73

Data extraction and quality assessment 75

The relevant data were independently extracted by 2 authors (Y .K. and Y .J.) according to the 76 
Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement  77 
[36]. The following information was collected from each paper: surname of first author, year of 78 
publication, cohort name, number of participants and cases , age, sex, geographical region or 79 
country, period of follow-up, categories of tea consumption, the effect estimates (ESs [RRs or 80 
HRs]) and 95% CIs for each category of tea consumption, and adjusted variables.  81 
 Quality assessment of the included studies was performed using the Newcastle-Ottawa 82 
quality assessment scale [37]. We assigned a maximum score of 9 points to each study based 83 
on the following criteria: selection of participants (0-4 points), comparability of cohorts (0-2 84 
points), and ascertainment of outcomes of interest (0-3 points). Studies with total scores of 0-3, 85 
4-6, or 7-9 points were regarded as low, moderate, or high quality , respectively.  Any 86 
discrepancies between authors in data extraction and quality assessment were resolved by 87 
reviewing and discussing the original papers. 88

Statistical analysis 90 
The natural logarithm values of each study ’s ESs (RRs or HRs)  were combined using a 91 
DerSimonian and Laird [38] random effects model , which considers both with in-study and 92 
between-study variability. If a study provided separate ESs by the presence or absence of 93 
disease history, we used the ESs for the latter [32]. The summary ESs were displayed as forest 94 
plots. The Cochran Q test [39] was used to assess heterogeneity among studies, and the I2 95 
statistic [40] was used to quantify inconsistency . Subgroup analyses stratified by sex, 96 
geographic region , follow -up years , and sample size  were conducted to investigate the 97 
variations in ESs among the studies. To assess how much a specific study may influence 98 
inferences, we performed a sensitivity analysis by omitting a single study at a time and deriving 99 
pooled ESs. Publication bias was e valuated with the Begg test [41] and the Egger regression 100

test [42], as well as funnel plot visualizations.  101 
To estimate the study -specific slope lines, a linear dose-response meta-analysis was 102 
conducted using the methods proposed by Greenland and  Longnecker [43-45]. The median 103 
value of tea consumption was assigned to each category based on information presented in the 104 
original paper. When the highest category was open -ended, we regarded it as of the same 105 
magnitude as the adjacent category. Referring to previous papers, we considered 150 mL of tea 106 
consumption to be 1 cup of tea [9, 33]. To investigate a potential nonlinear association between 107 
tea consumption and risk of mortality, we used restricted cubic splines with 4 knots at fixed 108 
percentiles (5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) of aggregated tea consumption. The p-value 109 
for non-linearity was computed by testing the null hypothesis that the regression coefficient of 110 
the second spline is equal to zero  [46]. A 2-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to 111 
indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were carried out  with Stata 17.0 112 
(StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).  113

Results 115 
Study characteristics 116 
A total of  38 prospective cohort data set s (27 papers) including 1,956,549 participants and 117 
218,948 deaths from all causes, 53,234 deaths from CVD, and 56,049 deaths from cancer were 118 
eligible for this meta-analysis [6-32] (Figure 1). Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the 119 
included studies. Among them, 7 papers reported all estimates of mortality from all  causes, 120 
CVD, and cancer [17, 21-23, 25, 26, 30], respectively . Six papers provided estimates of 121 
mortality from all causes and CVD [10, 15, 18, 24, 27, 28], and 1 paper reported estimates of 122 
mortality from all  causes and cancer  [9]. The other papers provided mortality from either all 123 
causes [6, 8, 13, 16, 31, 32], CVD [7, 11, 12, 19, 20] or cancer only [14, 29]. The follow-up 124 
durations varied from 5 years to 23 years , and the mean follow-up time was 12.3 years. The 125

All-cause mortality 132 
Thirty-three prospective cohort data set s (20 papers) involving 1,825,815 participants and 133 
218,948 deaths examined the association between tea consumption and all-cause mortality [6, 134 
7, 9, 10, 13, 15-18, 21-28, 30-32]. The summary ES for all-cause mortality, comparing the 135 
highest and lowest levels of tea consumption, was 0.90 (95% CI, 0.86-0.95), with significant 136 
heterogeneity among studies ( I2=81.6%, p<0.001) (Figure 2). The sensitivity analysis results, 137 
excluding 1 study at a time, showed that the pooled ESs ranged from 0.89 (95% CI, 0.85-0.94) 138 
to 0.92 (95% CI, 0.88-0.96) (Supplementary Figure 1). The inverse association between tea 139 
consumption and the risk of all-cause mortality was slightly stronger in men (ES, 0.87; 95% CI, 140 
0.77-0.98) than in women (ES, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.79-1.01), but the difference was not statistically 141 
significant (p for difference = 0.97)  (Table 2). By geographic region, the strongest inverse 142 
association between tea consumption and all-cause mortality was shown in Asia (ES, 0.84; 95% 143 
CI, 0.77-0.91), while an insignificant positive association was observed in Europe  (ES, 1.12; 144 
95% CI, 0.88-1.42) (p for difference = 0.05). We did not observe significant variation according 145 
to follow-up times (p for difference = 0.17) or number of participants (p for difference = 0.87). 146 
We found a nonlinear association between tea consumption and all -cause mortality ( p for 147 
nonlinearity <0.0001) (Figure 3). A significant reduction in all -cause mortality was observed 148 
with up to 2 cups/day of tea consumption , and additional consumption did no t show a further 149 
decrease in mortality. 150

CVD mortality 152 
Thirty-one prospective cohort data sets (18 papers) involving 1,820,381 participants and 53,234 153 
deaths examined the association between tea consumption and CVD mortality [7, 10-12, 15, 154 
17-28, 30]. The summary ES for CVD mortality, comparing the highest and lowest levels of tea 155 
consumption, was 0.86 (95% CI, 0.79-0.94), showing significant heterogeneity among studies 156 
(I2=74.6%, p<0.001) (Figure 4). When the pooled ES was estimated after omitting 1 study at a 157 
time, the ESs ranged from 0.85 (95% CI, 0.78-0.92) to 0.88 (95%  CI, 0.82-0.95) 158 
(Supplementary Figure 2). The inverse association between tea consumption and CVD 159 
mortality was similar in men ( ES, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.71-0.99) and women ( ES, 0.86; 95% CI, 160 
0.75-0.99). By geographic region, the largest reduction in CVD mortality was found in Asia 161 
(ES, 0.75; 95% CI, 0.65-0.88), but the difference was not statistically significant ( p for all 162 
comparisons > 0.1) (Table 2). Some evidence for a nonlinear association was found between 163 
tea consumption  and CVD mortality (p for nonlinearity = 0.0001) ( Figure 5). Despite the 164 
significant nonlinearity showing an identical degree of risk reduction for 1.5‒3 cups per day, 165 
the ES decreased by 27% with up to 8 cups of tea consumption per day.  166

Twenty-two prospective cohort data sets  (11 papers) involving 1 ,439,658 participants and 169 
56,049 deaths examined the association between tea consumption and cancer mortality [9, 13, 170 
14, 17, 21-23, 26, 29, 30]. The summary ES for cancer mortality, comparing the highest and 171 
lowest levels of tea consumption, was 0.90 (95% CI, 0.78-1.03), with significant heterogeneity 172 
among studies (I 2=78.8%, p<0.001) (Figure 6). When we calculated the pooled ES after 173 
excluding 1 study at a time, the ESs ranged from 0.87 (95% CI, 0.75-1.00) to 0.94 (95% CI, 174 
0.82-1.07) (Supplementary Figure 3). We found no significant difference by sex ( p for 175

Discussion 187 
The current systematic review and meta -analysis of 38 prospective cohort data sets (from 27 188 
papers) with 1,956,549 total participants found inverse associations between tea consumption 189 
and all-cause and CVD mortality. People with the highest level of tea consumption had a 10% 190 
lower risk of all -cause mortality and a 14% lower risk of CVD mortality than those with the 191 
lowest consumption level. The results from  the dose-response analyses showed a nonlinear 192 
association between tea consumption and mortality from all causes, CVD, and cancer. The most 193 
significant reduction in all-cause mortality was observed at 2 cups/day of tea consumption, and 194 
further intake did not appear to further lower the risk of death. A plateau in CVD mortality was 195 
observed at moderate intake levels (1.5‒3 cups/day of tea consumption), but a continued decline 196 
in mortality risk was observed at higher intake levels.  For cancer mortality, the greatest 197 
reduction in mortality risk was observed at the level of consuming 1.5 cups of tea per day, and 198 
additional intake did not lower mortality. 199 
Many previous observational studies have reported the association between tea 200

drinking and the risk of chronic diseases. A recent meta -analysis of 19 prospective cohort 201 
studies, with a total of 1,076,300 participants, found that people consuming 4 or more cups of 202 
tea per day had a 17% lower risk of type 2 diabetes than people who did not drink tea [47]. For 203 
CVD risk, a meta -analysis of 7 prospective cohort studies involving 9,211 cases of coronary 204 
heart disease among 772,922 participants indicated that green tea consumers had less risk of 205 
coronary heart disease than those who did not consume green tea. The risk was 11% lower for 206 
1 cup/day, 16% lower for 2 cups/day, 15% lower for 3 cups/day, and 12% lower for 4 cups/day 207 
[48]. Furthermore, a meta -analysis of 5 prospective cohort studies, including 11,421 stroke 208 
cases among 645,393 participants, reported that 900  mL/day of green tea consumption was 209 
associated with a 23% reduced risk of stroke [49]. Regarding risk of cancer, meta-analyses of 210 
prospective cohort studies showed that high tea consumption was inversely associated with 211 
several types of cancer, including glioma and ovarian, liver, bladder, and lymphoid cancer [50-212 
52]. The lower risk of mortality with tea consumption observed in the current study may be due 213 
to the inverse association between tea consumption and the risk s of many chronic diseases 214 
reported in previous studies.   215 
A previous meta-analysis reported 26% lower and 24% lower risks of cardiac death and 216 
all-cause death , respectively, per 3 -cup increment of tea consumption [34]. Another meta-217 
analysis published in 2015 found that green tea consumption was inversely associated with 218 
mortality from CVD and all  causes, and black tea consumption was inversely associated with 219 
mortality from cancer and all causes [33]. However, in that meta-analysis, when classifying the 220 
types of tea, the types of tea were estimated and analyzed according to the region where the 221 
study was conducted [33]. A recent meta-analysis from 2020 observed that each 1-cup increase 222 
in daily tea intake was associated with 4% and 2% lower risks of mortality from CVD and all 223 
causes, respectively [35]. Previous results and the results of our study are consistent in that tea 224 
consumption was inversely associated with the risk of CVD and all -cause mortality, and our 225

10 
findings indicated that consuming 1.5‒2 cups of tea daily was associated with lower mortality 226 
rates from CVD, cancer, and all causes. 227 
 In the subgroup analysis by geographical region, the inverse association between tea 228 
consumption and risk of mortality from all  causes was stronger in Asia than in Europe. These 229 
differences observed across regions may be due to regional differences in the types of tea that 230 
are widely consumed. Among the studies included in this meta-analysis, those that reported the 231 
type of tea were mostly from Asia, and it was primarily green tea. Although studies conducted 232 
in Europe did not report the type of tea, it is generally known that people in Europe and North 233 
America mainly consume black tea  [1, 53]. Green tea and black tea go through different 234 
manufacturing processes, and their composition varies accordingly. The most abundant 235 
polyphenols in green tea are catechins such as epicatechin, epicatechin -3-gallate, 236 
epigallocatechin, and epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG). Meanwhile, the major polyphenolic 237 
components of black tea are theaflavin and thearubigin [53]. These differences in the 238 
composition of green tea and black tea may have influenced the difference in their associations 239 
with mortality risk. Most of the studies included in this meta -analysis did not report the ES by 240 
type of tea. The single study that reported ES by tea types found a significant inverse association 241 
between green tea consumption and mortality from all causes and CVD [27]. Specifically, 242 
drinking green tea 3 or more times a week was associated with a 21% lower risk of mortality 243 
from all causes and a 22% lower risk of mortality from CVD than drinking it fewer than 3 times 244 
a week. The researchers did not find a significant association between black tea consumption 245 
and mortality risk. It appears that more large -scale cohort studies need to be conducted in the 246 
future to determine whether mortality risk varies depending on the type of tea.  Another 247 
explanation for the differences by geographical region in the association between tea 248 
consumption and mortality may be differences in how tea  is consumed. In Europe, especially 249 
in the UK, it is common to add milk to tea [9]. Proteins in milk can combine with polyphenols 250

in tea  [54], reducing the polyphenols’ antioxidant capacity by inhibiting absorption and 251 
decreasing their bioavailability [55]. An experimental study found that the ferric iron-reducing 252 
antioxidant power value, an indicator of antioxidant activity, decreased as milk was added to 253 
tea [56]. The reduced antioxidant capacity of flavonoids after binding to proteins such as casein 254 
in milk may have attenuated the association between tea consumption and mortality.  To 255 
understand more clearly the effect s of adding milk to tea on the association between tea 256 
consumption and mortality, future prospective cohort studies that include information on how 257 
tea is consumed would need to be performed. 258 
  Because of the antioxidant properties of the polyphenols contained in tea, drinking tea 259 
has generally been considered a healthy dietary habit [57]. Antioxidative ability is an important 260 
factor in preventing disease and lowering the risk of premature death because oxidative stress 261 
is known to cause chronic disease by damaging normal cells and DNA. Tea flavonoids such as 262 
catechins or theaflavins have been found to decrease the oxidation of low-density lipoproteins, 263 
which cause atherosclerosis and increase the risk of CVD [58]. In addition, epigallocatechin-3-264 
gallate (EGCG) has been shown to have preventive effects against cancer by inhibiting the 265 
activity of urokinase, which invades cells and forms metastases in human cancers  [59], or by 266 
inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest specific to cancer cells [60]. 267 
 This study has offered a comprehensive meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies to 268 
examine the associations between tea consumption and the risk s of mortality from all causes, 269 
CVD, and cancer. Prospective cohort studies have relatively less recall or selection bias than 270 
other observational study designs. The present meta-analysis included a large number of people 271 
(1,956,549 total participants), including recently published large cohorts, and thus, the results 272 
could have considerabl y more statistical power. To identify the appropriate tea consumption 273 
level for minimizing mortality risk, w e performed a dose -response analysis that could show 274 
changes in ESs as tea intake increased. Additionally, unlike previous meta -analyses on the 275

association between tea consumption and mortality, we excluded studies where all participants 276 
were patients suff ering from a specific disease to examine the effect of tea consumption on 277 
mortality risk in the general population. Despite these strengths, several limitations should be 278 
mentioned as well. First, in most of the included studies, the data on tea consumption were 279 
assessed through a food frequency questionnaire , which allowed  the possibility of 280 
misclassification. Fortunately, such misclassification of dietary intake tends to lead to a null  281 
result, so it is unlikely that the associations between tea consumption and mortality risk were 282 
exaggerated. Second, the volume of tea consumption was usually reported in cups, but cup size 283 
may differ from study to study. Third, the chemical composition of each cup of tea, such as its 284 
polyphenols, could vary by season, climate, and plant age, which may affect the association s 285 
between tea consumption and the risk of mortality [53]. Finally, the adjustment factors differed 286 
across the studies, which might  have affected the pooled estimates in the meta -analysis. Most 287 
included studies controlled for potential confounders including smoking, alcohol consumption, 288 
and BMI, but the possibility of additional confounding factors cannot be completely ruled out. 289 
 In conclusion, t he current systematic review and meta -analysis of prospective cohort 290 
studies suggested that moderate daily tea consumption (1.5 ‒2 cups/day) was associated with 291 
lower risks of mortality from all causes, CVD, and cancer in the general population.  Future 292 
well-designed, large-scale prospective cohort studies analyzing different types of tea are needed 293 
to develop dietary guidelines for tea consumption to reduce the risk of chronic disease and 294 
premature death. 295
