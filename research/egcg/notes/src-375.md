# src-375: USP Comprehensive Review of Green Tea Extract Hepatotoxicity

**Citation:** Oketch-Rabah HA, Roe AL, Rider CV, Bonkovsky HL, et al. "United States Pharmacopeia (USP) comprehensive review of the hepatotoxicity of green tea extracts." *Toxicology Reports* 7:386-402 (2020). DOI: 10.1016/j.toxrep.2020.02.008. PMCID: PMC7044683.

**Type:** Systematic review / regulatory assessment (peer-reviewed, open access)

**Quality:** High -- comprehensive review by USP Expert Panel including DILIN experts, FDA liaisons, and academic hepatologists. Large expert panel (20+ authors) with formal causality assessment methodology.

## Core Findings

This is the definitive USP-sponsored comprehensive review of green tea extract (GTE) hepatotoxicity, conducted by the USP Green Tea Hepatotoxicity Expert Panel (2015-2020 cycle) with Drug-Induced Liver Injury Network (DILIN) experts performing formal causality assessment on 75 published case reports. The review concluded that GTE can cause severe hepatotoxicity, with liver injury associated with EGCG intake from 140 mg to ~1000 mg/day, and that no safe minimum dose can be established due to idiosyncratic/genetic susceptibility. USP mandated a cautionary labeling statement for its Powdered Decaffeinated Green Tea Extract monograph effective March 2019.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Case Reports and Causality Assessment
- 75 individual cases from 51 published reports identified
- 35 cases involved single-ingredient dietary supplements (SIDS); causality assessed on these
- DILIN scoring: 4 definite (>95%), 11 highly likely (75-94%), 14 probable (50-74%) = **29 confirmed cases**
- RUCAM scale independently confirmed 29 cases as probable or highly probable
- 22 of 34 cases occurred in women (reflecting higher supplement use, not necessarily greater susceptibility)
- 21 of 35 cases involved GTE for weight loss (likely fasting conditions)
- GTE intake in cases ranged from 500-3000 mg/day (approximately 250-1800 mg EGCG daily); median intake ~720 mg/day (~623 mg EGCG)
- Most cases manifested within 3 months; latency ranged 10 days to 7 months

### Minnesota Green Tea Trial (MGTT) -- Strongest Clinical Evidence
- RCT in 1021 postmenopausal women (513 GTE, 508 placebo)
- 800 mg EGCG/day for 12 months
- ALT increased by 5.4 U/L (95% CI 3.6-7.1), AST by 3.8 U/L (95% CI 2.5-5.1) vs placebo (both P<0.001)
- **Odds ratio for liver function abnormalities: 7.0 (P=0.0002; 95% CI 2.4-20.3)**
- 5.1% of treated subjects showed moderate or severe liver function test abnormalities
- Challenge-dechallenge-rechallenge pattern provided compelling causality evidence
- Previously suggested risk factors (COMT genotype, NSAIDs, statins, alcohol) did not appear to increase effects

### Pharmacokinetics / Bioavailability
- Fasting increases EGCG bioavailability ~5-fold (Cmax) and 3.9-fold (AUC, P=0.04) vs fed conditions
- EGCG half-life longer than EGC/EC; EGCG binds to albumin limiting urinary excretion
- Chronic dosing of 800 mg EGCG daily for 4 weeks increased systemic exposure by >60%
- HPMC capsule shells may reduce dissolution, paradoxically lowering systemic exposure
- Chemical interaction of polyphenols with dietary proteins reduces bioavailability

### Non-Clinical Toxicology (Animal Studies)
- Subchronic NOAEL in rats/mice: approximately 200-300 mg/kg EGCG
- **Fasted dog NOAEL: 40 mg/kg EGCG (HED ~22 mg/kg or ~1554 mg for 70-kg person)**
- NTP chronic study: NOAEL for liver effects 48.4 mg/kg EGCG (male mice), 145 mg/kg (female mice)
- Diversity Outbred mice: 16% (43/272) developed severe hepatotoxicity at 50 mg/kg EGCG, showing genetic susceptibility
- PD-1 knockout mice: immune-mediated liver injury from EGCG, supporting idiosyncratic mechanism

### Regulatory Assessments Summarized
- **USP**: Mandated cautionary label on PDGTE monograph (effective March 2019)
- **EFSA (2018)**: EGCG >=800 mg/day increases transaminases; could not identify safe dose (one case at 375 mg)
- **Health Canada (2017)**: Strengthened cautionary risk statement
- **Norway**: >0.4 mg EGCG/kg bw/day as bolus may cause adverse effects (~28 mg/day for 70-kg person)
- **France/Spain**: Banned Exolise (GTE weight-loss product) in 2003 after liver injury cases
- FDA did not allow further fasting-condition human studies with Polyphenon E due to dog mortality

### Contaminants
- Reviewed solvent residues, pesticide residues, toxic elemental impurities, and pyrrolizidine alkaloids
- No evidence that contaminants (rather than EGCG itself) caused observed hepatotoxicity
- Liver injury pattern from GTE is hepatocellular, different from SOS pattern caused by pyrrolizidine alkaloids

## Methodology

- Systematic search of PubMed, Google Scholar, and other databases (June 2008 - September 2017)
- 204 clinical articles and 127 non-clinical articles retrieved
- Studies categorized into 3 tiers by quality: not useful (excluded), limited usefulness (qualitative), useful for risk assessment (quantitative)
- 20 clinical + 8 supportive studies included; 10 sub-chronic + 2 chronic animal studies included
- DILIN expert opinion causality assessment (12 experts in 4 groups of 3) + RUCAM scale used independently
- Causality scored: definite (>95%), highly likely (75-94%), probable (50-74%), possible (25-49%), unlikely (<25%)

## Limitations

- Most case reports lacked complete information on intake amounts, product composition, duration, and medical history
- No controlled dose-response study in humans; dose-response inferred from case reports and animal data
- Cannot establish a minimum safe dose due to idiosyncratic/genetic variability
- Literature search cutoff September 2017; cases published after this date not included
- Product mislabeling and underreporting of adverse events limit prevalence estimates
- Hu et al. estimated ~4.9% hepatotoxicity rate from clinical studies, but accuracy is questionable
- Some panel members had industry ties (Taiyo Kagaku, Herbalife, dietary supplement companies)

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q2 (Safety/hepatotoxicity/regulatory)**: PRIMARY source. This is the most comprehensive regulatory review of GTE hepatotoxicity. Covers case reports, clinical trials, animal toxicology, pharmacokinetics, and regulatory actions worldwide. Essential evidence for dose-response, risk factors, and regulatory assessments.
- **Q4 (Optimal delivery strategy)**: Strong evidence that taking GTE with food significantly reduces hepatotoxicity risk (5-fold lower EGCG bioavailability). Capsule/tablet bolus doses are riskier than tea as a beverage. USP explicitly warns against taking on empty stomach.
- **Q6 (Case against EGCG)**: Supports the case against isolated EGCG supplements. Documents liver injury at commonly consumed doses, genetic susceptibility, and inability to establish safe threshold.
- **Q7 (Whole tea vs isolated EGCG)**: GTEs contain much higher catechin concentrations than brewed tea. The review distinguishes between traditional green tea beverages and concentrated extracts throughout.

## Claims to Verify

1. **"140 mg to ~1000 mg/day" dose range** -- This appears in the abstract and discussion. The low end (140 mg) is notably lower than EFSA's cited case of 375 mg. The exact source for the 140 mg figure within the case reports should be cross-referenced with OSM Table 2.
2. **"ODDS ratio 7.0" in MGTT** -- Verified from the text at lines 1598-1602 with P-value and CI. This is a strong and specific claim.
3. **Norway's 0.4 mg/kg bw/day threshold** -- This translates to only ~28 mg/day for a 70-kg person, which seems remarkably low. Should be verified against the original Norwegian Food Safety Authority source.
4. **"5.1% of treated subjects showed moderate or severe abnormalities"** in MGTT -- This is 26 out of 513, stated at line 1598. Cross-references with MGTT primary publications would be valuable.
