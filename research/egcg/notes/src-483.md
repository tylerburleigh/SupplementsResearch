# src-483: Food Inhibits the Oral Bioavailability of EGCG in Humans

**Authors:** Naumovski N, Blades BL, Roach PD
**Year:** 2015
**Venue:** Antioxidants (Basel), 4(2):373-393
**DOI:** 10.3390/antiox4020373
**PMID:** 26783711
**PMCID:** PMC4665468

## Core Findings

This crossover pharmacokinetic study in 4 healthy volunteers demonstrated that food substantially inhibits the oral bioavailability of pure EGCG (500 mg). Taking EGCG capsules without food after an overnight fast produced AUC values 2.7x higher than with a light breakfast (p=0.044) and 3.9x higher than when EGCG was incorporated in strawberry sorbet (p=0.019). Cmax was 3.6x higher in the fasted state (824 vs 232 ng/mL, p<0.001). The authors conclude EGCG should be taken without food to maximize systemic absorption.

## Key Evidence / Data Points

### Pharmacokinetic Results (Table 5)

| Parameter | Capsules Fasted | Capsules Fed | Strawberry Sorbet |
|---|---|---|---|
| AUC(0-8) (ug/mL/8h) | 173.8 +/- 67.6 | 64.1 +/- 53.7 | 44.5 +/- 22.9 |
| Cmax (ng/mL) | 824.2 +/- 75.1 | 231.8 +/- 134.3 | 218.0 +/- 160.0 |
| Cav (ng/mL) | 382.6 +/- 92.5 | 92.0 +/- 46.0 | 87.6 +/- 43.0 |
| Cmin (ng/mL) | 86.9 +/- 75.2 | 49.3 +/- 24.6 | 25.0 +/- 5.8 |
| Tmax (min) | 60 +/- 34.6 | 120 +/- 34.6 | 120 +/- 34.6 |
| T1/2 (min) | 78.6 +/- 94.2 | 136.2 +/- 85.2 | 228.6 +/- 97.2 |

- Fasted AUC was 2.7x higher than fed (p=0.044) and 3.9x higher than sorbet (p=0.019)
- Fasted Cmax was significantly higher than both fed conditions (p<0.001)
- Fasted Cav was significantly higher than both fed conditions (p=0.004 and p=0.002)
- No significant difference between fed capsules and sorbet for AUC, Cmax, or Cav (p=1.000)
- Tmax delayed from 60 min (fasted) to 120 min (both fed conditions) -- significantly different (p<0.001)
- T1/2 not significantly different between groups (p>0.05)

### Cross-Study Comparison

- Results consistent with Ullmann et al. (2003): similar 400 mg dose fasted gave Cmax 862 ng/mL and Cav 568 ng/mL (comparable to present study)
- Results consistent with Chow et al. (2005): EGCG in green tea extract showed 3.5x higher AUC and 5.7x higher Cmax when taken fasted vs with muffins

## Methodology

- **Design:** 3-way crossover study, each participant served as own control
- **Participants:** n=4 (3 male, 1 female), age 31.25 +/- 9.54 years, BMI ~23.1 kg/m2
- **Dose:** 500 mg pure EGCG (Teavigo, DSM Nutritionals, verified >97% pure by HPLC)
- **Three delivery conditions:**
  1. Two 250 mg capsules with 100 mL water (fasted, 10h overnight fast)
  2. Two 250 mg capsules with breakfast (50g Special K cereal + 200 mL full cream milk)
  3. 500 mg EGCG incorporated into 200g strawberry sorbet
- **Blood collection:** 7 time points (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8h) via IV catheter
- **EGCG quantification:** HPLC-ESI-MS, LOQ 31.25 ng/mL, LOD 15.63 ng/mL, recovery 98% +/- 2%
- **Analysis:** One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc; AUC by linear trapezoidal rule
- **Sorbet EGCG recovery:** 97.4% +/- 1.3%, confirming dose integrity

### Mechanism Discussion

The authors propose three categories of factors reducing EGCG bioavailability with food:
1. **Vehicle effects:** Despite acidic sorbet (pH<4) preserving EGCG during storage (97% recovery), food matrix did not protect EGCG during GI transit
2. **Biological fluid effects:** Pancreatic bicarbonate rapidly neutralizes acidic chyme in the duodenum; EGCG is highly unstable at neutral/alkaline pH (only 1% remaining after 1h in simulated intestinal fluid per Record & Lane 2001)
3. **Physiological responses to food:** Delayed gastric emptying prolongs EGCG transit time and exposure to alkaline intestinal conditions; capsules without food may elicit weaker pancreatic response

### Proposed Solutions

The paper notes that encapsulation strategies (oil-in-water sub-micrometer emulsions, liposomes, protein/polyphenol microparticles) have been proposed to protect EGCG from intestinal degradation, but their effect on in vivo bioavailability remains untested.

## Limitations

- **Very small sample size (n=4):** This is the primary limitation. Despite crossover design minimizing inter-individual variation, n=4 limits generalizability and statistical power.
- Large standard deviations relative to means (e.g., AUC SD 67.6 for mean 173.8 in fasted state)
- Only tested one dose (500 mg); no dose-response data
- Only free (unconjugated) EGCG measured in plasma; metabolites not quantified
- Single food type per condition; cannot identify which specific food components inhibit absorption
- Only healthy young adults; results may not generalize to elderly or diseased populations
- No direct comparison of EGCG from brewed tea vs pure EGCG

## Claims to Verify

- **n=4 sample size** is extremely small for a pharmacokinetic crossover study. While the effect sizes (2.7-3.9x differences) are large enough to reach significance despite n=4, these numbers should be interpreted cautiously. The large SDs (e.g., AUC fasted: 173.8 +/- 67.6) suggest high inter-individual variability.
- **97.4% EGCG recovery from sorbet:** This seems remarkably high given that EGCG is known to be unstable during processing with heating and light exposure. The sorbet was prepared using a cold process method from Hirun & Roach (2011), which may explain this. The extraction was done in duplicate on five different storage containers.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**Q4 (optimal delivery strategy):** Directly relevant. Provides primary evidence that fasting administration of pure EGCG capsules is the optimal oral delivery strategy. Fasted state yields 2.7-3.9x higher bioavailability than fed conditions. Suggests encapsulation technologies (liposomes, emulsions) may be needed to overcome intestinal degradation but remain untested in vivo.

**Q7 (whole tea vs isolated EGCG):** Relevant to comparison. Study uses pure EGCG (Teavigo) rather than tea or tea extract, and shows that the food effect on bioavailability is similar whether EGCG comes from pure form or green tea extract (consistent with Chow et al. 2005 findings using Polyphenon E). EGCG from any matrix (tea, extract, pure capsules) appears to be similarly inhibited by co-ingestion with food. The Cmax of ~824 ng/mL for 500 mg pure EGCG fasted is comparable to 862 ng/mL for 400 mg in the Ullmann study, suggesting pure EGCG bioavailability is similar to that in tea extract.
