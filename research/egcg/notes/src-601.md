# src-601: Tea Consumption and Mortality Meta-Analysis (Kim & Je, 2024)

## Core Findings

This meta-analysis of 38 prospective cohort datasets (27 papers, 1,956,549 participants) found that the highest versus lowest tea consumption was associated with significantly lower all-cause mortality (ES 0.90, 95% CI 0.86-0.95) and CVD mortality (ES 0.86, 95% CI 0.79-0.94), but the association with cancer mortality did not reach statistical significance (ES 0.90, 95% CI 0.78-1.03). Dose-response analyses revealed nonlinear relationships, with the greatest risk reduction at 1.5-2 cups/day for all outcomes, and no additional benefit from higher consumption for all-cause or cancer mortality.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Primary Pooled Estimates (Highest vs Lowest Tea Consumption)

| Outcome | ES (95% CI) | I2 | Deaths |
|---------|-------------|-----|--------|
| All-cause mortality | 0.90 (0.86-0.95) | 81.6% | 218,948 |
| CVD mortality | 0.86 (0.79-0.94) | 74.6% | 53,234 |
| Cancer mortality | 0.90 (0.78-1.03) | 78.8% | 56,049 |

### Dose-Response Findings

- **All-cause mortality**: Greatest reduction at 2 cups/day (ES 0.91, 95% CI 0.88-0.94); additional consumption showed no further reduction (p for nonlinearity <0.0001)
- **CVD mortality**: Plateau at 1.5-3 cups/day, but ES decreased 27% up to 8 cups/day, suggesting sustained benefit at higher doses (p for nonlinearity = 0.0001)
- **Cancer mortality**: Greatest reduction at 1.5 cups/day (ES 0.92, 95% CI 0.89-0.96); significant reduction maintained up to 5.5 cups/day (p for nonlinearity <0.0001)

### Geographic/Tea Type Differences

- **Asia** (primarily green tea): Strongest inverse associations for all-cause mortality (ES 0.84, 95% CI 0.77-0.91) and CVD mortality (ES 0.75, 95% CI 0.65-0.88)
- **Europe** (primarily black tea): Insignificant positive association for all-cause mortality (ES 1.12, 95% CI 0.88-1.42); p for difference vs Asia = 0.05
- **US**: Non-significant inverse for all-cause (ES 0.92, 95% CI 0.83-1.02)
- Only one study (Wang 2020, China-PAR) reported results by tea type: green tea >=3 times/week associated with 21% lower all-cause mortality and 22% lower CVD mortality; no significant association for black tea

### Sex Differences

- All-cause: men ES 0.87 (0.77-0.98) vs women ES 0.90 (0.79-1.01), p for difference = 0.97
- CVD: men ES 0.84 (0.71-0.99) vs women ES 0.86 (0.75-0.99), p for difference = 0.83
- No significant sex differences for any outcome

### Healthy-User Bias and Confounding Assessment

- All studies adjusted for age and smoking; most adjusted for alcohol and BMI
- The weaker association in European cohorts may partly reflect milk addition to tea reducing polyphenol bioavailability, not just tea type differences
- FFQ-based assessment introduces misclassification (likely bias toward null)
- Adjustment factors varied across studies; residual confounding cannot be ruled out

## Methodology

- **Design**: Systematic review and meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies
- **Databases**: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus through April 2024
- **Registered**: PROSPERO (CRD42023400484)
- **Statistical approach**: DerSimonian-Laird random effects model; restricted cubic splines for nonlinear dose-response (4 knots at 5th, 35th, 65th, 95th percentiles); 150 mL = 1 cup
- **Quality assessment**: Newcastle-Ottawa Scale; all studies scored 7-9 (high quality)
- **Follow-up range**: 5-23 years (mean 12.3 years)
- **Exclusion**: Studies where all participants had a specific disease

## Limitations

1. Tea intake via FFQ, subject to misclassification
2. Cup sizes varied across studies
3. Tea polyphenol composition varies by season, climate, plant age
4. Adjustment factors differed across studies
5. Most studies did not report results by tea type -- green vs black tea comparisons rely on geographic inference
6. High heterogeneity across all outcomes (I2 74-82%)
7. Cannot fully rule out healthy-user confounding despite statistical adjustments

## Relevance to Research Questions

**Q3 (Epidemiological evidence on green tea and mortality)**: This is the most comprehensive and recent meta-analysis on tea consumption and mortality, providing strong evidence that moderate tea consumption (1.5-2 cups/day) is associated with lower all-cause and CVD mortality. The geographic subgroup analysis provides indirect but meaningful evidence that green tea (predominant in Asian cohorts) may have stronger protective effects than black tea, with Asian cohorts showing the largest effect sizes. However, direct green tea vs black tea comparisons are limited to a single study (Wang 2020).
