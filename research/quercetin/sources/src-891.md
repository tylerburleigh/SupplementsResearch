Abstract

Quercetin is a dietary flavonoid purported to improve human endurance exercise capacity. However, published findings are mixed.

Purpose

The study’s purpose was to perform a systematic review of the literature and meta-analysis to examine whether quercetin ingestion increases endurance exercise capacity.

Methods

A search of the literature was conducted using the key words quercetin, performance, exercise, endurance, and aerobic capacity. Eleven studies were identified as meeting the inclusion criteria providing data on 254 human subjects. Across all studies, subject presupplementation V˙O
2max
ranged from 41 to 64 mL·kg
−1
·min
−1
(median = 46), and median treatment duration was 11 d with a median dosage of 1000 mg·d
−1
. Effect sizes (ES) were calculated as the standardized mean difference, and meta-analyses were completed using a random-effects model.

Results

The ES calculated for all studies combining V˙O
2max
and endurance performance measures indicates a significant effect favoring quercetin over placebo (ES = 0.15,
P
= 0.021, 95% confidence interval = 0.02–0.27), but the magnitude of effect is considered between trivial and small, equating to a ∼3% improvement of quercetin over placebo. Using a subgroup meta-analysis comparing quercetin’s effect on endurance exercise performance versus V˙O
2max
, no significant difference was found (
P
= 0.69). Meta-regression of study ES relative to subjects’ fitness level or plasma quercetin concentration achieved by supplementation was also not significant.

Conclusions

On average, quercetin provides a statistically significant benefit in human endurance exercise capacity (V˙O
2max
and endurance exercise performance), but the effect is between trivial and small. Experimental factors that explain the between-study variation remain to be elucidated.

Quercetin is a polyphenolic flavonoid, a natural component in several plant foods (e.g., skin of grapes, onions, apples) (
3,15,26
). The first investigation on quercetin administration in humans was published in 1975 (
24
), and the number of publications has increased dramatically during the past decade. Quercetin is generally recognized as safe by the US Food and Drug Administration, and there are several purported health and physiological benefits of quercetin administration including cardioprotective, anticarcinogenic, antioxidant, antiapoptotic, and ergogenic properties (
5,6,8,14,20,27,41
). Because it is difficult to ingest sufficient quantities in the typical diet, supplemental forms of quercetin may prove to be advantageous; consequently, quercetin has become of interest to military, athletic, and elderly populations (
15
).

Several investigations have focused upon quercetin’s antioxidant properties (
6,19,25,27,34,45,46
). However, unrelated to potential antioxidant effects (
44
), recent studies have examined whether quercetin supplementation enhances endurance exercise capacity. This interest has largely stemmed from impressive results obtained in mice where 7 d of quercetin supplementation (12.5–25 mg·kg
−1
body weight) was associated with greater time to fatigue (40% increase during treadmill running) and volitional physical activity (wheel running) paralleling increased expression of markers of mitochondrial biogenesis (peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor coactivator (PGC)–1α, sirtuin, and mitochondrial DNA (
16
). A study investigating markers of mitochondrial biogenesis in humans (
n
= 26 subjects) after quercetin administration (1000 g·d
−1
for 2 wk) observed trends toward increased markers of mitochondrial biogenesis such as cytochrome
c
oxidase and muscle mitochondrial DNA but failed to reach statistical significance (
41
). Such trends have not been reported for other markers such as sirtuin, PGC-1α, citrate synthase, and cytochrome
c
(
40,41
). Therefore, whether quercetin can enhance mitochondrial biogenesis in humans remains to be determined.

The first human exercise study investigating quercetin supplementation was published in 2006 (
33
), with several more published in the past few years. Some studies report increased endurance exercise capacity in humans after ingestion of quercetin (
14,33,41
), whereas most others fail to find statistically significant benefits in endurance exercise capacity (i.e., endurance exercise performance or aerobic capacity) (
2,9,13,21,22,40,47,49
). Possible reasons for the inconsistent findings among these studies may include the range of subject fitness levels (i.e., potentially eliciting greater quercetin benefits for lower fit subjects), differences in plasma quercetin concentration obtained via the various supplementation protocol/supplement types, differences in research design, and/or inadequate statistical power.

In medicine, a systematic review combined with a meta-analysis can estimate the treatment effects of an intervention and play a role in designing new research. The purpose of this investigation was to systematically review the literature and conduct a meta-analysis to identify quercetin’s effect on endurance exercise capacity (assessed by either maximal oxygen uptake (V˙O
2max
) and/or endurance exercise performance). Moreover, we sought to determine whether different experimental factors (related to test subjects, exercise protocol, or supplementation regimen) could explain the variability in effect size (ES) among studies.

METHODS

Systematic Review.

A thorough systematic search of the research literature was performed for the effects of quercetin ingestion on endurance exercise capacity. For the purposes of this review, endurance exercise capacity was divided into two subcategories: 1) V˙O
2max
, operationally defined as peak oxygen uptake achieved during an incremental exercise test to volitional exhaustion; and 2) endurance exercise performance, operationally defined as either performance in a time trial (>10-min duration) or time to fatigue at a set relative intensity during continuous cycling, running, or other aerobic activity. The literature search began in January 2010 and continued through July 2010. PubMed, SPORTDiscus, ISI Web of Knowledge, ProQuest Dissertation & Theses, and the American College of Sports Medicine database of Annual Meeting proceedings were searched. Reference lists from the retrieved publications and a relevant narrative review article (
15
) were also examined for studies not identified with the database searches. The search terms were quercetin and (performance or exercise or endurance or aerobic capacity or oxygen uptake). The retrieved studies were delimited to those involving human subjects.

Study inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Studies meeting the following inclusion criteria were considered for review: 1) the study was conducted on humans, 2) the study had at least two trials (or separate groups of subjects) in which quercetin was consumed in one trial (or group) and placebo in the other, and 3) a test of endurance exercise capacity (as defined above) was measured. Studies were excluded for the following: 1) the effect observed in a study could not be attributed to quercetin (e.g., the two conditions differed by more than quercetin) or 2) when insufficient data were reported in a study to calculate an ES for endurance exercise capacity. For such studies, we attempted to retrieve the necessary data by contacting the corresponding author by e-mail and/or telephone. All studies administered other substances along with quercetin because of its poor absorption in pure form and/or the use of commercially available products.

Selection of studies.

In total, 1610 publications were originally identified by the database searches and review of article reference lists. However, 1588 were initially excluded on the basis of the title or the review of the abstract or because of duplicate results obtained from the different databases. Twenty-two publications were fully evaluated. On the basis of the inclusion/exclusion criteria, 11 articles/abstracts were excluded, resulting in a total of 11 articles/abstracts for the meta-analyses.

Data extraction and study quality assessment.

For the meta-analysis, endurance exercise capacity data were extracted in the form of means, SD, and sample sizes for quercetin and placebo conditions. These descriptors were reported for pre- and posttreatment values in crossover studies where subjects served as their own control and also in independent quercetin and placebo treatment groups. If available, individual subject data were retrieved to calculate intertrial correlations for endurance exercise capacity. If a study included any treatment condition other than quercetin and placebo, the data for those conditions were omitted for the purpose of the meta-analysis. All 11 studies were assessed for quality on the basis of the Physiotherapy Evidence-Based Database (PEDro) Scale. The scale yields a total possible score of 11 points, with a greater score corresponding to higher quality (
36
).

Endurance exercise capacity data comparing the quercetin and placebo conditions were converted to a standard format by calculating the standardized mean difference, heretofore called the ES. For crossover studies (
n

2

1/2

1/2
/paired difference SD] and standardized mean difference SE [i.e., (1/
n

n
= 2), the respective median intertrial correlation was substituted in the calculation. Median (minimum (min)–maximum (max)) intertrial correlations were 0.95 (0.90–0.99) for V˙O

2,13,40,48

4

21,47

2max

In studies that evaluated more than one experimental factor level, e.g., quercetin dosages of 1000 and 1400 mg·d

for independent subject groups compared with a single placebo group (
40

2max
were measured in the same subjects (
13,14,47

4

Q

I

4

For each study, the relative difference score for V˙O
2max

To calculate the mean difference score for all studies combined, each study’s relative difference score was multiplied by the study’s relative weighting obtained from the meta-analysis (i.e., studies yielding more precise estimates of the ES driven by factors such as larger sample sizes). These values were then summed. The weighted mean difference score was provided only as a descriptive measure to place the magnitude of ES on a comparative basis with effects of other known ergogenic aids. The weighted mean difference was calculated for all measures (V˙O
2max
and performance) and for performance alone.

Moderator variables.

We also sought to determine the role of experimental factors in explaining the interstudy variation observed in ES. These experimental factors were treated as moderator variables in the meta-analysis. A subgroup meta-analysis (i.e., meta-analyses comparing subsets of studies using
Q
tests on the basis of ANOVA) was used to probe potential moderator variables such as whether the ES was influenced by the type of assessment (V˙O
2max
test vs endurance exercise performance test) and whether the study was published or not. Meta-regression (using a method-of-moments model) was used to assess the relationship between study ES and subject fitness level and between study ES and plasma quercetin level achieved with supplementation. Other experimental factors that were also considered for either meta-regression or subgroup meta-analysis included the daily dosage of the supplement and the form of quercetin (chew, powder added to liquid, or as a solid contained within food such as a bar). In 9 of the 11 studies, the daily dosage was the same (1000 mg·d
−1
), making it inappropriate for meta-regression. The variation in quercetin supplement form (liquid with or without CHO or other potentially interactive ingredient, chew tablet, or added to food) made it difficult to categorize into distinct subgroups for additional probing.

Meta-regression.

Meta-analyses and meta-regression were conducted with the Comprehensive Meta-analysis software (version 2.2; Biostat, Inc., Englewood, NJ). An
α
level of 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance in all analyses. ES of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were considered to be small, moderate, and large, respectively (
10
); an ES of 0.1 or less was considered trivial. The effect of publication bias on the meta-analyses was addressed by combining a funnel plot assessment with the Duval and Tweedie trim and fill correction (
18
). This is a preferred method for assessing the extent of publication bias (i.e., the tendency for negative or nonsignificant findings to not be published resulting in an inflated overall ES) as well as for making a correction to the overall ES.

DISCUSSION

The main finding of this study is that quercetin supplementation can improve human endurance exercise capacity (V˙O
2max
and endurance exercise performance), but this benefit is only trivial to small in magnitude (overall ES = 0.15). The ES equates approximately to a 3% improvement of quercetin over control. Because the quercetin versus placebo comparison in the meta-analysis was based on experimental research designs (i.e., each study in the meta-analysis had both quercetin and placebo conditions/groups that were randomly assigned), one can make cause-andeffect claims regarding the effect of quercetin. There was no significant difference between studies that assessed quercetin’s effect using V˙O
2max
tests versus those that used endurance exercise performance tests. Variation in study ES was not significantly associated with subject fitness level or the plasma quercetin achieved with supplementation.

V˙O
2max
and endurance exercise performance were combined for the purpose of this meta-analysis because they are considered to be either a direct measure or predictor of endurance exercise capacity. Also, V˙O
2max
and endurance exercise performance are highly correlated (
r
> 0.9) (
12,35
), indicating that they assess a common construct. However, the effect of quercetin on performance was also of particular interest because it is touted as an ergogenic aid. Quercetin significantly improved exercise performance, with a small magnitude of effect (ES > 0.2) approximating roughly a 5% benefit (as computed via the weighted mean difference), which could be of interest particularly for competitive endurance athletes. The smallest yet meaningful change in performance for elite male cyclists has been reported to be as little as 1%, which is difficult to detect in single studies because of the typical measurement error (i.e., 0.7%–4.7%) (
43
). However, at the present time, there are insufficient studies to evaluate the effects of quercetin for elite endurance athletes, and thus, further examination is merited, particularly with field-based assessments and/or peak power incremental tests that minimize measurement error (
43
).

The ES of quercetin can be compared with meta-analyses conducted on another ergogenic aid such as caffeine. Two other meta-analyses on caffeine’s acute effect on endurance exercise performance (
11,17
) reported an ES of 0.4 and 0.5 compared with placebo. The ES (0.41, 95% CI = 0.31–0.51) reported by Doherty and Smith (
17
) combined endurance, short-term, and graded exercise test protocols and is equivalent to a ∼12% mean difference. Therefore, the potential benefit of quercetin is less effective than acute caffeine ingestion for endurance performance.

A detailed analysis of purported mechanisms for quercetin is beyond the scope of this review. The interested reader is referred to a recent narrative review (
15
). Briefly, the one mechanism that has received the most attention is quercetin’s potential to induce mitochondrial biogenesis (through PGC-1α), which in turn would increase oxidative capacity and subsequently V˙O
2max
and/or endurance exercise performance. This is based upon observations of increased mitochondrial DNA and voluntary wheel running in mice after 1 wk of quercetin supplementation (
16
). There is still inadequate knowledge of the possible underlying mechanisms in humans and the potential for human–rodent species differences. Human studies have used a chronic supplementation regimen (1000 mg·d
−1
for a week or more) with the aim of increasing mitochondrial biogenesis. Most subjects were nonelite endurance athletes because it has been reasoned that highly fit subjects with already elevated mitochondrial density would be less likely to obtain benefits (
13
) (i.e., ceiling effect on V˙O
2max
), whereas subjects with low fitness levels (and therefore presumed low mitochondrial densities) would be more likely to benefit from quercetin administration. Our meta-regression analysis did not reveal a significant correlation between a study’s mean subject fitness level and the study ES
;
however, most studies used subjects with mean V˙O
2max
clustering around 45 mL·kg
−1
·min
−1
, and no study tested a very low-fitness cohort. We conclude that human studies designed to enhance mitochondrial biogenesis do not indicate the same level of efficacy as originally observed in the mouse (at most, a small ES on performance). Whether this trivial-to-small effect translates into other populations (e.g., elite athletes whose V˙O
2max
may otherwise have limited capacity for additional improvement or patients with mitochondrial defects) cannot be assessed. More human studies with these populations are indicated.

The other purported mechanism by which quercetin may act is via adenosine receptor antagonism. Other adenosine receptor antagonists (i.e., caffeine) have acute performance-enhancing effects potentially via the CNS (
7,23,50
), and it is possible that quercetin could act in a similar manner (
1,31,50
) at concentrations achievable in plasma with oral administration of common doses (
9,28,34–40,42,49,50
). To date, only one study was specifically designed to examine the acute effects of oral quercetin (
9
) and compared it with caffeine. Interestingly, neither acute caffeine nor quercetin ingestion improved exercise performance in the heat (
9
). The meta-regression of plasma quercetin did not reveal a significant (or positive) association with the ES for endurance exercise capacity but was limited by the fact that not all studies reported plasma quercetin (
14,21,33
). Because only one study has examined the acute “central” effect of quercetin (presumably via adenosine antagonism), other putative mechanisms cannot be ruled out. Therefore, more dose–response data are needed to understand potential acute effects of quercetin.

There are recognized limitations associated with the conduct of this meta-analysis. For example, not knowing the intertrial correlations in some of the crossover studies is a commonly occurring difficulty in meta-analysis research (
4
) because most primary research studies report neither this correlation nor the data necessary to calculate it. To adjust for the missing correlations, Borenstein (
4
) recommends that a sensitivity analysis be conducted by assessing the effect of varying the assumed intertrial correlations in applicable studies. An estimated intertrial correlation of subjects’ test–retest scores for V˙O
2max
or exercise performance was needed to calculate ES in only two studies. Our sensitivity analysis manipulated the intertrial correlations ranging from the observed min to max values (vs a derived median value). The overall ES was negligibly affected when using the max known intertrial correlation (i.e., ES deviated by 0.001,
P
= 0.010) but altered slightly more when substituting the lowest intertrial correlation (i.e., ES = 0.18,
P
= 0.001). However, the overall conclusion would not be modified.

Another potential limitation of the present systematic review and meta-analysis includes possible publication bias despite inclusion of two known unpublished studies. The inclusion of unpublished data has been questioned because these have not been rigorously peer reviewed (
21,47
). However, the peer-review filter is not perfect. Including unpublished data can alter meta-analysis findings such that statistical significance is lost, highlighting the possibility that the published literature may be affected by selective reporting biases (
30
). Therefore, it has been recommended that results of studies from the “gray literature” (i.e., literature not published in peer-reviewed journals) also be included in meta-analyses (
29
). Thus, two abstracts were included in the meta-analysis (
21,47
) on the basis of our manual searching of conference abstracts. There are no apparent reasons why these two studies are not yet published, at least concerning the quality of the studies gauged by the PEDro scoring system. Our results indicated that the overall ES for the unpublished studies was significantly lower than that for the published studies. Inclusion of unpublished studies did not introduce bias toward finding a benefit of quercetin supplementation on endurance exercise capacity because the effect was just the opposite, i.e., a reduction in the overall ES with their inclusion (from 0.25,
P
= 0.006 for the 9 published studies compared with 0.15 for all 11 studies). In fact, without the two abstracts, the reported magnitude of ES on quercetin would increase from “trivial to small” to “small.”

Publication bias takes place when research that is reflected in the published literature is systematically unrepresentative of the population of completed studies (
48
). Meta-analyses, therefore, tend to be based, to a larger extent, on published data because of the difficulty of identifying unpublished studies and retrieving the data necessary to calculate an ES. As a result, the overall ES tends to be inflated in a meta-analysis if an inadequate search is conducted and not all completed studies are included. The inclusion of the two unpublished abstracts from our analysis is, therefore, appropriate to minimize publication bias. Because there was moderate heterogeneity in the summary ES for quercetin and none of the factors probed seem to account for this ES dispersion, the effect of publication bias may be a contributing factor. Because additional well-designed studies are performed (and published) over time, the present conclusions derived from this meta-analysis may be altered.

One limitation of the current meta-analysis is that all studies administered other substances along with quercetin, because of its poor absorption in pure form and/or the use of commercially available products. Interactions of any of these substances with quercetin cannot be categorically excluded. However, in order for any such interaction to be of relevance to the present review, it would have to be synergistic, diminutive, or redundant to quercetin’s effect on endurance exercise capacity. No such effects have been reported to date.

In conclusion, the meta-analysis of available literature indicates that quercetin provides a statistically significant benefit for a composite measure of endurance exercise capacity (exercise performance and V˙O
2max
), but this effect is between trivial and small in magnitude (ES = 0.15), equating roughly to a 3% improvement with quercetin over placebo. Human studies on quercetin designed to examine performance (overall finding, at most, a small ES) do not indicate the same level of efficacy as originally observed in the mouse or with other endurance exercise ergogenic aids (such as caffeine). The variation among study ES does not seem to be associated with factors such as the endurance exercise test measure, initial subject fitness level, or the plasma concentration obtained with quercetin. What additional experimental factors explain the moderate variation observed for the effects of quercetin among human exercise studies remains unclear on the basis of the available literature.
