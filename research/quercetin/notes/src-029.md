# Reader Note: src-029

## Source Identification
- **Title:** Quercetin supplementation promotes recovery after exercise-induced muscle damage: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
- **Authors:** Daniel Rojano-Ortega, Jose Pena-Amaro, Antonio J Berral-Aguilar, Francisco J Berral-de la Rosa
- **Journal:** Biology of Sport, 2023;40(3):813-825
- **DOI:** 10.5114/biolsport.2023.121320
- **PMID:** 37398956 | **PMCID:** PMC10286622
- **Type:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs (PRISMA-compliant, PROSPERO-registered CRD42021266801)

## Summary

First systematic review and meta-analysis examining quercetin supplementation for recovery after exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD) in humans. Included 13 RCTs (total N=249 participants, sedentary to well-trained). Nearly all studies used 1000 mg/day quercetin. Found significant benefits for muscle soreness (SMD: -1.33, p=0.03), creatine kinase (SMD: -1.15, p=0.02), and oxidative stress (SMD: -0.92, p=0.03). No significant effect on IL-6. Concludes that 1000 mg/day for >7 days (up to 12 weeks) is safe and efficacious for reducing muscle damage and soreness in young men.

## Key Findings

1. **Muscle Soreness (Q1-relevant):** Meta-analysis of 4 studies showed quercetin significantly reduced muscle soreness 0/24 h after exercise (SMD: -1.33; 95% CI: [-2.57, -0.09]; p=0.03), though with high heterogeneity (I2=87%). Effect compared favorably to citrulline (-0.99), omega-3 (-0.93), and beetroot juice (-0.56) from other meta-analyses.

2. **Creatine Kinase (Q1-relevant):** Pooled analysis of 6 studies showed quercetin significantly reduced CK levels 24/48 h post-exercise (SMD: -1.15; 95% CI: [-2.09, -0.21]; p=0.02; I2=85%). Three studies also found lower LDH levels. Suggests quercetin protects muscle fiber membranes from damage.

3. **Oxidative Stress (Q1-relevant):** Meta-analysis showed quercetin significantly decreased oxidative stress markers (SMD: -0.92; 95% CI: [-1.73, -0.11]; p=0.03; I2=80%). Antioxidant capacity showed a near-significant increase (SMD: 0.26; p=0.06; I2=50%), becoming significant after removing one outlier study (Sholten & Sergeev, n=8 total).

4. **Inflammation / IL-6 (Q1-relevant):** Meta-analysis found no significant difference in IL-6 between quercetin and placebo groups immediately after exercise (SMD: -0.09; p=0.58; I2=45%). Individual studies showed mixed results on TNF-alpha and other cytokines.

5. **Functional Recovery (Q1-relevant):** 4 of 5 studies measuring functional variables found significant improvements in the quercetin group: greater maximal isometric voluntary contraction, improved maximal voluntary contraction at multiple velocities, and increased rate of torque development. Mechanism may involve quercetin's role as an adenosine receptor antagonist improving neuromuscular efficiency.

6. **Safety Profile (Q7-relevant):** Cites Andres et al. (2018) finding that only mild adverse effects reported at 1000 mg/day for up to 12 weeks. No long-term safety data beyond 12 weeks or at doses above 1000 mg/day. Concludes 1000 mg/day is safe within this timeframe.

7. **Dose and Duration (Q6-relevant):** 12 of 13 studies used 1000 mg/day; one used 500 mg/day. Supplementation periods ranged from a single dose to 24 days, with most using 7-14 days. One study showed benefit from a single 1000 mg dose 3 hours before exercise. Authors conclude 1 week at 1000 mg/day is sufficient for positive results.

8. **IGF-I/IGF-II (Q1-relevant):** Sgro et al. (2021) found quercetin increased plasma levels of anabolic factors IGF-I and IGF-II after EIMD, suggesting a novel mechanism for promoting muscle regeneration.

## Coverage Signal

Narrow but deep in its specific domain. This is the only systematic review/meta-analysis specifically examining quercetin for exercise-induced muscle damage recovery. Provides quantitative pooled effect sizes across multiple outcome domains (soreness, CK, oxidative stress, IL-6). However, the scope is tightly circumscribed: healthy young male adults, exercise-induced (not disease-related) muscle damage, and short-term supplementation. Generalizability to longevity, chronic disease, older adults, or female populations is limited.

## Quality Concerns

1. **Small total sample size:** Only 249 participants across 13 studies, with individual study group sizes of 5-20. Most studies did not perform a priori power analysis, raising underpowered concerns.

2. **High heterogeneity:** I2 values of 80-87% for the significant outcomes (soreness, CK, oxidative stress) indicate substantial between-study variability, weakening confidence in the pooled estimates.

3. **Methodological quality gaps:** Randomization sequence generation and allocation concealment were not described in any included study. Blinding of outcome assessment was not reported in any study. One study (Demirci) did not confirm randomization. These are significant risk-of-bias concerns.

4. **Population limitations:** 11 of 13 studies included only men; all participants were young (mean ages 15-29). Results cannot be generalized to women, older adults, or clinical populations.

5. **Publication bias not assessed:** Fewer than 10 studies per analysis meant funnel plots were not constructed, leaving publication bias unevaluated.

6. **Sensitivity analysis fragility:** Some effect sizes became non-significant or nearly significant when individual studies were removed (e.g., oxidative stress became p=0.06 after removing Demirci, Duranti, or Sholten & Sergeev studies). This suggests results are driven by a subset of studies.

7. **Heterogeneous exercise protocols:** Studies used very different muscle damage protocols (cycling, eccentric contractions, boxing, swimming, resistance training), making direct comparison difficult.

8. **Biology of Sport journal:** Peer-reviewed and PMC-indexed, but a niche sports science journal. The study has moderate methodological rigor for a systematic review (PRISMA-compliant, PROSPERO-registered).

## Extractable Data Points for Synthesis

- Muscle soreness reduction: SMD -1.33, p=0.03, I2=87% (4 studies)
- CK reduction 24/48h: SMD -1.15, p=0.02, I2=85% (6 studies)
- Oxidative stress reduction: SMD -0.92, p=0.03, I2=80% (meta-analysis)
- Antioxidant capacity increase: SMD 0.26, p=0.06, I2=50% (near-significant)
- IL-6: SMD -0.09, p=0.58, I2=45% (no significant effect)
- Standard dose across studies: 1000 mg/day
- Safety conclusion: mild adverse effects only at 1000 mg/day up to 12 weeks (citing Andres et al. 2018)
- No long-term safety data beyond 12 weeks or above 1000 mg/day
- Quercetin may increase IGF-I and IGF-II (Sgro et al. 2021) -- novel anabolic mechanism
- Quercetin as adenosine receptor antagonist may improve neuromuscular efficiency
- Compared favorably to other supplements for soreness reduction (citrulline, omega-3, beetroot)
