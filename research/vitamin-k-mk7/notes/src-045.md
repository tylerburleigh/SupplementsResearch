# src-045: Treatment with vitamin D3 and/or vitamin K2 for postmenopausal osteoporosis

**Authors:** Iwamoto J, Takeda T, Ichimura S
**Year:** 2003
**Venue:** The Keio Journal of Medicine
**Citations:** 19
**Quality:** ok -- full review article text available (raw text extraction from PDF, some formatting artifacts)

## Core Findings

This narrative review examines the evidence for combined treatment with alfacalcidol (1-alpha-hydroxyvitamin D3) and menatetrenone (vitamin K2/MK-4) for postmenopausal osteoporosis. The authors conclude that combined treatment may have synergistic anabolic effects, but its efficacy is highly dependent on patient age, time since menopause, and severity of osteoporosis. The combination works best in early postmenopausal women with mild osteoporosis and may be harmful in elderly patients with established/severe osteoporosis by increasing bone resorption beyond the capacity of increased bone formation to maintain trabecular integrity.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Clinical studies of combination therapy (Q3: synergistic vs additive effects)

1. **Ushiroyama et al. (2002)** -- Early postmenopausal women (mean age 52.8-54.1), osteopenia/osteoporosis. Combined treatment increased lumbar BMD by **4.92% over 2 years**, while each agent alone only sustained BMD. Combination increased PICP (bone formation marker) and urinary pyridinoline (resorption marker), with a trend toward increased osteocalcin.

2. **Iwamoto et al. (2000)** -- Late postmenopausal women (mean age 64.0 years). Combined treatment increased lumbar BMD by **1.35% over 2 years**, significantly greater than either agent alone. Each agent alone only sustained BMD.

3. **Kobayashi et al. (2002)** -- Elderly patients with established osteoporosis. Combined treatment **increased bone resorption** (urinary deoxypyridinoline), **decreased lumbar BMD**, and **increased vertebral fracture incidence**. Alfacalcidol alone increased BMD more than the combination.

### Mechanistic evidence for synergy (Q3)

- In vitro: menatetrenone's effect on mineralization by human periosteal osteoblasts is enhanced in the presence of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3.
- In vivo (rats): menatetrenone's effect on femoral BMD in ovariectomized rats is significant only when vitamin D3 is present in the diet. The effect correlates with plasma 25-OH vitamin D3 levels.
- In humans: menatetrenone's effect on lumbar BMD is greater in patients with higher serum levels of vitamin D metabolites.
- The authors hypothesize the combination has anabolic effects (increases both formation and resorption), which works when trabecular bone architecture has not deteriorated past a fracture threshold.

### Surrogate vs hard endpoints (Q1)

- Both agents individually prevent vertebral fractures despite no significant BMD increase, suggesting bone quality/microarchitecture improvements.
- The review relies primarily on BMD (surrogate) and vertebral fracture incidence as endpoints.
- Only three clinical studies of combination therapy are cited (refs 28-30), all with small sample sizes and 2-year durations. No large RCTs with hard fracture endpoints.

## Methodology

- **Study type:** Narrative review (not systematic review or meta-analysis)
- **Scope:** Preclinical (ovariectomized rat) and clinical studies of alfacalcidol and/or menatetrenone for postmenopausal osteoporosis
- **Literature base:** 33 references, mostly Japanese studies; some references in Japanese only
- **Limitations of included studies:** Small sample sizes, short durations (2 years), no standardized outcome measures across studies, pharmacologic (not physiologic) doses in animal studies

## Limitations

1. This is a **narrative review**, not a systematic review or meta-analysis. No formal search strategy, quality assessment, or quantitative synthesis.
2. Only **three clinical studies** of combination therapy are cited; all are small and from Japanese populations.
3. The vitamin K2 form used is **menatetrenone (MK-4)**, not MK-7. Findings may not generalize to MK-7 supplementation.
4. The alfacalcidol used is a **prescription active vitamin D metabolite**, not over-the-counter vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol). This is pharmacologically distinct.
5. No direct head-to-head comparison of combination vs individual agents in a single large RCT.
6. Some cited references are in Japanese only and cannot be independently verified.

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q3 (Synergistic vs additive effects of K2 + D3):** Highly relevant. Provides mechanistic rationale for synergy (vitamin D3 enhances K2's effect on osteoblast mineralization; K2 efficacy depends on vitamin D status) and clinical evidence showing the combination outperforms monotherapy in specific populations. However, also shows the combination can be counterproductive in elderly/severe osteoporosis.
- **Q1 (Hard clinical endpoints vs surrogates):** Moderately relevant. Notes that fracture prevention occurs without BMD increases, highlighting the limitation of BMD as a surrogate. The combination studies primarily use BMD as the endpoint, with only vertebral fracture incidence as a harder endpoint.

## Claims to Verify

- **BMD increase of 4.92% (Ushiroyama et al.):** This is a surprisingly precise and large number. The primary study (ref 28, Maturitas 2002) should be checked to confirm the population size and whether this was the primary or secondary endpoint.
- **BMD increase of 1.35% (Iwamoto et al.):** This is the authors' own study (ref 29). The small magnitude and potential for reporting bias should be noted.
- **Negative effects in elderly (Kobayashi et al.):** This study (ref 30, Clinical Calcium 2002, in Japanese) cannot be independently verified from English-language sources. The claim that combination treatment increased fracture incidence in elderly patients is critical and should be corroborated.
