# src-101: Vascular Calcification in CKD: Role of Vitamin K-Dependent MGP

**Citation:** Roumeliotis et al. (2020), Frontiers in Medicine, 7:154. doi:10.3389/fmed.2020.00154
**Type:** Mini Review (peer-reviewed)
**Citations:** 51
**Quality:** ok -- full text extracted via raw text fallback

## Core Findings

Matrix Gla Protein (MGP) is one of the most powerful natural inhibitors of vascular calcification, but requires vitamin K-dependent gamma-carboxylation and phosphorylation to become biologically active. In CKD patients, vitamin K deficiency is prevalent and reflected by elevated circulating dp-ucMGP (dephosphorylated uncarboxylated MGP), which independently predicts vascular calcification, cardiovascular morbidity, and mortality. MK-7 supplementation produces dose-dependent reductions in dp-ucMGP, with higher doses (360 ug/day) achieving up to 86% reduction in hemodialysis patients, suggesting potential to slow or reverse vascular calcification, though hard cardiovascular outcome data are still pending from ongoing RCTs.

## Key Evidence

### MK-7 vs K1 vs K2 (Q2)
- MK-7 exhibits highest efficacy among vitamin K forms due to better bioavailability and longer half-life (line 296-299)
- Rotterdam Study (n=4,807, 10-year follow-up): K2 intake negatively correlated with aortic calcification, all-cause mortality, and CVD; K1 showed no association (line 307-314)
- Animal model: K2 (but not K1) reduced arterial mineralization in warfarin-treated rats; aortic K2 concentration was 3x higher than K1 (Spronk et al., line 342-346)
- Animal model: High MK-7 supplementation reversed cardiovascular mineralization with 10-fold upregulation of MGP expression in uremic rats (Scheiber et al., line 348-353)
- Meta-analysis (Chen et al., 21 studies, 222,592 subjects): both K1 and K2 dietary intake associated with aortic calcification (line 320-323) -- unlike Rotterdam, this found K1 also associated

### Dose-Response (Q5)
Dose-response data from multiple trials are detailed in the paper's Table 1:

1. **Healthy volunteers (Theuwissen et al., n=42, 3 months):** 10 ug/day = 12.1% dp-ucMGP decrease; 180 ug = 39.7%; 360 ug = 56% (line 366-375)
2. **HD patients (Westenfeld et al., n=53, 6 weeks):** 45 ug = 17.9%; 135 ug = 36.7%; 360 ug = 61.1% (line 477-483)
3. **HD patients (Caluwe et al., n=200, 8 weeks, thrice-weekly):** 360 ug = 17%; 720 ug = 33%; 1080 ug = 46% (line 484-493)
4. **HD patients (Aoun et al., n=50, 4 weeks):** 360 ug/day = 86% dp-ucMGP reduction (line 393-396)
5. **CKD stage 3-5 (Kurnatowska et al., n=42, 9 months):** 90 ug/day MK-7 + vit D = 19% dp-ucMGP decrease, slower VC progression vs D alone (line 378-386)
6. **Kidney transplant recipients (Mansour et al., n=60, 8 weeks):** 360 ug/day = 55.1% dp-ucMGP decrease, 14.2% arterial stiffness reduction (line 509-515)

### Arguments Against Supplementation / Limitations (Q4)
- Population-based studies have "failed to show clear-cut results" regarding ucMGP association with VC/CVD (line 230-233) -- attributed to insensitive measurement methods
- Meta-analysis (Lees et al., 13 controlled studies, 12,888 patients): vitamin K produced non-significant improvement in arterial stiffness despite reducing VC by 9.1% and dp-ucMGP by 44.7% (line 409-420)
- All existing interventional studies have limitations: none is a large RCT with hard CV outcomes, small sample sizes, varying MK-7 doses, only surrogate markers assessed (line 423-427)
- No toxicity or thrombosis risk documented even at large doses (line 450-454), but long-term safety in large CKD cohorts not yet established
- Ideal method for vitamin K status assessment still debated; interactions with medications (e.g., phosphate binders) not fully elucidated (line 431-446)
- Several ongoing RCTs listed (KING, iPACK-HD, VitaVasK, etc.) that may provide definitive answers

## Methodology

Mini review synthesizing evidence from in vitro studies, animal models, observational cohort studies, and interventional clinical trials. Focus on CKD/ESRD/hemodialysis populations. Sources drawn from PubMed literature. No original data collection.

## Limitations

- Review article (not primary data), though it comprehensively summarizes existing evidence
- Many cited interventional studies have small sample sizes (n=17-200)
- No hard cardiovascular outcomes in any cited trial (only surrogate markers like dp-ucMGP, arterial stiffness, calcification scores)
- Several ongoing trials had not reported results at time of publication (2020)

## Relevance

Directly addresses Q2 (MK-7 vs K1): provides strong evidence MK-7 is the most effective form, with specific comparative data from Rotterdam Study and animal models showing K2 superior to K1 for vascular outcomes. Directly addresses Q5 (dose-response): comprehensive dose-response data across 6+ studies from 10 ug/day to 1080 ug/day. Addresses Q4 (arguments against): documents the key limitation that no large RCT with hard CV endpoints has been completed, and notes the non-significant arterial stiffness finding in meta-analysis.
