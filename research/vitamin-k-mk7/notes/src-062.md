# src-062: Vitamin K Supplementation on Cardiovascular Risk Factors (Meta-Analysis)

**Citation:** Zhao et al. (2024). "The effect of vitamin K supplementation on cardiovascular risk factors: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Journal of Nutritional Science*, vol. 13, e3. DOI: 10.1017/jns.2023.106

**Quality:** ok -- full text available, 39K chars, substantive methods/results/discussion.

## Core Findings

This meta-analysis of 17 RCTs found that vitamin K supplementation significantly reduced HOMA-IR (a measure of insulin resistance) but had **no significant effect on any other CVD risk factor** including glucose, HbA1c, insulin, weight, BMI, blood pressure, CRP, or lipid profile (TC, TG, LDL, HDL). The only positive finding -- HOMA-IR reduction (WMD: -0.24, P = 0.047) -- came with high heterogeneity (I2 = 88.3%) and was borderline significant. Subgroup analysis showed vitamin K2 had a stronger effect on HOMA-IR than K1.

## Methodology

- **Design:** Systematic review and meta-analysis following PRISMA guidelines, registered at PROSPERO (CRD42023454787).
- **Search:** PubMed/MEDLINE, SCOPUS, Web of Science, Embase from inception through April 2022.
- **Included:** 17 RCTs (parallel design except one crossover), published 2008-2021.
- **Participants:** Adults (age range 40-70) with diverse conditions: postmenopausal women (5 studies), CKD/renal (4), diabetes/prediabetes (3), and others (PCOS, vascular disease, rheumatoid arthritis, healthy). Both sexes; some studies female-only.
- **Interventions:** Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) or K2 (MK-7, MK-4) at doses 90 ug to 1000 ug/day, durations 4-144 weeks.
- **Analysis:** Random-effects model, WMD with 95% CI, Cochran's Q and I2 for heterogeneity, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, NutriGRADE quality score (8.2 = very good).
- **Risk of bias:** Cochrane RoB 2 tool -- most studies low risk, 4 studies had unclear overall risk, 2 had high risk (Oikonomaki 2019, and one other).

## Subgroup Analysis

- Vitamin K2 showed significant effect on HOMA-IR vs. K1; no other subgroup differences.
- Subgroup analyses by dose and duration showed no significant effects on any outcome.

## Limitations

1. **High heterogeneity** for HbA1c (I2=90.7%), HOMA-IR (I2=88.3%), and insulin (I2=88.3%) -- limits confidence in pooled estimates.
2. **Vitamin D confound:** Some included studies co-administered vitamin D to both intervention and control groups, complicating interpretation.
3. **Small study counts** for some outcomes (e.g., only 2 studies for CRP, 3 for HbA1c).
4. **Diverse populations** with different health conditions make generalization difficult; too few studies per subgroup for population-based analysis.
5. One study (Mansour 2017) was single-arm with no control group; one (Oikonomaki 2019) was open-label with no placebo.
6. Search only through April 2022.

## Relevance to Research Questions

**Q1 (MK-7 effect on CVD events):** Indirectly relevant. This meta-analysis examines CVD risk factors (BP, lipids, glucose, CRP, BMI) rather than CVD events. It finds no benefit of vitamin K (including MK-7) on most risk factors. The null finding for blood pressure, lipids, and CRP suggests limited cardiovascular benefit. However, it does not address hard CVD endpoints (MACE, mortality, arterial calcification).

**Q6 (Meta-analysis methodology and disagreements):** Directly relevant. This is a well-conducted meta-analysis (PROSPERO-registered, PRISMA-guided, NutriGRADE 8.2) that found essentially null results for vitamin K on most CVD risk factors. Its findings largely agree with prior meta-analyses by Shahdadian et al. (2018) and Suksomboon et al. (2017). It disagrees with narrative reviews (Varsamis et al. 2021) that suggested vitamin K benefits glucose/lipid metabolism, but notes those reviews included non-RCT and in vitro evidence.

## Claims to Verify

- The borderline HOMA-IR result (P = 0.047) with I2 = 88.3% should be interpreted cautiously -- the high heterogeneity and borderline p-value suggest this may not be robust.
- CRP analysis was based on only 2 studies, making that null finding very weak evidence.
- One included study (Mansour 2017) had no control group (single-arm trial); another (Oikonomaki 2019) had no placebo. These should arguably have been excluded per the stated inclusion criteria requiring a control group.
