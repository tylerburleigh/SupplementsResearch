# Sulforaphane Supplementation for Human Health and Longevity: A Critical Evidence Review

## Executive Summary

Sulforaphane, an isothiocyanate (a sulfur-containing plant compound) derived from cruciferous vegetables, has been investigated in over 50 clinical trials and thousands of preclinical studies for its health-promoting properties. The evidence base forms a steep pyramid: extensive mechanistic and animal data supporting the Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway (a master cellular stress-response system) as the validated mechanism of action, a large body of human biomarker trials showing reproducible pharmacodynamic effects (measurable changes in how the body responds at a cellular level), but no randomized controlled trials demonstrating effects on hard clinical endpoints such as mortality, disease incidence, or functional decline.

The largest and most rigorous human trial (n=291) demonstrated a 61% increase in detoxication of benzene (a common air pollutant) and a 23% increase in detoxication of acrolein (another common air pollutant) sustained over 12 weeks, but these remain surrogate biomarker outcomes, not disease endpoints. Several RCTs have missed their prespecified primary endpoints, and of 84 clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, only 39 have been published, meaning approximately 50% of registered trials remain unpublished [25].

A critical tension underlies the entire field: the Nrf2 activation that makes sulforaphane attractive for cancer prevention may also be hijacked by established tumors for pro-survival advantage. There is no consensus on optimal dosing, formulation, or treatment duration.

## 1. Evidence Hierarchy: Mechanistic Data to Clinical Endpoints

### The Validated Mechanism: Nrf2/Keap1 Signaling

The strongest mechanistic evidence for sulforaphane centers on the Nrf2/Keap1 signaling pathway. A comprehensive review in the *Annual Review of Pharmacology and Toxicology* (Ma, 2013) establishes that among many putative cellular targets, only Keap1-Nrf2 signaling is considered a validated target [1]. Sulforaphane modifies critical cysteine thiols on Keap1, disrupting the ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of Nrf2 (the cell's waste-disposal system). This allows newly synthesized Nrf2 to enter the nucleus and activate antioxidant response element (ARE)-dependent genes governing over 200 cytoprotective proteins [1].

Preclinical carcinogenesis data is robust across multiple organ sites (skin, oral, stomach, colon, lung, bladder) and both chemical and genetic models, with anti-tumorigenic efficacy dampened in Nrf2-disrupted animals [1, 2]. This provides causal evidence that Nrf2 mediates the chemopreventive effect. A separate authoritative review confirms that broccoli preparations rich in glucoraphanin or sulforaphane exert demonstrable pharmacodynamic action in over 20 clinical trials [2].

### The Inverted Pyramid of Evidence

The evidence hierarchy for sulforaphane shows an inverted pyramid. The base -- extensive mechanistic and preclinical evidence -- far outweighs the tip of human clinical data:

- **Mechanistic and preclinical:** Thousands of publications across cell culture, *C. elegans* (lifespan extension via DAF-2/DAF-16 insulin/IGF-1 signaling; Qi et al., 2021), mice (improved skin collagen, reduced MMP9 via Keap1-Nrf2 activation; Petkovic et al., 2021), and other models [4].
- **Biomarker RCTs (50+ trials):** The vast majority of human trials use surrogate biomarkers: NQO1 induction (a detoxification enzyme), oxidative stress markers, Phase II enzyme activity (the body's conjugating enzymes that neutralize toxins), urinary metabolite excretion, gene expression changes [2, 4, 5].
- **Clinical endpoint RCTs (0 trials):** No RCT has demonstrated efficacy on mortality, disease incidence, or functional decline.

A systematic review of 17 human RCTs (2003-2013) found the most consistent evidence for blood glucose and lipid profile improvements, along with oxidative stress marker reduction, with weaker evidence for anti-inflammatory effects, *H. pylori* reduction, and cancer protection [6].

### Psychiatric and Cognitive RCTs: The Closest to Clinical Endpoints

Several RCTs have tested sulforaphane on psychiatric and cognitive outcomes using validated clinical rating scales, representing the closest approximations to clinical endpoint evidence:

- **Schizophrenia:** A 24-week RCT (n=77) found large effect sizes (Cohen's d = 0.83-0.86 at 24 weeks, a standardized effect size where 0.8 is conventionally considered large) for reducing negative symptoms (PANSS), though it lacked functional outcome measures, and the placebo group showed greater improvement in positive symptoms [7].
- **Depression post-cardiac intervention:** A 6-week RCT (n=60) showed significantly greater HAM-D improvement (30% response rate vs. 6.67%, P=0.042), but remission was not significant (P=0.052) [8]. Notably, the dose and formulation used in this trial were not reported in the available literature, making these results non-replicable from published data.
- **ASD irritability:** A 10-week RCT (n=60) showed significant improvement as an adjunct to risperidone (P=0.001) [9].
- **Cognitive function in older adults:** A 12-week RCT (n=144) found improved processing speed and working memory, but no synergistic effect with brain training [10, 11].

All are small, short-duration trials in specific populations without independent replication.

### The Null Pattern: Biomarker Modulation Without Clinical Translation

Multiple RCTs have shown sulforaphane effects on surrogate biomarkers while failing to affect harder endpoints:

- In **prostate cancer** (n=98), a double-blind RCT found gene expression changes (AMACR 7-fold reduction, ARLNC1 4.3-fold reduction) but no significant differences in HDAC activity, Ki-67 (a marker of cell proliferation), p21, or other tissue biomarkers [12].
- In **former smokers** (n=37 completers), a Phase II RCT showed significant Ki-67 reduction in bronchial tissue but no improvement in bronchial histopathology or apoptotic markers [13].
- In **prediabetes** (n=74), a rigorous RCT found a statistically significant 0.2 mmol/L fasting glucose reduction that failed to meet its prespecified 0.3 mmol/L primary endpoint [14].

### Observational Claims

A widely cited claim of 32-43% lower all-cause mortality from broccoli intake 1-2 times per week (n > 12,000 adults) lacks a specific citation in the source article, which is a non-peer-reviewed commercial health blog [3]. The effect size is unusually large for a dietary observational study and almost certainly reflects healthy-user bias. No RCT has corroborated this association with actual mortality endpoints.

## 2. Bioavailability and Pharmacokinetics

### The Formulation Hierarchy

Sulforaphane bioavailability depends critically on formulation type, creating a clear hierarchy [4, 15]:

1. **Pure stabilized SFN supplements** (e.g., Prostaphane, BROQ): Highest and most predictable bioavailability.
2. **Glucoraphanin + myrosinase combinations** (e.g., Avmacol): Approximately 35% bioavailability.
3. **Glucoraphanin-only supplements** (e.g., Crucera-SGS/Thorne): Rely entirely on gut bacterial myrosinase for conversion, yielding the least predictable bioavailability.

Jed Fahey's lab at Johns Hopkins has tested multiple commercial supplements over decades and found many are mislabeled or ineffective. While Avmacol's use in a published schizophrenia RCT is directly verified [7], claims about Prostaphane and BROQ verification and the broader comparative bioavailability hierarchy draw primarily from a third-party blog [15] without direct citations to primary literature. The limited comparative bioavailability data across products should be noted.

### The Gut Microbiome Bottleneck

Serum sulforaphane levels show a pronounced bimodal distribution after oral supplementation (~0.2 nmol/mL in low converters vs. ~1.3 nmol/mL in high converters) [14]. This six-fold variability has direct clinical implications: a dose that produces therapeutic levels in one person may be subtherapeutic in another, making standardized dosing unreliable across individuals.

This variability is mechanistically linked to gut microbiota composition. The *Bacteroides*-encoded BT2160 transcriptional regulator (part of the BT2156-BT2160 operon) is required for converting inactive glucoraphanin to bioactive sulforaphane, and its abundance correlates with serum SFN levels (P=0.046) and glycemic response [14]. The Qidong, China chemoprevention trials similarly noted "large individual variability in bioavailability" [1].

### Tissue-Level Delivery

Oral glucoraphanin at 450 mg/day (1 mmol) produces measurable sulforaphane and its metabolites in human skin tissue, with SFN detected in skin biopsies of 6 of 12 subjects, and achieves 3.1-fold NQO1 mRNA induction in non-UVB skin biopsies [16]. However, only 50% of tested subjects showed detectable skin SFN levels, underscoring the interindividual variability in tissue delivery.

In a Phase II RCT in former smokers, higher SFN bioavailability correlated with greater reduction in bronchial Ki-67 proliferation index (P for trend = 0.019), demonstrating a dose-response relationship between circulating SFN levels and biomarker efficacy [13].

### Sustained Bioavailability

A 12-week RCT in Qidong, China (n=291) using a broccoli sprout beverage providing 600 umol glucoraphanin + 40 umol SFN daily demonstrated that urinary SFN metabolite levels did not decline over the full 84-day dosing period, indicating no tolerance or metabolic adaptation develops with chronic daily supplementation [17].

### Allometric Scaling Challenges

A comprehensive review cataloguing over 3,000 publications and over 50 clinical trials highlights the challenge of translating animal doses to human-equivalent doses, with correction factors of 0.081 for murine doses and 0.162 for rat doses (based on 70 kg body weight) [4]. Urinary metabolite excretion measured by isotope dilution mass spectrometry has been used as an "internal dose" biomarker to compare actual systemic exposure across formulations [4].

### Myrosinase: The Critical Enzyme

Myrosinase enzyme activity is the critical bottleneck for glucoraphanin-to-sulforaphane conversion and is highly sensitive to food preparation. Heating destroys myrosinase; raw or lightly steamed broccoli (1-3 minutes) preserves enzymatic activity [15, 18]. Adding mustard powder (a rich source of exogenous myrosinase) to cooked broccoli increases SFN bioavailability by over 4-fold [18]. For sprouts, the conversion factor is approximately 0.425 mg SFN per gram of fresh broccoli sprouts, though this varies with sprout age and growing conditions and is not backed by a primary citation [15].

## 3. Effect Sizes and Clinical Significance

### The Largest Effect: Air Pollutant Detoxication

The largest sulforaphane RCT (n=291; Egner et al., 2014) tested a broccoli sprout beverage in Qidong, China, and demonstrated a 61% increase in benzene detoxication and 23% increase in acrolein detoxication via urinary mercapturic acid biomarkers, sustained over 12 weeks [17]. While this is the most robust biomarker-level evidence, it measured surrogate endpoints (carcinogen excretion), not actual cancer incidence or mortality. Crotonaldehyde showed no response, indicating selectivity. The population was heavily polluted rural Chinese adults, limiting generalizability.

### Cancer RCTs: Gene Expression Without Clinical Translation

A systematic review of 8 sulforaphane cancer therapy RCTs (total N approximately 403) found that no study demonstrated statistically significant benefits on hard clinical endpoints [5]. 75% of trials had high risk of bias. The strongest signals were on gene expression biomarkers (e.g., AMACR 7-fold reduction in prostate cancer), but the clinical significance of these changes remains unproven. Only one trial assessed overall survival (pancreatic cancer, n=40) and found a non-significant trend (P=0.291) [5].

### The Prostate Cancer Disconnect

In a double-blind RCT (n=98) in men scheduled for prostate biopsy, 200 umol/day SFN confirmed bioavailability (elevated urine/plasma metabolites) and produced 40 differentially expressed genes including downregulation of AMACR (7-fold) and ARLNC1 (4.3-fold). However, no changes were observed in blood HDAC activity or prostate tissue biomarkers (H3K18ac, HDAC3, HDAC6, Ki67, p21) [12]. This trial illustrates the central challenge: sulforaphane reliably reaches target tissues and modulates gene expression, but downstream biomarker changes and clinical endpoints remain largely null.

### Psychiatric Effects: Promising but Confounded

In schizophrenia, a 24-week RCT (n=77) found large effect sizes for negative symptoms (Cohen's d = 0.83-0.86 at 24 weeks, P <= 0.001) [7]. However, the effect only emerged at 24 weeks and was absent at 12 weeks; no improvement was seen on the Clinical Global Impression (CGI) scale; there was a major amisulpride imbalance (17 SFN vs. 1 placebo patients); and the tablet contained moringa and beta-glucans, preventing attribution to SFN alone [7].

### Prediabetes: A Missed Primary Endpoint

In a rigorous 12-week RCT (n=74, *Nature Microbiology*), 150 umol/day SFN reduced fasting blood glucose by 0.2 mmol/L (P=0.04), failing to meet the prespecified 0.3 mmol/L primary endpoint [14]. No significant changes occurred in HbA1c, BMI, HOMA-IR, lipids, or other secondary outcomes.

Exploratory subgroup analysis found a 0.4 mmol/L reduction in MARD-like prediabetics (a subtype characterized by mild age-related diabetes features) with specific gut microbiota profiles, and a 0.7 mmol/L reduction when combining MARD phenotype with high BT2160 operon abundance -- but these are post hoc, underpowered findings requiring confirmation [14].

### Skin Biomarkers Without Clinical Benefit

A skin bioavailability trial (n=6 per arm) demonstrated 3.1-fold NQO1 mRNA induction in skin biopsies and reduced IL-1beta and TNF-alpha, but no significant effect on UVB-induced erythema [16]. A topical broccoli sprout extract study (n=4) demonstrated Nrf2 activation and keratin 17 upregulation -- proof-of-concept for epidermolysis bullosa simplex but without testing actual blistering outcomes [19].

## 4. Risks, Drug Interactions, and the Nrf2 Paradox

### The Nrf2 Double-Edged Sword

Nrf2 has a well-documented dual role in cancer: protective against carcinogenesis in normal cells, but hijacked by established tumors for pro-survival advantage [1, 20]. Multiple mechanisms of tumor Nrf2 hijacking are documented:

- Somatic NRF2 mutations (spontaneous DNA changes in tumors) disrupting Keap1 binding (ETGE 57%, DLG 43%)
- KEAP1 mutations and promoter methylation (a chemical modification that silences tumor-suppressor genes)
- Oncogenic Kras/Braf/Myc-driven Nrf2 transcription
- p62/DJ-1 positive feedback loops

Critically, Keap1 knockout mice did not develop spontaneous cancer over 2 years, suggesting Nrf2 overactivation alone is insufficient for tumor initiation but may promote established tumors [1].

### Direct Preclinical Evidence of Tumor Promotion

Zheng et al. (2022) found that daily intraperitoneal SFN injection (25 mg/kg for 3 months) in mice, starting 6 months after a hepatocarcinogen dose, significantly increased liver-to-body weight ratio, tumor growth, and number and size of hepatomas compared to vehicle-treated mice [21]. NRF2, NQO1, and PCNA expression were further elevated in treated mice, suggesting SFN stimulates tumor progression via sustained NRF2 activation in already-initiated tumors.

### The Nuanced Picture

The Surh (2021) "Nrf2 paradox" review argues that in most tumors, Nrf2 is already constitutively hyperactivated through mutations and epigenetic changes, so additional activation by dietary sulforaphane may have marginal further effect [22]. Sulforaphane also has pro-oxidant capacity in cancer cells (depleting GSH and generating ROS), which could make it selectively toxic to tumor cells. The net effect depends on whether SFN's pro-oxidant or antioxidant actions dominate in a given tumor context [22].

### Hormetic Dose-Response Concerns

Sulforaphane exhibits a hormetic (biphasic) dose-response mediated through the Nrf2/ARE pathway. SFN-induced hormetic Nrf2 activation reduces pathology in models of Parkinson's, Alzheimer's, stroke, and renal nephropathy. However, low-dose SFN enhanced cell proliferation in multiple tumor cell lines, a concerning finding with uncertain clinical implications [23]. The optimal therapeutic window for cancer prevention versus potential cancer promotion has not been established in humans.

### Interference with Cancer Therapy

Sulforaphane may interfere with cancer immunotherapy. SFN blocks T cell-mediated immune response required for immune surveillance of tumors, and could interfere with immune checkpoint inhibitors (CTLA-4 antibodies, PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies) or CAR T cell therapy [22]. Conversely, preclinical studies show SFN can potentiate the efficacy of conventional chemotherapy agents (gemcitabine, cisplatin, doxorubicin, 5-fluorouracil, sorafenib) while mitigating their toxic side effects [24]. The net effect in cancer patients remains unresolved.

### Cardiovascular Complexity

In preclinical mouse models, Nrf2 was required for cholesterol crystal-induced inflammasome activation, potentially promoting IL-1beta-mediated vascular inflammation [1]. This finding has not been demonstrated in humans and contrasts with Nrf2's generally protective role in other contexts. Nrf2 activation by SFN could theoretically worsen certain cardiovascular conditions, though this remains speculative without human evidence.

### Drug Metabolism Interactions

Sulforaphane induces phase II detoxification enzymes (NQO1, GSTs, UGTs) and modulates CYP enzymes, which could alter the metabolism and efficacy of co-administered drugs [1]. However, the chronic, daily supplementation regimens used in clinical trials (up to 12-24 months) have not been evaluated for long-term effects on drug metabolism.

### General Safety Profile

Sulforaphane is consistently well-tolerated across clinical trials. In the systematic review of 8 cancer RCTs, no study reported significant adverse event differences between SFN and placebo [5]. The most comprehensive safety data (Cipolla et al., 2015, 78 patients, 6 months) showed 89% Grade 1 and 11% Grade 2 events, with no Grade 3/4 events and only one dropout due to adverse events [5]. Participants most commonly reported gastrointestinal side effects (loose stools, nausea, diarrhea), which were also the primary reason for discontinuation [14, 5].

### Publication Bias and Unpublished Safety Data

Of 84 sulforaphane clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, only 39 have been published, meaning approximately 50% of registered trials remain unpublished (Saito et al., 2025) [25]. These registrations include various trial statuses, not only completed trials. This raises concerns about publication bias distorting the perceived safety and efficacy profile. Negative or adverse results may be disproportionately represented among unpublished trials.

## 5. Supplements vs. Dietary Sources

### Sprouts: The Viable Dietary Source

Broccoli sprouts contain dramatically more glucoraphanin than mature broccoli. The primary-source estimate is 20-50x higher concentrations, established by Fahey et al. (1997) [4]. Popular sources claim 100-400x more sulforaphane potential at 3-day germination, but this figure lacks primary source verification and should be treated with caution [18, 15]. Clinically relevant sulforaphane doses (e.g., 40-60 mg/day) are estimated to be achievable from roughly 100-140g of fresh broccoli sprouts daily based on an approximate conversion factor of 0.425 mg SFN/g drawn from secondary sources [15, 4]. However, this conversion factor is not backed by a primary citation, and actual SFN content varies widely with sprout age, seed variety, and growing conditions, so the gram-weight needed to match clinical trial doses cannot be stated precisely.

### The Myrosinase Problem: A Genuine Rationale for Supplements

Cooking destroys myrosinase, the enzyme required to convert glucoraphanin to sulforaphane, making cooked broccoli a much less reliable source [15, 18]. Adding mustard powder (a rich source of exogenous myrosinase) to cooked broccoli can increase sulforaphane bioavailability by over 4-fold [18]. This myrosinase-sensitivity problem is a genuine rationale for supplements: they provide either pre-formed sulforaphane or stabilized myrosinase alongside glucoraphanin, bypassing the thermal instability issue.

### Food-Based Delivery in Clinical Trials

Many clinical trials have used broccoli sprout-derived preparations (beverages, extracts, soups) rather than purified supplements [17, 4, 5, 6]. The Qidong, China trials used hot-water broccoli sprout extracts reconstituted as beverages, achieving measurable detoxication biomarker changes in the largest human trial to date [17]. Dwibedi et al. (2025) noted their broccoli sprout extract was available at very low cost per dose and could be delivered as a functional food [14].

### Supplement Quality Concerns

Jed Fahey's lab has tested many commercial supplements over decades and found a significant number are mislabeled or ineffective [15]. While Avmacol has been directly used in a published clinical trial [7], claims that Prostaphane and BROQ have been verified in clinical trials rest primarily on a third-party blog [15] without direct primary-source citations, and should be treated as incompletely verified. Home-sprouting broccoli seeds offers a low-cost, high-potency dietary source but carries bacterial contamination risk, particularly concerning for immunocompromised individuals and pregnant women [15].

### The Dosing Consistency Problem

Glucoraphanin content in broccoli sprouts varies widely depending on seed variety, sprout age, growing conditions, and preparation method [15, 4, 14]. Clinical trials have used standardized preparations with verified sulforaphane content measured by HPLC, which is impossible to replicate with home-grown sprouts. However, even whole-food formats achieve pharmacodynamic effects: Traka et al. (2008) used 400g frozen broccoli per week, and Traka et al. (2019) used broccoli soup with 214-492 umol glucoraphanin per week, both producing measurable biomarker changes [5].

### No Head-to-Head Comparison

No head-to-head trial comparing supplement vs. dietary sulforaphane delivery on clinical endpoints has been conducted. The real question is not whether supplements are needed for consistency, but whether the consistency advantage translates into clinically superior outcomes, which remains empirically unanswered [4, 5].

## 6. Largest Trials and Null Results

### The Largest Trial: Qidong Detoxication Study (n=291)

The Egner et al. (2014) RCT in Qidong, China used a daily broccoli sprout-derived beverage providing 600 umol glucoraphanin + 40 umol SFN for 12 weeks, achieving a 61% increase in benzene detoxication and 23% increase in acrolein detoxication (both P <= 0.01) [17]. Bioavailability did not decline over 12 weeks. However, crotonaldehyde showed no response, and these are surrogate biomarker endpoints, not disease outcomes.

### The Longest Trial: Former Smoker Lung Study (12 months)

A Phase II RCT in former smokers (n=37 completers) using 95 umol/day SFN for 12 months found a significant 20% reduction in bronchial Ki-67 proliferation index vs. 65% increase in placebo (P=0.014), but no improvement in bronchial histopathology scores, caspase-3, or TUNEL apoptotic markers [13]. This mixed result illustrates the pattern where positive sub-endpoints may be highlighted while null primary endpoints receive less attention.

### Systematic Review of Cancer RCTs: Predominantly Null

The ElKhalifa et al. (2023) systematic review of 8 cancer RCTs found [5]:

- No study demonstrated statistically significant benefits on hard clinical endpoints
- 75% of trials had high risk of bias
- PSA showed no change in 2 of 3 prostate cancer studies
- Overall survival was non-significant in pancreatic cancer (P=0.291)
- Ki-67 was non-significant in breast cancer (P=0.32)
- Nrf2-regulated genes showed no changes after 1-year treatment

### Prediabetes: Missed Primary Endpoint

The Dwibedi et al. (2025) RCT (n=74) failed to meet its prespecified primary endpoint of 0.3 mmol/L fasting glucose reduction, achieving only 0.2 mmol/L [14]. The most interesting findings came from exploratory post-hoc subgroup analyses requiring confirmatory trials.

### Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: Negative on Functional Outcome

A double-blind RCT of SFX-01 after subarachnoid hemorrhage (n=105, 28 days, 300 mg BID) found that sulforaphane did not reduce large vessel vasospasm or improve functional outcome on the modified Rankin Scale (P=0.237) [26]. This is one of the few sulforaphane trials with a hard clinical endpoint, and it was negative.

### COPD: Null on the Primary Mechanism

A published correction titled "Lack of Effect of Oral Sulforaphane Administration on Nrf2 Expression in COPD" documents a trial that found no effect of sulforaphane on Nrf2 expression in COPD patients [27]. Since Nrf2 activation is sulforaphane's primary validated mechanism, a null result on this pathway in a respiratory disease population is notable.

### Breast Biopsy: Null Between-Group Comparisons

A 2-8 week RCT in 54 women scheduled for breast biopsy found that between-group comparisons showed no significant differences in tissue biomarkers (H3K18ac, H3K9ac, HDAC3, HDAC6, Ki-67, p21) after multiple comparison adjustment, though within-group comparisons showed favorable changes [28]. This pattern of null between-group results presented alongside favorable within-group analyses is a recurring methodological concern.

### Cognitive Benefits Without Biomarker Changes

A 12-week RCT in 144 healthy older adults showed improvements in processing speed and negative mood, but no significant changes in biomarkers of oxidative stress, inflammation, or neural plasticity [10, 11]. This disconnect between functional improvements and null biomarker results raises questions about whether the cognitive effects are reproducible and mechanistically grounded.

### Schizophrenia: Caveats Under the Headline

The 24-week schizophrenia trial (n=77) showed large effect sizes on negative symptoms (d=0.83-0.86), but notable limitations received less emphasis: the effect was not significant at 12 weeks, the placebo group showed greater improvement in positive symptoms, there was a substantial amisulpride imbalance, no improvement on global clinical impression (CGI), and the tablet contained additional active ingredients [7].

### Publication Bias

A comprehensive analysis of 84 sulforaphane clinical trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov found that only 39 have been published, meaning approximately 50% of registered trials remain unpublished (this figure reflects all registered trials, not only those confirmed as completed) [25]. This raises significant concerns about publication bias, as positive results are more likely to be published than null or negative findings.

### Meta-Analysis of Cardiometabolic Outcomes

A meta-analysis of 10 clinical trials on broccoli sprout supplementation and cardiometabolic health found significant reductions in systolic (-10.9 mmHg) and diastolic (-6.95 mmHg) blood pressure, but only marginally significant changes in blood lipid biomarkers [29]. These effect sizes are notably large -- comparable to antihypertensive medications -- and should be interpreted with caution given the meta-analysis was published in a small journal with limited citations, included only 10 studies, and may overestimate true effects. Even in this area with the most consistent findings, the evidence base remains thin -- only 10 studies over 15 years.

## 7. Dosing Protocols and Practical Guidance

### No Consensus on Optimal Dosing

There is no expert consensus on an optimal sulforaphane dosing protocol. Multiple comprehensive reviews covering over 50 clinical trials conclude that regimens are "variable and inconsistent" and it is impossible to recommend a specific effective dose [4, 5]. Key unresolved questions include:

- **Optimal SFN dose:** Studies range from 50 to 600+ umol/day
- **Glucoraphanin vs. direct SFN delivery:** No clear superiority established
- **Divided vs. once-daily dosing:** Insufficient data
- **Timing with food:** Sparse data
- **Minimum treatment duration:** May be 24 weeks for some indications

### Formulation Comparison

- **Prostaphane/BROQ** (pure stabilized SFN): ~10 mg SFN per pill. Highest bioavailability. Two pills deliver ~20 mg SFN (~120 umol) [15].
- **Avmacol Extra Strength** (GR + myrosinase): ~10.5 mg SFN per pill at approximately 35% bioavailability. Two pills deliver ~24 mg SFN (~136 umol) [7, 15].
- **Glucoraphanin-only products** (e.g., Crucera-SGS): Rely on gut bacterial conversion. Bioavailability unpredictable and dependent on individual gut microbiota [14, 15].
- **Broccoli sprout beverages** (clinical trial preparations): 600 umol GR + 40 umol SFN daily. Achieved largest trial effects [17].
- **Broccoli sprouts** (home-grown): SFN content is approximately 0.425 mg per gram fresh sprouts based on secondary sources, though this conversion factor lacks primary citation and varies with growing conditions [15]. Estimated 100-140g may provide ~40-60 mg SFN, but actual delivery is variable. Cost-effective but carries bacterial contamination risk.

### Food Timing and Preparation

Food timing data is sparse. The bipolar depression protocol specifies taking Avmacol after breakfast with warm water (<=40 degrees Celsius), and the prediabetes trial specifies morning administration suspended in water [30, 14]. No systematic comparison of with-food vs. without-food administration has been published. Cooking destroys myrosinase; raw or lightly steamed sprouts (1-3 minutes) are recommended [15, 18].

### Duration Considerations

The schizophrenia trial is notable for showing that effects only emerged at 24 weeks and were absent at 12 weeks, suggesting prolonged treatment may be necessary for some indications [7]. The cancer systematic review found that longer durations (6-12 months) were more likely to show efficacy signals than short durations (2-8 weeks) [5].

## Conclusions and Recommendations

### What the Evidence Supports

1. **Sulforaphane activates the Nrf2 pathway in humans.** This is the single best-validated mechanism, confirmed across dozens of biomarker trials with consistent pharmacodynamic effects on Phase II enzyme induction, oxidative stress markers, and gene expression.

2. **Bioavailability is achievable but variable.** Formulation matters significantly. Pure stabilized SFN and glucoraphanin-plus-myrosinase products deliver reliable doses; glucoraphanin-only products are unreliable due to gut microbiome-dependent conversion. Interindividual variability in serum levels spans a 6-fold range.

3. **Biomarker effects are reproducible.** The 61% benzene detoxication increase (n=291), 3.1-fold NQO1 induction in skin, AMACR 7-fold downregulation in prostate, and Ki-67 reduction in bronchial tissue represent consistent, measurable pharmacodynamic effects across multiple tissue types and populations.

4. **Short-term safety is acceptable.** Across 8 cancer RCTs, psychiatric RCTs, and metabolic RCTs, sulforaphane is well-tolerated with primarily mild GI side effects. No Grade 3/4 adverse events have been reported.

### What the Evidence Does Not Support

1. **No clinical endpoint evidence exists.** Zero RCTs have demonstrated effects on mortality, disease incidence, or functional decline. The entire clinical evidence base rests on surrogate biomarkers.

2. **Several RCTs have missed primary endpoints.** The prediabetes trial (n=74) and subarachnoid hemorrhage trial (n=105) both failed on their prespecified primary outcomes. The COPD trial showed null effects on Nrf2 itself.

3. **The Nrf2 paradox remains unresolved.** Tumors hijack this same pathway for pro-survival advantage. Direct preclinical evidence shows SFN can promote hepatoma progression in mice with established tumors. The safety of chronic supplementation in cancer patients or those at high cancer risk has not been established.

4. **No consensus dosing exists.** The 50-600 umol/day range across trials, variable formulations, and inconsistent durations prevent evidence-based dose recommendations.

5. **Publication bias is a significant concern.** Of 84 registered clinical trials, only 39 have been published.

### Practical Guidance (With Caveats)

For healthy adults interested in sulforaphane, the evidence most supports:

- **Dietary approach first:** Roughly 100-140g fresh broccoli sprouts daily (raw, not heated) may provide an estimated ~40-60 mg SFN, though actual SFN delivery is highly variable due to interindividual differences in bioavailability (6-fold serum variability documented) and variable glucoraphanin content in home-grown sprouts. This cannot be considered equivalent to HPLC-verified clinical trial preparations. Home-sprouted seeds also carry bacterial contamination risk, particularly concerning for immunocompromised individuals and pregnant women. Cost-effective and avoids supplement quality concerns.
- **If using supplements:** Avmacol has been verified in a published clinical trial [7]. Prostaphane and BROQ are recommended by secondary sources as having the highest bioavailability [15], but direct comparative trial data is limited. Glucoraphanin-only products should be avoided due to unpredictable conversion.
- **Realistic expectations:** Anticipate biomarker-level effects (enhanced detoxication pathways, antioxidant enzyme induction), not clinical disease prevention.
- **Duration matters:** If trialing supplementation, a minimum of 12-24 weeks may be needed to assess individual response, though this timeline is derived from a single psychiatric trial [7] and may not generalize to other indications or healthy populations.
- **Avoid if:** Currently undergoing cancer treatment, have an active tumor (especially with known NRF2/KEAP1 mutations), or are taking immunosuppressive medications. The Nrf2 paradox means the theoretical risks are highest in these populations.

### Key Unresolved Questions

1. Do the reproducible biomarker shifts translate into meaningful reductions in disease incidence over decades?
2. What is the net effect of Nrf2 activation in individuals with subclinical or undiagnosed tumors?
3. Does gut microbiota composition determine who responds, and can non-responders be converted?
4. Is there a dose or duration above which the risks of Nrf2 overactivation outweigh benefits?
5. Will the approximately 45 unpublished completed trials, when eventually released, shift the risk-benefit calculus?

## Methodology

This review was conducted as a structured deep research investigation using systematic search, full-text reading, and evidence synthesis.

**Search strategy:** 51 searches across PubMed, OpenAlex, Tavily, Perplexity, Linkup, Gensee, and Exa databases. Searches were divided into 27 discovery searches (broad topic identification) and 24 recovery searches (targeted source retrieval).

**Source tracking:** 543 total sources tracked (339 academic, 204 web). Sources were distributed across providers: PubMed (154), OpenAlex (185), Linkup (103), Tavily (87), Perplexity (6), Gensee (4), and Exa (4).

**Deep reading:** 20 sources received full-text reading with structured note-taking covering study design, methodology, effect sizes, limitations, and relevance to 7 research questions. An additional 6 sources were assessed at the abstract level with quality ratings.

**Findings:** 73 structured findings were extracted and mapped to 7 research questions covering evidence hierarchy, bioavailability, effect sizes, risks, supplements vs. dietary sources, largest trials and null results, and dosing protocols.

**Quality assurance:** Source quality assessment categorized sources as on-topic with evidence (20), abstract-only relevant (6), degraded/unread (23), and mismatched (3). Reader-validated findings were tracked through a structured handoff process.
