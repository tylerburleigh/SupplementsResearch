Investigating the clinical efficacy, safety and molecular mechanism of sulforaphane in autism spectrum disorder: an integrated study combining meta-analysis, network pharmacology, and computational biology

Download PDF

Abstract
Background
Sulforaphane, a natural antioxidant rich in cruciferous vegetables, has emerged as a promising dietary supplement for autism spectrum disorder (ASD). However, its therapeutic efficacy remains controversial, and the pharmacological mechanisms are not fully elucidated.
Methods
Eligible randomized controlled trials were retrieved from PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases. Review Manager 5.4 was used for meta-analysis and bias risk assessment. Network pharmacology, Mendelian randomization, GEO data analyses, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation were employed to explore the mechanisms of sulforaphane in ASD.
Results
Six trials involving 333 participants were included in the meta-analysis. Pooled results demonstrated that both 4–5 weeks and 8–10 weeks of sulforaphane supplementation significantly decreased the scores on the Social Responsiveness Scale compared to placebo controls. No significant difference was observed in the incidence of adverse events. Network pharmacology identified 10 core targets of sulforaphane in ASD, including AKT1, EGFR, HSP90AA1, SRC, CASP3, STAT1, MAPK1, MMP9, MAPK8, and JAK2. These targets were implicated in the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, Chemokine signaling pathway, Chemical carcinogenesis - reactive oxygen species, TNF signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, mTOR signaling pathway, and IL-17 signaling pathway. Mendelian randomization further revealed an inverse association between STAT1 levels and ASD risk. GEO transcriptomic data provided independent validation for the network pharmacology predictions. The binding energies between sulforaphane and the top 10 core targets are all ≤ -4.0 kcal/mol. Molecular dynamics simulations further validated the stable interaction between MMP-9 and sulforaphane.
Conclusion
Sulforaphane may serve as an efficacious and safe adjunctive therapy for ASD, mediated by its anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory effects along with the modulation of autophagy.

Introduction
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a pervasive neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by social communication impairments and repetitive behaviors, with an estimated global prevalence of 0.72% [
1
]. While behavioral interventions are the primary treatment, they can impose a significant burden on families in terms of time and resources [
2
]. Although, risperidone and aripiprazole are approved in the USA for the management of irritability and agitation in individuals with ASD, their clinical utility is constrained by an increased risk of adverse effects and limited efficacy in addressing core autistic symptoms [
2
,
3
]. Consequently, numerous preclinical and clinical studies are currently underway to identify effective and safe therapeutic agents that target the core symptoms of ASD.
In recent decades, interest in complementary and alternative medicine for ASD, including dietary supplements, has grown significantly. Sulforaphane, an aliphatic isothiocyanate whose precursor, glucoraphanin, is abundant in cruciferous vegetables, has attracted considerable attention as a dietary supplement due to its potential in reducing the risk of neurodegenerative disease, diabetes, cardiometabolic syndrome, and several types of cancer [
4
,
5
,
6
]. Sulforaphane has anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, and neuroprotective properties, which are highly relevant to the therapeutic targets of ASD [
6
]. Beyond these well-documented properties, emerging evidence suggests that sulforaphane may also exert its neuroprotective effects by modulating cellular autophagy [
7
,
8
], a process critical for maintaining neuronal homeostasis in ASD [
9
]. This multifaceted potential has prompted recent studies to explore the effects and safety of sulforaphane supplementation in individuals with ASD.
The first clinical trial reported by Singh et al. [
10
] demonstrated that a majority of participants with ASD receiving sulforaphane showed improvement in social interaction, abnormal behavior, and verbal communication. In contrast, a recent study found no significant clinical improvement in children with ASD treated with sulforaphane [
11
], highlighting ongoing controversy regarding its efficacy. Moreover, the pharmacological mechanisms underlying sulforaphane’s effects in ASD remain understood. To address this gap, this study employed an integrated approach, combining meta-analysis, network pharmacology, Mendelian randomization, GEO data analyses, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy, safety, and underlying pharmacological mechanisms of sulforaphane in ASD.
Methods
Registration and search strategy
The meta-analysis followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis guidelines [
12
] and was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42025635045). We performed a systematic literature search in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library from inception to to February 21, 2025. Screening processes were conducted with the following terms in the title or abstract: (sulforaphane) AND (autism OR autistic OR ASD OR asperger OR “pervasive developmental disorder”). The full search strategy is described in Table
S1
. We included studies published in English without publication year restrictions and screened the reference lists of included articles to identify additional relevant studies. Two authors independently screened the retrieved records, and discrepancies were resolved by consulting with other authors.
Selection criteria
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the efficacy or safety of sulforaphane supplementation in subjects with ASD were eligible for inclusion. The diagnosis of ASD should be according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), the Autism Diagnostic Interview-Revised (ADI-R), or the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule, Second Edition (AODS-2). There were no restrictions regarding age, gender, severity of ASD, or therapeutic doses. The experimental group received sulforaphane alone or sulforaphane combined with other medications, while the control group received a placebo or other medications. Included studies should report at least one outcome measure related to core symptoms of ASD (e.g., ABC and SRS) and/or adverse events. Case reports, conference reports, review studies, protocol designs, and animal/cell experiments were excluded.
Data extraction and quality assessment
Two reviewers independently implemented the information extraction from each study. The extracted information included the first author, year of publication, sample size, gender, age, intervention methods, outcome measures, and assessment points were extracted. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus or consulting with other authors. Quality assessment was performed in accordance with the Cochrane Collaboration Risk of Bias Assessment tool [
13
]. Seven domains of bias were evaluated for each study: sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, outcome data, selective outcome reporting, and other sources of bias.
Data synthesis and statistical analysis
Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.3 software (Cochrane Library Software, Oxford, UK). Treatment effects for continuous outcomes were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). A Hedges’ g effect size of 0.2 − 0.3 was often considered a “small” effect, around 0.5, a “medium” effect, and 0.8 or more, a large effect [
14
]. When the standard deviation (SD) of the mean change was not directly reported, it was imputed using the following formula: SD change = √[(SD baseline^2 + SD final^2) – (2 × 0.5 × SD baseline × SD final)] [
15
]. Relative risk (RR) was calculated to evaluate the rate of adverse events. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the Q-test (with a significance threshold of
p
< 0.10) and quantified by the I
2
statistic. An I
2
value greater than 50% was considered to represent substantial heterogeneity. Accordingly, a random-effects model was applied when significant heterogeneity was present; otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used [
16
]. Planned sensitivity and subgroup analyses were not conducted due to the limited number of included studies. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
Toxicity prediction of sulforaphane
To further evaluate the safety and toxicity profile of sulforaphane, we utilized ProTox 3.0 (
https://tox.charite.de
), a computational platform for chemical toxicity prediction. Using sulforaphane’s SMILES notation as input, the tool generated comprehensive predictions, including organ toxicity, Tox21-nuclear receptor signaling pathways, molecular initiating events, and metabolic pathways.
Screening candidate targets of Sulforaphane and ASD
The Canonical SMILES and two-dimensional structure in SDF format of sulforaphane were retrieved from PubChem (
https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/
). These data were subsequently utilized for candidate target prediction in SwissTargetPrediction (
http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/
) and PharmMapper (
https://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/
). Disease genes associated with ASD were identified using GeneCards (
https://www.genecards.org/
), OMIM (
https://omim.org/
), DrugBank (
https://go.drugbank.com/about
), and TTD (
https://db.idrblab.net/ttd/
). Following the removal of duplicates, all gene symbols were standardized using the UniProt database (
https://www.uniprot.org/
). The Venn diagram was used to identify common targets between sulforaphane and ASD (
https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/static/others/jvenn/example.html
).
Construction of the protein-protein interactions (PPI) network
Based on the common targets, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING (
https://string-db.org/
) with the interaction score ≥ 0.7 (high confidence). Disconnected nodes were hidden in the network. We used Cytoscape 3.10.3 software and its network analyzer tool to construct the PPI network, and the top 10 core targets were screened out according to the degree value (the number of connections of a node).
Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses
GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted based on the Metascape database (
http://metascape.org/
), with
Homo sapiens
set as the species. The top 10 significantly enriched biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), as well as the 20 significantly enriched KEGG pathways were identified (Min Overlap = 3,
P
< 0.05, Min Enrichment = 1.5). Visualization of the enrichment results was carried out using an online plotting tool (
http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn
).
Mendelian randomization analysis
We conducted a two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis to investigate the genetic causal relationships between the AKT1 (eqtl-a-ENSG00000142208), HSP90AA1 (eqtl-a-ENSG00000080824), SRC (eqtl-a-ENSG00000197122), CASP3 (prot-a−364), and STAT1 (eqtl-a-ENSG00000115415) and ASD (finn-b-KRA_PSY_AUTISM). Instrumental variables were selected as single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) based on the following criteria: genome-wide significant SNPs (
p
< 1 × 10
− 6
), LD independence (r2 < 0.001, distance > 10,000 kb), and F-statistics > 10 to ensure strong instruments. Causal estimates were primarily derived from the inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median, MR-Egger regression, simple mode, and weighted mode. A p-value below 0.05 was considered suggestive of evidence for a potential causal effect. To validate the robustness of the findings, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses, including Cochran’s Q test for heterogeneity, the MR-Egger intercept test for assessing horizontal pleiotropy, and leave-one-out analysis. All analyses were performed using the TwoSampleMR package in R.
Expression validation of core target genes based on the GEO database
The GSE18123 dataset (GPL570) was retrieved from the GEO database, which comprised 66 male ASD children and 33 male typically developing children. All samples were derived from peripheral blood. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the GEO2R online tool provided by the GEO database, and the expression values of 10 core target genes were calculated.
Molecular docking
Protein structures of 10 core targets were collected from the RCSB PDB database (
https://www.rcsb.org/
). The 3D structure of sulforaphane was downloaded from the PubChem database in SDF format. The molecular docking was performed based on the CB-Dock-2 tool (
https://cadd.labshare.cn/cb-dock2/php/index.php
). CB-Dock-2 is a protein-ligand docking method that automatically identifies binding sites, calculates the center and size, customizes the docking box size based on the query ligands, and then performs molecular docking using AutoDock Vina [
17
]. CB-Dock-2 was also used to visualize the docking results.
Molecular dynamics simulation
Molecular dynamics simulations of the sulforaphane-MMP9 complex were performed using Gromacs 2022. Force field parameters were generated using the Gromacs pdb2gmx tool and the AutoFF web server. The AMBER14sb force field was applied to the receptor protein, while the GAFF2 force field was used for the ligand. The system was solvated in a cubic box with TIP3P water molecules extending 1 nm from the solute. Ions were added using the gmx genion tool to neutralize the system. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method with a cutoff distance of 1 nm. All bonds were constrained using the SHAKE algorithm, and the integration step was set to 1 fs employing the Verlet leapfrog algorithm. Prior to production MD, the system underwent energy minimization involving 3000 steps of steepest descent followed by 2000 steps of conjugate gradient optimization. The minimization procedure was performed sequentially by constraining the solute while minimizing water molecules, then constraining counterions, and finally minimizing the entire system without constraints. The simulation was conducted under NPT ensemble conditions at 310 K for 100 ns. During the simulation, the gromacs tools g_rms, g_rmsf, g_hbond, g_gyrate, and g_sasa were used to calculate the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bonds (HBonds), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent accessible surface area (SASA), respectively. The molecular mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann surface area (MM-PBSA) binding free energy of the complex was computed using the g_mmpbsa package.

A 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was performed to investigate the binding of sulforaphane to MMP-9. RMSD serves as a key indicator of conformational stability. As shown in Fig.

The Rg, which reflects the overall compactness of the protein structure, remained stable throughout the simulation (Fig.

Discussion
To the best of our knowledge, this work represents the first comprehensive integration of meta-analysis, network pharmacology, Mendelian randomization, GEO data analyses, and computational docking to systematically evaluate the efficacy, safety and mechanism of sulforaphane in ASD. Overall, our meta-analysis of 333 ASD participants indicates that sulforaphane may improve social functioning with no significant adverse events. Network pharmacology results indicate that sulforaphane may exert its anti-autistic effects by modulating multiple targets and pathways, which is consistently supported by Mendelian randomization, GEO dataset mining, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulation.
The first key finding of this study is the time-dependent efficacy of sulforaphane. Sulforaphane supplementation at 4–5 weeks and 8–10 weeks significantly reduces the SRS scores in ASD patients, with the effect size increasing as treatment duration extends. Similarly, an open-label trial involving 15 children with ASD revealed that three months of sulforaphane supplementation resulted in a further significant reduction in SRS scores compared to one month [
22
]. However, our meta-analysis revealed that this effect peaked at 18 weeks and subsequently declined, with no significant benefits sustained during the 18- to 36-week treatment period. As noted by Zimmerman et al. [
21
], this attenuation may reflect treatment fatigue or a regression toward baseline in long-term studies. In addition, placebo effects may also influence the reliability of rating scales assessed by parents or caregivers. With regard to the ABC scale, we only found that 8–15 weeks of sulforaphane supplementation significantly decreased the inappropriate speech subscore with a considerable effect size. Overall, the current findings provide preliminary support for the short-term positive effects of sulforaphane supplementation on social functioning in ASD.
In terms of safety, our meta-analysis revealed no significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between the two groups. To date, across seven clinical studies involving individuals with ASD, no serious adverse events have been reported at varying doses of sulforaphane supplementation [
11
,
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
23
,
24
]. Our toxicity predictions also suggest that sulforaphane may have low systemic toxicity. Bent et al. [
22
] reported a single instance of ruptured appendix during the trial, though it was deemed to be unrelated to the sulforaphane supplementation. The most frequently reported adverse reactions in both groups across existing studies were gastrointestinal symptoms. A study conducted on human stomach mucosa cells demonstrated that sulforaphane exhibited protective effects against induced oxidative stress at low concentrations, whereas high concentrations led to increased apoptosis and reduced cell viability [
25
]. It is noteworthy that in the study by Bent et al. [
22
], sulforaphane was administered in the form of its precursor at a relatively higher dose (222–555 µmol/day) to school-age children with ASD. In addition, Singh et al. [
10
] reported that two ASD participants experienced single unprovoked seizures during sulforaphane treatment. Notably, this study administered a higher dose of sulforaphane (50–150 µmol/day for 18 weeks) compared to other studies. A previous toxicity study demonstrated that sulforaphane exhibits proconvulsant effects at high doses (200 mg/kg) in mice, significantly decreasing the threshold for epileptic seizures [
26
]. Accumulating evidence indicates that low concentrations of sulforaphane can protect against oxidative stress and reactive oxygen species formation, whereas high concentrations of sulforaphane exhibit cytotoxic effects by inducing DNA damage responses, altering gene expression, and disrupting the cell cycle [
25
,
27
,
28
]. While current evidence indicates that sulforaphane is a promising and safe therapeutic option for individuals with ASD, further research is warranted to thoroughly evaluate the risks and benefits of high-dose sulforaphane, particularly in vulnerable pediatric populations.
To date, the potential mechanism of sulforaphane in ASD remains incompletely understood. In this network pharmacology study, 10 core therapeutic targets of sulforaphane were identified, including AKT1, EGFR, HSP90AA1, SRC, CASP3, STAT1, MAPK1, MMP-9, MAPK8, and JAK2, all of which are strongly associated with the pathogenesis of ASD. For example, AKT phosphorylates several targets to transduce growth, proliferation, and survival signaling. Enhanced AKT activity is a significant contributor to neuroinflammation and neuronal autophagy in ASD [
29
]. A comprehensive meta-analysis of ASD animal models revealed significantly elevated p-AKT protein expression in cortical tissues [
30
]. Our analysis of the GEO dataset further corroborated this finding, demonstrating markedly increased AKT1 expression levels in peripheral blood samples from ASD subjects. Previous in vivo pharmacological studies have revealed that sulforaphane treatment effectively suppresses Akt phosphorylation/activation [
31
,
32
]. STAT1 is an ASD-associated gene capable of responding to cytokines and growth factors. Our Mendelian randomization analysis revealed a significant inverse correlation between STAT1 levels and ASD risk. Notably, sulforaphane treatment was found to enhance STAT1 phosphorylation, suggesting its potential therapeutic mechanism [
33
]. Accumulating evidence from numerous ASD animal models and human studies has demonstrated elevated expression/activity of caspase-3 [
34
,
35
]. Our Mendelian randomization and GEO dataset analyses further support a potential role of CASP3 in the pathogenesis of ASD. Sulforaphane has been shown to exert neuroprotective effects by suppressing caspase-3 activation, thereby inhibiting neuronal apoptosis [
7
,
36
]. Both MAPK1 and MAPK8 are key regulators of autophagy and inflammation [
37
]. The MAPK/ERK signaling pathway contributes to the pathogenesis of ASD through the disruption of autophagy, which in turn affects the density of dendritic spines [
38
]. The inhibition of MAPK1 reverses the elevated levels of p-CRMP2 in the offspring of ASD model mice, thereby preventing excessive synaptic pruning and social deficits [
39
]. MAPK8 mediates neuroinflammatory responses in an immune-dysregulated ASD subgroup [
40
]. RNA-seq analysis of hippocampal postmortem brain samples revealed a significant increase in MMP9 expression in male children with ASD compared to healthy controls [
41
]. MMP9 activation-mediated release of inflammatory cytokines represents one of the key neuroinflammatory mechanisms in ASD and exacerbates blood-brain barrier disruption [
42
,
43
]. As a potent MMP-9 inhibitor, sulforaphane has been shown to reduce MMP-9 expression levels in cerebral ischemia and encephalomyelitis, thereby attenuating neuroinflammation and promoting blood-brain barrier repair [
44
,
45
]. Our prediction results align with previous findings that sulforaphane exhibits high blood-brain barrier permeability [
46
]. This implies that orally administered sulforaphane can deliver effective concentrations to sites of brain pathology, thereby forming the material basis for its effects in suppressing neuroinflammation and decreasing blood-brain barrier permeability.
GO enrichment analysis revealed that the key BP terms were primarily associated with response to xenobiotic stimuli, response to oxidative stress, response to nutrient levels, and the regulation of chemical synaptic transmission. The predominant CC terms were related to synaptic structures, while the most significantly enriched MF terms involved protein kinase activity, oxidoreductase activity, protein kinase binding, and kinase regulator activity. Moreover, the term “chemical carcinogenesis - reactive oxygen species” was significantly enriched in our KEGG enrichment analysis. These functional enrichment analyses collectively indicate that the oxidative stress response is a key pathway implicated in sulforaphane’s mechanism of action against ASD. Oxidative stress and redox imbalance induced by elevated levels of reactive oxygen species are established contributors to the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying ASD [
47
]. As a natural antioxidant, sulforaphane can alleviate oxidative stress in both the periphery and brain of mouse models of ASD by upregulating the expression of enzymatic antioxidants, including, superoxide dismutase 1, glutathione peroxidase 1, and glutathione reductase, as well as decreasing the levels of lipid peroxides [
48
]. Clinical evidence demonstrates that sulforaphane supplementation can significantly decrease the plasma redox ratio of free and total glutathione in individuals with ASD to alleviate oxidative stress [
21
]. Sulforaphane can also attenuate valproic acid-induced reactive oxygen species production in human neural progenitor cells and protect developing neural networks from valproic acid-induced synaptic alterations [
49
]. Together, the aforementioned findings further substantiate our predicted gene function annotations, which implicated sulforaphane in the regulation of oxidative stress in ASD.
Sulforaphane is a well-characterized and potent inducer of Nrf2, a redox-sensitive transcription factor. The canonical activation mechanism involves sulforaphane modifying key cysteine residues on Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1), the primary negative regulator of Nrf2. This modification disrupts the Keap1-Nrf2 complex, preventing Nrf2 ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Subsequently, Nrf2 accumulates and translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to the Antioxidant Response Element (ARE), initiating the transcription of a battery of cytoprotective genes responsible for counteracting inflammation and oxidative stress. Furthermore, the transcriptional repressor BACH1 competes with Nrf2 for binding at ARE sites [
50
,
51
,
52
]. Dysregulation of the Nrf2-Keap1-BACH1 axis has been implicated in ASD [
53
]. Consistent with this, recent evidence demonstrates that sulforaphane treatment ameliorates core autism-like behaviors, which are correlated with increased Nrf2 expression, subsequent upregulation of downstream antioxidants, and a concomitant reduction in oxidative stress markers [
48
,
49
,
54
]. These findings are consistent with our functional enrichment analyses, which identified “response to oxidative stress” and “chemical carcinogenesis - reactive oxygen species” as core pathways associated with sulforaphane treatment in ASD.
In the KEGG enrichment analysis, Chemokine signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, Th17 cell differentiation, TNF signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and mTOR signaling pathway were significantly enriched, thus highlighting the involvement of inflammation and autophagy in sulforaphane’s mechanism of action. Chemokines are a family of small proteins that mediate neuroinflammatory responses in ASD by binding to their receptors. The dysregulation of chemokine and chemokine receptor production has been observed in both experimental ASD models and individuals with ASD [
55
,
56
]. IL-17 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine characteristically secreted by Th17 cells and plays a critical role in mediating neuroinflammation in ASD [
55
]. Notably, a recent study demonstrated that sulforaphane ameliorates autism-like symptoms in BTBR mice by reducing expression levels of IL-17 and STAT3 [
57
]. STAT3 is a key transcription factor responsible for the differentiation of Th17 cells, indicating that sulforaphane may alleviate inflammation and immune responses in ASD by suppressing Th17-related signaling pathways [
57
]. Substantial evidence indicates that levels of the pro-inflammatory marker TNF-α are significantly elevated in the peripheral blood of individuals with ASD [
58
,
59
]. Two weeks of sulforaphane supplementation decreased mRNA levels of TNF-α in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from individuals with ASD [
23
]. Furthermore, upregulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway is involved in the neuroinflammation and neuronal autophagy of ASD [
29
,
60
]. Previous results indicated that sulforaphane could inhibit the activity of the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, possibly in an NRF2-independent manner [
61
,
62
]. Correspondingly, mTOR inhibition can enhance PI3K/AKT/mTOR-mediated autophagic activity and improve social interaction in the ASD rat models [
63
]. MAPK signaling serves as a potential pathway for autophagy regulation and can activate autophagy as a downstream effector of reactive oxygen species [
64
,
65
]. The activation of the MAPK pathway is closely implicated in the pathogenesis and disease severity of ASD [
66
,
67
]. Sulforaphane regulates autophagy via dual inhibition of oxidative stress and the MAPK signaling pathway [
68
,
69
,
70
]. All such findings indicated that sulforaphane might be a promising treatment for ASD by targeting the inflammation-related and autophagy-related pathways.
This study investigated the effects of sulforaphane on ASD by integrating meta-analysis, network pharmacology, Mendelian randomization, and computational biology methods, thereby providing new insights for further studies into its anti-autistic mechanisms. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The primary limitations of this meta-analysis encompassed a relatively small sample size, heterogeneity in the ages of participants, and inconsistent therapeutic dosage of sulforaphane. The limited number of included studies was inadequate for the dependable implementation of sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis. Publication bias was not assessed with a funnel plot or Egger’s test because the meta-analysis included fewer than 10 studies. Although the included studies exhibited a relatively high methodological quality, the number of studies investigating any outcome was limited. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, further experimental investigation is warranted to validate the potential pharmacological mechanisms of sulforaphane against ASD.
Conclusion
In conclusion, this study suggests the potential of sulforaphane as an effective adjunctive therapy to improve social function in ASD. Our findings support the safety of oral sulforaphane supplementation in children and adolescents with ASD. Furthermore, sulforaphane may exert its therapeutic effects through anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, as well as the modulation of autophagy, by targeting multiple pathways.
