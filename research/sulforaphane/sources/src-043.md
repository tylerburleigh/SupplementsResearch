Abstract
Introduction Sulforaphane, an isothiocyanate derived from cruciferous vegetables (e.g., broccoli sprouts), has been 
explored for its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. This is the first systematic review and meta-analysis to 
explore the therapeutic potentials of sulforaphane in schizophrenia.
Methods We searched PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Central for studies from inception to April 
2025. We included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the efficacy of sulforaphane in schizophrenia. 
The primary outcomes were changes in the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) total and its subscales. 
Secondary outcomes included cognitive measures, metabolic markers, and safety.
Results Four RCTs with 369 schizophrenia patients were included. Sulforaphane did not significantly improve PANSS 
total or positive symptom scores at the latest follow-up (ranging from 24 weeks to 18 weeks) or at a consistent 
12-week time point. However, a modest improvement in negative symptoms was found at 12 week- time point (MD= 
-1.06; 95% CI: -1.95 to -0.16; p = 0.02), which was not maintained at the latest follow-up. General psychopathology 
scores improved significantly (MD= -1.5; 95% CI: -2.78 to -0.23; p = 0.02). No cognitive benefits were observed. 
Sulforaphane led to significant reductions in metabolic markers, including LDL, triglycerides, and cholesterol. 
Discontinuation rates were lower in the sulforaphane group (RR = 0.68; 95% CI: 0.49 to 0.95; p = 0.02).
Conclusion The study provides initial insights into sulforaphane’s potential therapeutic effects in schizophrenia, 
showing modest improvements in general psychopathology and negative symptoms, with favorable metabolic 
changes and lower discontinuation rates. Due to limited data and heterogeneity, the study findings should be 
interpreted with caution.

Introduction
Schizophrenia is one of the most prevalent long-term 
disabling psychiatric disorders, affecting around 0.7% to 
1% of the general population [ 1]. It involves two main 
psychiatric manifestations: positive and negative symp -
toms. The positive symptoms manifest as hallucinations, 
delusions, and disorganized speech or behavior, while 
the negative symptoms consist of cognitive impairment 
with reduced motivation and expressiveness factors that 
affect memory and mental processing [ 2, 3]. In addi -
tion to these symptoms, schizophrenia involves broader 
symptom domains such as cognitive deficits and depres -
sive symptoms [ 4, 5]. Schizophrenia roughly disrupts 
daily life, with around 24–50% of individuals experienc -
ing suicidal ideation and about 20–40% making suicide 
attempts [6]. Patients with schizophrenia have a mortal -
ity rate of 2-3-fold compared to healthy people, with a 
reduced expected life of 10–20 years [ 7].
The main treatment guidelines emphasize the use of 
first-generation antipsychotics, such as haloperidol and 
chlorpromazine, as well as second-generation agents like 
quetiapine and olanzapine [ 8]. There are also psycho -
social interventions, such as psychoeducation therapy, 
family interventions, and cognitive-behavioral therapy, 
which act as add-on treatments due to their beneficial 
effects [ 2, 8]. The main goal of antipsychotics is restor -
ing the patient’s normal daily functioning, followed by 
maintenance doses of therapy to prevent the risk of hos -
pitalization and improve quality of life [ 2]. However, cur-
rent pharmacotherapies have shown a modest effect in 
handling cognitive impairment and negative symptoms, 
urging further investigation of adjunctive agents [ 9, 10]. 
Also, approximately 30% of patients undergo treatment 
resistance in addition to the different side effects, such as 
extrapyramidal symptoms, sedation, and various meta -
bolic changes [11– 13].
Sulforaphane, a compound derived from crucifer -
ous vegetables, has recently been proposed as a poten -
tial therapeutic agent owing to its antioxidant and 
anti-inflammatory properties [ 14]. The primary mecha -
nism involves the activation of nuclear factor erythroid 
2–related factor 2 (Nrf2), which regulates the cellular 
antioxidant response by enhancing glutathione expres -
sion, an essential antioxidant whose depletion is impli -
cated in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia [ 15, 16]. 
Additionally, it inhibits neuroinflammatory pathways 
by mitigating the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines 
[17]. According to a preclinical study by Shirai et al. 2012, 
sulforaphane has been proposed to have antipsychotic 
activity in an animal model of schizophrenia due to the 
multidimensional mechanisms of action [18].
A single-arm trial by Shiina (2015) involving 10 
patients with schizophrenia found that sulforaphane did 
not improve positive or negative symptoms but showed 
potential to improve cognitive function [ 19]. Several ran-
domized controlled trials (RCTs) estimated the efficacy 
of sulforaphane for patients with schizophrenia with 
conflicting results [ 20– 23]. Dickerson (2021) found no 
significant effects of sulforaphane on any clinical out -
comes [23]. Hei (2022) also found no significant improve-
ment in overall symptoms but reported improvements in 
specific cognitive domains, including working memory, 
reasoning, and verbal learning [ 22]. Both Huang (2025) 
and Chen (2025) found that sulforaphane significantly 
reduced PANSS negative symptom scores but with no 
significant effects on cognitive outcomes [ 20, 21]. A 
recent meta-analysis in autism by Guo et al. showed 
that sulforaphane significantly improved irritability and 
hyperactivity symptoms in autistic patients [ 24]. Several 
nutraceuticals and phytoceuticals have been explored 
as adjunctive treatments for schizophrenia. A recent 
network meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 22 
interventions and reported benefits for some, such as citi-
coline, N-acetylcysteine, and sarcosine [25]. Sulforaphane 
was included in this network but represented by only one 
RCT, limiting confidence in its estimated efficacy. So, to 
resolve this discord, we conducted this systematic review 
and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials RCTs 
to assess the efficacy of sulforaphane as an adjunctive 
therapy for patients with schizophrenia. To the best of 
the author’s knowledge, no previous systematic review 
and meta-analysis provide precise evidence regarding its 
therapeutic potential and safety profile that could poten -
tially improve the overall treatment outcome better than 
the traditional antipsychotics.
Methods
Protocol
We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic 
Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA statement) guide -
lines when reporting this study [ 26]. This manuscript 
was conducted in adherence to the Cochrane Handbook 
of Systematic Reviews of Interventions [ 27]. All authors 
worked independently at every stage of the study. This 
study was registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024603653).
Search strategy and data source
We conducted a comprehensive search in April 2025 
across the following databases: PubMed, Web of Science, 
Scopus, and the Cochrane Library for eligible studies. 
The search approach used MeSH (medical subject head -
ings) and their synonyms. The search terms used were 
“Sulforaphane” AND “Schizophrenia” . The search terms 
were combined using Boolean operators to ensure com -
prehensive retrieval of relevant studies. Additionally, 
Google Scholar results and references of included stud -
ies were manually screened for any possible studies to

Eligibility criteria
Studies were selected based on predefined inclusion and 
exclusion criteria. Studies satisfying the following cri -
teria were included: [ 1] Population: patients diagnosed 
with schizophrenia according to validated diagnostic 
tools (e.g., DSM or ICD criteria) [ 2], Intervention: sul -
foraphane as an adjunct to antipsychotics [ 3], Compara-
tor: placebo adjunctive to antipsychotics or alone [ 4], 
Outcomes: any efficacy or safety outcomes related to 
schizophrenia [ 5], Study design: randomized controlled 
trials (RCTs). Studies were excluded if they were: [1] case 
reports, case series, observational studies, and confer -
ence abstracts [ 2], animal studies or in vitro studies [ 3], 
reviews, book chapters, theses, letters, and overlapped 
datasets, and [4] non-English studies.
Screening and study selection process
Two independent authors (NF and YK) used Rayyan for 
semi-automated screening of the literature search results 
[28]. Studies were screened in two phases; we screened 
the titles/abstracts for potential clinical studies in the 
first phase. In the second phase, we retrieved the full-
text articles of the selected abstracts for further eligibility 
screening. Any disagreements between the two reviewers 
were resolved through consultation with the first author 
(OK).
Data extraction
Data extraction was independently performed by two 
authors (AS and OY) using a standardized data extrac -
tion form developed in Microsoft Excel. Extracted data 
included study characteristics, such as author(s), year of 
publication, country and locations, study design, sample 
size, inclusion criteria, treatment protocol, comparator, 
outcome measures, and key findings of included studies. 
The Second sheet included baseline characteristics of the 
included studies such as, age, sex, and duration of illness. 
A third outcome sheet was specifically designed to col -
lect the outcomes of each study. Any disagreements in 
data extraction were resolved by consensus or consulta -
tion with the first author (OK).
Risk of bias assessment
The quality of the studies included was evaluated by two 
independent authors (AS and MM) using the Cochrane 
risk of bias assessment tool for RCTs version two (ROB2) 
[29]. Bias potential was evaluated across five domains, 
including (1) bias due to randomization process (2) bias 
due to deviations from intended interventions (3) Bias 
due to missing outcome data (4) Bias in measurement of 
the outcome (5) Bias in selection of the reported result. 
The overall judgment for each domain was classified into 
one of three levels: low, moderate, or high risk of bias. 
Conflict between authors was resolved through mutual 
agreement or by consulting the primary investigator 
(OK).
Outcome definition
The primary efficacy outcomes included changes in the 
total Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) 
score and its subscales: PANSS Positive, PANSS Nega -
tive, General Psychopathology, PANSS Depression, and 
PANSS Cognition. PANSS is a 30-item clinician-tool 
used to assess positive, negative, and general psychopa -
thology symptoms in schizophrenia, and it is the gold 
standard for measuring treatment response [ 30]. Sec -
ondary outcomes were the effects of sulforaphane on 
cognitive assessments, the metabolic markers, discontin -
uation rates, and adverse events. Cognitive function was 
assessed using the MATRICS Consensus Cognitive Bat -
tery (MCCB), assessing total score and specific domains 
(e.g., verbal learning, spatial working memory, reason -
ing/problem solving) [ 31]. Metabolic markers, including 
low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein 
(HDL), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and 
fasting glucose (FG), were measured using standard labo-
ratory assays in fasting blood samples.
Data synthesis and statistical analysis
We analyzed data using RevMan software, version 5.4. 
For continuous data, the mean difference (MD) between 
the two groups, along with its standard deviation (SD) 
and total number of patients, were pooled using the 
inverse variance method with a random effect model. 
For dichotomous data, the frequency of events and the 
total number of patients were pooled as risk ratios (RR) 
between the two groups, also using the inverse vari -
ance method with a random effect model. Heterogene -
ity was assessed using the chi-square test and I² statistic; 
a p-value from the chi-square test < 0.1 and I² >50% was 
considered indicative of significant heterogeneity. We 
conducted the analysis based on the primary endpoint 
of each included studies and a consistent time across all 
studies. Subgroup analyses were conducted according 
to the reported daily sulforaphane dose (~ 66 µmol/day, 
~ 100 µmol/day, and 136 µmol/day). Sensitivity analysis 
was conducted by excluding one study in each scenario to 
explore sources of heterogeneity.
Publication bias
In accordance with Egger et al., we did not assess publi -
cation bias using Egger’s test for funnel plot asymmetry. 
The Egger test requires a minimum of 10 included studies 
to be applicable [32].

Results
Study selection
A systematic search of relevant databases identified 
a total of 230 articles. After removing duplicates, we 
excluded 152 studies during the initial screening of titles 
and abstracts as they did not fulfill the pre-defined inclu -
sion criteria. This process resulted in 22 studies being 
selected for full-text review. Upon further evaluation, 
17 studies were excluded for specific reasons, which 
are detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram Fig.  1. Con -
sequently, 4 studies met all eligibility criteria and were 
included in the final meta-analysis.
Summary and baseline characteristics
Four RCTs comprising 369 schizophrenia patients were 
included in the study. Three studies were conducted 
in China [ 20– 22] and one study in the USA [ 23]. The 
treatment duration varied across studies: two studies 
lasted 24 weeks, one 22 weeks, and one 18 weeks. The 
mean age of participants ranged from 23 years to 45 
years. Sulforaphane and placebo were adjunct to antipsy -
chotics in all included studies. The doses of sulforaphane 
administered across the studies ranged from 66 µmol to 
136 µmol. Baseline characteristics and a summary of the 
studies included are shown in Tables 1 and 2.
Risk of bias assessment
A summary and graph of the risk of bias in our included 
studies are shown in (Fig.  2). Results of the risk of bias 
assessment showed that the quality of the included stud -
ies ranged from low risk to some concerns. Three stud -
ies yielded an overall low risk of bias. However, Huang et 
al. demonstrated some concerns in domain 2 (bias due to 
deviations from intended intervention).

Results of the efficacy outcomes
Psychiatric outcomes
Four studies, including 369 patients, reported the effi -

Results of safety and metabolic outcomes
Metabolic outcomes
Three studies reported the metabolic outcomes. Sulfora -
phane led to a statistically significant reduction in the 
following metabolic markers: LDL (MD= -0.25, 95% CI 
[-0.42, -0.07], P-value = 0.005, I2 = 0%), triglycerides (MD= 
-0.25, 95% CI [-0.42, -0.08], P-value = 0.003, I 2 = 0%), 
and cholesterol (MD= -0.35, 95% CI [-0.57, -0.12], 
P-value = 0.002, I2 = 0%). No significant differences were 
observed regarding HDL (MD= -0.07, 95% CI [-0.14, 
0.01], P-value = 0.07, I 2 = 0%) or glucose levels (MD= 
-0.04, 95% CI [-0.24, 0.16], P-value = 0.68, I2 = 0%). There 
was no detectable heterogeneity in the pooled analysis of 
all the markers (I2 = 0%), as shown in Fig. 4.
Discontinuation causes
All four included studies reported all causes of discontin -
uation. The discontinuation rate was significantly lower 
in the sulforaphane group compared to placebo group 
(RR = 0.68, 95% CI [0.49, 0.95], P-value = 0.02, I 2 = 0%), 
with no detectable heterogeneity, as shown in Fig. 5.

Adverse events
The studies included did not provide sufficient data 
regarding adverse events to be pooled in a meta-analysis. 
However, all included studies reported that sulforaphane 
was well-tolerated. In Dickerson et al., there were 2 seri -
ous adverse events in the sulforaphane group involving 
2 patients, and 6 serious adverse events in the placebo 
group involving 5 participants. Hei et al. reported no sig -
nificant differences between sulforaphane and placebo 
on the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS), 
Barnes Akathisia Rating Scale (BARS), and Simpson–
Angus Scale (SAS), and most items on the Systematic 
Assessment for Treatment Emergent Events (SAFTEE). 
Huang et al. found that sulforaphane was well tolerated 
and compared to placebo, as evaluated by the Treat -
ment Emergent Symptom Scale (TESS). Similarly, Chen 
et al. reported no significant differences in TESS scores 
between sulforaphane and placebo.
Discussion
Our meta-analysis showed no significant difference 
between sulforaphane and placebo in improving PANSS 
total score and PANSS positive subscale with no signifi -
cant heterogeneity in 12 weeks and 24 weeks. No individ-
ual study reported any meaningful improvement in either 
PANSS total or positive symptom scores. This lack of 
effect was consistent among all included studies despite 
variation in baseline PANSS total and positive scores and 
clinical presentation among the included studies. The 
baseline PANSS positive subscale ranged from 22 in the 
highest sample size to 12 in the lowest. This raises con -
cerns about the potential of sulforaphane to exert any 
therapeutic benefit on positive symptoms.
The pooled analysis of the PANSS negative subscale 
in 12 weeks favored sulforaphane over placebo. How -
ever, the pooled analysis of the PANSS negative sub -
scale in 24 weeks showed no significant difference. The 
analysis came with heterogeneity, which was resolved by 
leave-one-out sensitivity analysis when excluding Huang 
(2025). Huang et al. (2025) was the most significant study 
regarding the reduction of the PANSS negative subscale. 
This may be explained by using a high dose of 136 umol 
compared to other studies and came with a larger sam -
ple size than Chen (2025), which used the same dose. 
Also, the study was on patients who were diagnosed with 
schizophrenia for 10 years or less with prominent nega -
tive symptoms, and most of the participants had negative 
symptom scores higher than positive symptoms. On the 
contrary, Chen (2025) included patients who were diag -
nosed for 10 to 30 years, Hei (2022) included early-stage 
patients, and Dickerson (2021) was focused on schizo -
phrenia or schizoaffective disorder patients with moder -
ate psychotic symptoms. Dickerson (2021) was the study 
reporting the least improvement in PANSS negative 
symptoms score. The mean age of participants in Dicker-
son et al. ranged from 42 to 45 years while in Huang the 
mean age was around 23 years. Dickerson et al. study had 
higher baseline PANSS total scores and PANSS positive 
subscale than Huang et al. These variations demonstrate 
the need to identify the patients which may benefit the 
most from sulforaphane augmentation.
Dose-based subgroup analyses provided preliminary 
insights into a potential dose-response relationship. At 
the primary endpoint, sulforaphane at lower doses (66 
and ~ 100 µmol/day) did not show a statistically signifi -
cant improvement in PANSS total score, but with the 
highest dose (136 µmol/day) a trend toward improve -
ment was observed that did not reach statistical signifi -
cance. Regarding the PANSS negative subscale, at the 
primary endpoint a borderline improvement ( P = 0.05) 
was observed with the 136 µmol/day dose, and a sta -
tistically significant improvement was observed at the 
12-week analysis, while the 100 µmol/day dose showed 
no effect. However, these observations should be inter -
preted with caution due to the limited number of trials 
and the small sample size. In an open-label study where 
45 patients with schizophrenia received a high dose 
of sulforaphane 507.4 µmol/day, sulforaphane signifi -
cantly improved the PANSS total and the PANSS nega -
tive subscale [ 33]. From a pharmacokinetic perspective,

sulforaphane is formed by the hydrolysis of its precur -

microbiota [ 34]. It is metabolized via the mercapturic 
acid pathway, undergoing conjugation with glutathione 
and subsequent excretion as N-acetylcysteine derivatives 
in urine [ 35]. The absorption of sulforaphane is variable, 
ranging from ~ 1% to 40% among human volunteers [34]. 
Recent work by Zhu (2025) showed that combining glu -
coraphanin-rich extract with exogenous myrosinase and 
encapsulating it resulted in a conversion efficiency of ~ 
72.1%, versus ~ 29.3% for unencapsulated delivery [ 36]. 
These pharmacological characteristics demonstrate that 
dose escalation alone may have limited benefit. Future 
studies should therefore not only evaluate higher doses 
but also consider optimized formulations to improve bio-
availability while maintaining safety and tolerability.
The pooled analysis of general psychophysiology at 
the end of the study favored sulforaphane over placebo 
and came with no significant heterogeneity. Regarding 
cognition, even a previous study by Nouchi et al. (2021) 
reported a positive effect of sulforaphane on the cogni -
tion domain [ 37]. Our pooled effect of sulforaphane 
failed to exhibit significant improvements in cognitive 
outcomes, including both the overall MCCB total score 
and individual cognitive domains such as verbal learn -
ing, spatial working memory, and reasoning/problem-
solving. However, Hei (2022) showed improvement in 
working memory, attention, and processing speed. This 
study came with the largest sample size and was in early-
episode schizophrenia patients.
Although most individual studies included in our 
meta-analysis did not report significant improvements 
in metabolic markers, our pooled analysis showed that 
sulforaphane has a significant reduction in LDL, triglyc -
erides, and cholesterol. The reduction can be attributed 
to the pathway of sulforaphane, which is a phytochemi -
cal bioactive substance [ 38]. It is reported as an activator 
of nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor (Nrf2), which 
protects cells against oxidative damage by activation of 
Nrf2. Sulforaphane enhances mitochondrial function, as 
suggested by the decrease in ROS, the increase in mito -
chondrial membrane potential, and the upregulation of 
ATP production [ 39, 40], which contribute to amelio -
rating aberrant lipid metabolism and may contribute to 
reducing weight and controlling diabetes mellitus and 
hyperlipidemia, which are more prominent risks of some 
of antipsychotics [41, 42]. However, while the reductions 
were statistically significant, the magnitude of the change 
was modest. The limited clinical significance raises con -
cerns about whether these changes may translate to 
cardiovascular benefits. Further studies are needed to 
determine the metabolic benefits of sulforaphane.
The discontinuation was less in the sulforaphane group 
compared to the placebo group. The discontinuation 
causes were due to the withdrawal of consent, poor com -
pliance with regular antipsychotics, and some changed 
to another medication. However, some studies did not 
clearly specify the reasons for participants dropout. All 
included studies reported that sulforaphane was well-tol -
erated, with no significant adverse effects.
Strength points and limitations
Our study is the first up-to-date meta-analysis to evalu -
ate the efficacy of sulforaphane in schizophrenia patients. 
encompassing data from four RCTs. The studies were of 
high quality with no high risk of bias. The analysis was 
conducted at different time points, including the latest 
follow-up of the studies and a consistent 12-week point. 
The metabolic marker analysis showed no heterogeneity. 
Our study showed that the discontinuation was more in 
the placebo group than in the sulforaphane group, which 
indicates that the drug is well tolerated.
However, our study was not without limitations. The 
limited number of RCTs and small sample size of 369 
patients is a significant limitation. Also, the schizophrenia 
patients included in the studies were not homogenous. 
There was a study with early-episode patients [ 22] and 
others with long-standing patients [ 20, 21]. The duration 
of the trials and doses was different, which could explain 
the heterogeneity found in some of the analyses. Also, 
the geographical concentration, as all studies were con -
ducted in China except for Dickerson (2021) which was 
conducted in the USA. The heterogeneity and small sam-
ple size may limit the generalizability of our results. The 
adverse effects of sulforaphane are not well explained.
Significance of the findings and future research
Our study is the first to provide a contribution to the 
literature about the efficacy and safety of sulforaphane 
in schizophrenia. While the overall psychiatric and cog -
nitive outcomes did not favor sulforaphane over the 
placebo. Sulforaphane was associated with statistically 
significant improvement in general psychopathology 
symptoms. The effect on negative symptoms is unclear. 
While there was no improvement at the latest follow-
up analysis, there was a statistically significant modest 
improvement at 12 weeks with no heterogeneity.
Sulforaphane was also associated with favorable 
changes in the pooled metabolic markers, including 
reduction in LDL, TG, and cholesterol. These findings 
may be relevant if further explored, given the high car -
diometabolic risks in patients with schizophrenia. The 
reduced discontinuation rate may indicate better overall 
tolerability.
We recommend further research be conducted with 
larger sample sizes with standardized treatment dura -
tion and consistent antipsychotic co-administration to 
confirm the long-term efficacy and safety of sulforaphane

in schizophrenia. Consider the specific patients who 
may benefit the most from sulforaphane as an adjunct in 
schizophrenia. Additionally, studies should explore the 
effect of sulforaphane on cognitive parameters and give 
clear data about the adverse effects and safety of the drug. 
Future trials in schizophrenia may consider a higher dose 
of the sulforaphane preparation ≥ 136 µmol/day and con -
sider optimized formulations to enhance the bioavailabil-
ity of sulforaphane.
Conclusion
In conclusion, our meta-analysis findings provide a valu -
able initial overview regarding the efficacy of sulfora -
phane in schizophrenia. While the overall findings did 
not demonstrate consistent improvements across vari -
ous psychiatric outcomes, there was a modest effect on 
general psychopathology and negative symptoms at 12 
weeks. Sulforaphane also appeared to produce favorable 
metabolic changes and a lower discontinuation rate. The 
favorable effects on LDL, triglycerides, and cholesterol 
are remarkable given the high cardiometabolic risks in 
this population. Given the limited number of studies and 
the heterogeneity. These results should be interpreted 
with caution. More well-powered, longer-term trials 
are needed to better understand sulforaphane’s poten -
tial therapeutic role, particularly in relation to negative 
symptoms and metabolic health in schizophrenia. Future 
research should also focus on identifying specific patient 
subgroups that may benefit the most from sulforaphane 
augmentation.
