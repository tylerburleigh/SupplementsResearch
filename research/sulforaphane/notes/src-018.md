The quality of evidence summaries is moderate -- claims are generally anchored to real studies but sometimes oversimplified or lack precision (e.g., "100mg" vs "100g" appears conflated).

## Key Claims About Broccoli Sprouts and Sulforaphane

1. **Sulforaphane concentration:** Broccoli sprouts contain 100-400x more sulforaphane than mature broccoli and other cruciferous vegetables.
2. **NRF2 pathway activation:** Sulforaphane stimulates the NRF2 stress-response pathway, which controls 200+ anti-inflammatory and antioxidant genes. NRF2 activates every 80 minutes when stimulated by sulforaphane vs. every 129 minutes normally.
3. **Cancer risk reduction:** Cruciferous vegetable consumption is linked to lower risk of prostate, bladder, lung, and breast cancer (citing 5 studies).
4. **Detoxification:** A 12-week RCT using a broccoli sprout beverage showed participants excreted up to 61% more benzene (a carcinogen) and reduced acrolein metabolites.
5. **Cardiovascular benefits:** 100mg (or possibly 100g -- see claims to verify) of fresh broccoli sprouts for one week improved cholesterol markers and reduced oxidative stress. 10g/day of broccoli sprout powder for 4 weeks improved triglycerides and cardiovascular markers in type 2 diabetes patients.
6. **Anti-inflammatory:** Broccoli sprout powder containing 40mg sulforaphane (equivalent to ~100g sprouts) significantly reduced TNF-alpha and CRP in humans.
7. **Bioavailability hack:** Adding mustard powder to cooked broccoli increases sulforaphane bioavailability by over 4x (provides exogenous myrosinase).

## Claims to Verify

1. **"100mg of fresh broccoli sprouts for one week" (line ~79):** This is likely an error. 100mg is an implausibly small amount (about 3 seeds). The actual dose in the study was probably 100g. The article later states that 100g sprouts is equivalent to 40mg sulforaphane, which is consistent with the literature. This conflation of mg and g undermines dosing reliability.

2. **"100-400 times more sulforaphane" (lines 2, 60, 127):** This is a widely repeated claim. Fahey et al. (1997) is the usual primary source, reporting glucoraphanin concentrations 20-50x higher in sprouts vs. mature broccoli. The 100-400x figure may refer to sulforaphane potential specifically at 3-day germination. Needs primary source verification.

3. **NRF2 oscillation frequency (80 vs 129 minutes) (line ~59):** This comes from Xue et al. (2015). The specific claim about frequency change should be verified from the primary source -- the original paper may describe this in a more nuanced context.

4. **Missing references [9]-[11]:** Three studies cited in the body text do not appear in the visible reference list, making it impossible to verify the benzene excretion RCT, the diabetes trial, and the inflammation study from this source alone.

5. **"Eat up to 1/2 cup of broccoli sprouts daily" (line ~124):** The article equates 1/2 cup with 100mg, which is internally inconsistent. A 1/2 cup of broccoli sprouts weighs approximately 25-50g, not 100mg. This suggests the author or editor confused mg and g.

## Summary

This is a moderately useful secondary source for identifying relevant primary studies and understanding the general landscape of sulforaphane research. Its main value is as a pointer to specific studies (especially the benzene excretion RCT, cardiovascular trials, and the mustard seed bioavailability hack). However, it has several internal inconsistencies around dosing units (mg vs. g), missing references, and commercial bias. Any quantitative claims from this article should be verified against primary sources before being cited.
