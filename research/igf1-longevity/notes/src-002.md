# Source: src-002 - Growth Hormone, Not IGF-1 Is the Key Longevity Regulator in Mammals

## Bibliographic Info

- **Title:** Growth Hormone, Not IGF-1 Is the Key Longevity Regulator in Mammals
- **Author:** Holly M. Brown-Borg (University of North Dakota School of Medicine & Health Sciences)
- **Journal:** J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci, 2022, 77(9):1719-1723
- **DOI:** 10.1093/gerona/glac092
- **PMCID:** PMC9434454 | PMID: 35436323
- **Type:** Perspective/review article (Fellowship lecture)
- **Year:** 2022

## Core Findings

Brown-Borg argues that GH, rather than IGF-1, is the primary longevity regulator in mammals, challenging the prevailing paradigm that emphasizes IGF-1 based on invertebrate models. The central thesis is that while IGF-1 signaling is the dominant longevity determinant in yeast, worms, and flies, the mammalian endocrine system is more complex, and GH has distinct metabolic effects that drive longevity more powerfully than the secondary reduction in IGF-1.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### GH vs IGF-1 life span extension in mice
- Reduced GH signaling extends life span by **40-70%** in mutant mice
- Reduced IGF-1 signaling extends life span by only **~5%**
- Meta-analysis of **42 survival studies** (Garratt et al., 2017): GH signaling mutations robustly increase median life span with low heterogeneity (**5.6%**), compared to IGF-1 mutations (**34.1%** heterogeneity) and IRS mutations (**47.2%**)

### IGF-1R heterozygous mice -- context-dependent effects
- Initial report: IGF-1R heterozygous mice showed 26% life span extension (Holzenberger et al., 2003)
- However, on C57Bl/6 background: only 11% in females, no extension in males (Xu et al., 2014)
- Independent study (Bokov et al., 2011): less than 5% extension in females, no extension in males, no difference in end-of-life pathology

### GH drives 70-90% of circulating IGF-1
- GH action is responsible for 70-90% of circulating IGF-1 levels
- Liver-specific IGF-1R knockout: normal body weight, length, and development
- Liver-specific GH receptor knockout: decreased body size, low IGF-1, impaired glucose metabolism, hepatic steatosis
- GH antagonist transgenic mice (reduced IGF-1 but normal insulin) do NOT show life span extension

### Distinct metabolic actions of GH vs IGF-1
- **GH is diabetogenic**: promotes insulin resistance, stimulates gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis, inhibits glucose uptake
- **IGF-1 has insulin-like effects**: stimulates glucose uptake in skeletal muscle
- GH profoundly affects lipid metabolism (lipolysis, cholesterol, triglycerides); IGF-1 does not directly affect lipid levels
- Hepatocytes and adipocytes lack IGF-1 receptors but express GH receptors -- key for glucose homeostasis

### Human evidence
- Centenarians show enhanced insulin sensitivity and reduced IGF-1 levels
- Low IGF-1 bioactivity associated with human longevity (centenarians' offspring)
- Long-lived families secrete less GH under tighter regulation
- Neither lifelong GH deficiency nor GH resistance consistently extends human life span, BUT both confer major protection from diabetes, cancer, atherosclerosis, and age-related diseases
- GH excess (acromegaly): severely reduced life span; >50% develop diabetes
- Natural age-related decline in GH: 14% per decade during adult human life (may be protective)
- GH receptor exon 3 deletion is a marker of male-specific exceptional longevity (associated with increased GH sensitivity and taller stature -- a complex relationship)

## Methodology

This is a narrative review/perspective article, not a primary experimental study. It synthesizes evidence from:
- Transgenic and knockout mouse studies (Ames dwarf, Snell dwarf, GHRKO, IGF-1R+/-)
- Meta-analysis of 42 survival studies
- Human cohort studies of centenarians, long-lived families, and individuals with GH deficiency/resistance (Laron syndrome, GHRH receptor mutations)
- Pathophysiological evidence from acromegaly patients

## Relevance to Research Questions

**Q1 (Mechanisms by which IGF-1 influences aging/longevity):** Highly relevant. Argues that IGF-1 is NOT the key longevity regulator in mammals -- GH is. Provides quantitative evidence that IGF-1 reduction has minimal life span effects (5%) compared to GH reduction (40-70%). Challenges the IGF-1-centric view derived from invertebrate models.

**Q2 (Evidence from human studies on IGF-1 and healthspan/longevity):** Highly relevant. Reviews centenarian studies showing reduced IGF-1, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and tightly regulated GH. Notes that human GH deficiency/resistance protects against age-related diseases without consistently extending life span. Discusses the paradoxical GH receptor exon 3 deletion in long-lived males.

**Q3 (Interventions targeting IGF-1/GH axis):** Relevant. Discusses GH excess as harmful (acromegaly, diabetes, reduced life span) and GH deficiency as protective against disease. Notes that GH administration causes edema, arthralgia, carpal tunnel syndrome, and insulin resistance. The natural age-related GH decline (14% per decade) may be protective.

**Q4 (Sex differences in IGF-1/longevity):** Moderately relevant. Notes that IGF-1 signaling reductions preferentially extend life span in females and short-lived strains of mice, while GH signaling effects are not sex-dependent.

## Limitations and Caveats

1. **Perspective article, not systematic review:** This is an argument-driven synthesis, not a systematic review with formal methodology. The author selectively presents evidence supporting GH over IGF-1 as the key regulator.
2. **Primarily based on mouse models:** While human evidence is discussed, the quantitative life span comparisons (40-70% vs 5%) come from genetically modified mice, which may not translate directly to human interventions.
3. **Potential author bias:** Brown-Borg is a leading researcher on GH-deficient dwarf mice (Ames dwarf) and has a career investment in the GH-centric longevity model. Her own work is cited prominently (refs 10, 22, 43).
4. **Complexity acknowledged but underexplored:** The author acknowledges that the relationship is "more intricate" in humans but does not fully reconcile why reduced IGF-1 correlates with human longevity if IGF-1 is not the key regulator.
5. **Does not address tissue-specific IGF-1:** Focuses on circulating (endocrine) IGF-1 driven by GH. Local/autocrine/paracrine IGF-1 actions are not extensively discussed.
6. **No discussion of IGF-1 interventions:** Does not address potential therapeutic modulation of IGF-1 independently of GH.

## Claims to Verify

- **42 survival studies meta-analysis (Garratt et al., 2017):** This is a critical supporting claim. The heterogeneity figures (5.6% for GH vs 34.1% for IGF-1) should be verified against the original meta-analysis, as they are load-bearing for the central argument.
- **IGF-1R heterozygous 26% life span extension (Holzenberger et al., 2003):** Note that the wild-type controls in this study reportedly lived only 18 months, which is unusually short and could inflate the percentage extension. This concern is noted in the source.
- **GH accounts for 70-90% of circulating IGF-1:** This is a widely cited figure but the specific source references (Yakar et al., 1999; 2004) should be checked for exact wording and context.
