**Title:** Resveratrol and Clinical Trials: The Crossroad from In Vitro Studies to Human Evidence
**Authors:** Joao Tome-Carneiro, Mar Larrosa, Antonio Gonzalez-Sarrias, Francisco A. Tomas-Barberan, Maria Teresa Garcia-Conesa, Juan Carlos Espin
**Venue:** Current Pharmaceutical Design, 2013; 19(34):6064-6093
**DOI:** 10.2174/13816128113199990407
**PMCID:** PMC3782695 | PMID: 23448440
**Type:** Comprehensive review (open access)

## Core Findings

1. **Clinical evidence for resveratrol remains very limited despite massive preclinical output.** Over 9,000 publications existed by 2013, but human randomized clinical trials were scarce. Most preclinical studies used concentrations (micromolar range, up to 200 uM) far exceeding physiological levels (parent RES ~50 nM, metabolites ~2 uM in plasma).

2. **The strongest human evidence supports cardioprotective effects** through improvement of inflammatory markers (hsCRP, TNF-alpha), atherogenic profile (ApoB, LDL-ox), glucose metabolism (insulin sensitivity, HbA1c), and endothelial function (FMD). These effects were observed at both low (8-10 mg/day) and high (1-5 g/day) doses.

3. **The "Resveratrol Paradox" remains unsolved:** low bioavailability but observed bioactivity. The specific metabolite(s) or mediator(s) responsible for effects have not been identified. Effects do not scale dose-proportionally.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Bioavailability (Q2)
- RES is well absorbed but extensively metabolized to glucuronide and sulfate conjugates, yielding very low bioavailability of parent compound
- Physiological concentrations: parent RES typically <50 nM, metabolites up to ~2 uM; exceptional Cmax of 4.2 uM parent RES after 5 g micronized dose
- Most abundant circulating metabolite in humans: RES-3-O-sulfate (Cmax ~18 uM at 5 g dose)
- In rats/pigs, most abundant metabolite is RES-3-O-glucuronide (species difference)
- Nearly 20 RES-derived metabolites identified in plasma, urine, and tissues
- Dihydroresveratrol (DHRES) is a key gut microbiota-derived metabolite with pronounced interindividual variability
- Gut bacteria Slackia equolifaciens and Adlercreutzia equolifaciens identified as DHRES producers
- Micronized RES formulation increases plasma Cmax vs standard RES
- Food matrix effects: liquid formulations show similar absorption; capsules delay absorption but produce higher DHRES metabolites
- RES detected in human colorectal tissue (Patel et al., 0.5-1 g doses): up to 674 nmol/g in normal tissue, 94 nmol/g in tumor tissue
- RES detected in hepatic tissue after 5 g/day for ~14 days (Howells et al.)

### Clinical Trials Reviewed (Q1, Q5, Q6)

**Cardiovascular/Metabolic (strongest evidence):**
- Brasnyo et al. (n=19): 10 mg/day for 4 weeks improved insulin resistance in T2 diabetics via Akt pathway; decreased oxidative stress
- Tome-Carneiro et al. (n=75, 12 months): GE-RES (8 mg RES) decreased ApoB (-9.8%), LDL-ox (-20%), hsCRP (-26%), TNF-alpha (-19.8%), PAI-1 (-16.8%); increased IL-10 (+19.8%) in statin-treated CVD-risk patients. Longest trial reported. No drug interactions or adverse effects.
- Fujitaka et al. (n=34): 100 mg/day for 3 months improved FMD from ~4% to ~9% in metabolic syndrome patients; returned to baseline after discontinuation
- Magyar et al. (n=40): 10 mg/day for 3 months improved LDL-c (-8%), endothelial function (+50%) in stable CAD patients
- Bhatt et al. (n=57): 250 mg/day for 3 months in T2 diabetics improved HbA1c (-5%), systolic BP (-8.4%), total cholesterol (-8.5%)
- Crandall et al. (n=10): 1-2 g/day for 4 weeks improved insulin sensitivity (+18.4%) in obese/insulin resistant elderly

**Negative/Null Results (Q5):**
- Yoshino et al. (n=45): 75 mg/day for 12 weeks showed NO metabolic effects in nonobese postmenopausal women with normal glucose tolerance; no change in AMPK, SIRT1, NAMPT, PPARGC1A
- Poulsen et al. (n=24): 1.5 g/day for 4 weeks showed NO effects on insulin sensitivity, BP, energy expenditure, fat content, or inflammatory markers in obese but healthy men
- Zhu et al. (n=31): 5-50 mg/day for 3 months showed no significant effects on DNA methylation or PGE2 in women at increased breast cancer risk

**Safety Concerns (Q6):**
- Popat et al. (n=24): SRT501 (5 g/day) in multiple myeloma patients showed UNACCEPTABLE safety profile: 5 cases of renal failure leading to early termination; nausea (79%), diarrhea (71%). Minimal efficacy.
- Chow et al. (n=42): 1 g/day for 1 month INHIBITED CYP3A4, CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and induced CYP1A2 -- potential for drug interactions, especially with statins metabolized via CYP3A4
- Brown et al. (n=40): Single doses of 0.5-5 g were well tolerated acutely; no adverse effects reported
- Authors warn: "Natural does not equal safe" -- high-dose chronic consumption in medicated people is a concern

### Longevity and Aging (Q3)
- Yeast: RES extended replicative lifespan 70% via SIRT1 (Howitz et al., 2003)
- C. elegans, Drosophila, short-lived fish: lifespan extension via SIRT1
- Mice on HFD: 31% decrease in risk of death
- Controversy persists: some studies found no effect on survival in mice, or sex-dependent effects in flies
- RES mimics some calorie restriction effects in humans (Timmers et al., 2011): reduced sleeping/resting metabolic rate, activated AMPK, increased SIRT1 and PGC-1alpha in muscle, differential expression of 469 genes
- **No human longevity data exist; anti-aging effects are exclusively from preclinical models**

### Mechanism Debates (Q4)
- SIRT1 activation: central to many proposed mechanisms but exact role in calorie restriction and lifespan extension is debated
- Many proposed mechanisms from in vitro studies: NF-kB inhibition, PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway suppression, AMPK activation, eNOS activation, Nrf-2 activation, PGC-1alpha upregulation
- Critical concern: most in vitro studies used concentrations 100-1000x physiological levels
- Only a few mechanisms confirmed in humans: Akt pathway signaling, some gene regulation in blood cells and muscle
- FMD improvement does not scale proportionally with dose (10 mg and 100 mg produced similar FMD improvements)
- Hormetic-like responses suggested: effects sometimes lower at higher concentrations

## Methodology

- Systematic review of all available human randomized clinical trials from PubMed, Web of Knowledge, and Scopus
- Comprehensive Table 1 (pharmacokinetics) and Table 2 (clinical trials) with trial-by-trial critical analysis
- Included both randomized and non-randomized trials; noted design limitations for each
- Also reviewed preclinical evidence (in vitro and animal) with supplementary tables for cancer, cardiovascular, neuroprotective, and anti-aging effects
- Critical assessment approach: each trial's strengths, limitations, sample size, follow-up duration, and statistical methods evaluated

## Limitations

- Review published in 2013; does not include trials published after that date
- Many reviewed clinical trials had small sample sizes (n=6 to n=75), short follow-up periods, and design limitations (unblinded, placebo-uncontrolled, no covariates in statistical analysis)
- Authors note the "endless loop" in resveratrol research: hypothesis-generating in vitro/animal studies keep producing new mechanisms without human confirmation
- No meta-analysis performed; qualitative synthesis only
- Many trials used different RES formulations (standard, micronized, grape extract), doses, and durations, making cross-study comparison difficult

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Clinical trials):** HIGH -- comprehensive catalog and critical analysis of all available RCTs as of 2013
- **Q2 (Bioavailability):** HIGH -- detailed pharmacokinetics and metabolism section with Table 1 summarizing all human PK studies; addresses the "Resveratrol Paradox"
- **Q3 (Longevity vs disease):** MODERATE -- reviews preclinical aging evidence; notes no human longevity data; human evidence limited to calorie restriction mimetic effects
- **Q4 (Mechanism debates):** HIGH -- critically addresses the disconnect between in vitro concentrations and physiological relevance; debates SIRT1 role
- **Q5 (Challenging evidence):** HIGH -- documents negative/null trials (Yoshino, Poulsen), the failed SRT501 multiple myeloma trial with renal toxicity, and the dose-response non-linearity
- **Q6 (Dosing/safety):** HIGH -- addresses safety concerns including CYP interactions, renal toxicity at high doses, and drug interaction risks for medicated patients

## Claims to Verify

- "5 g micronized RES... highest RES amount ever ingested by humans" (Brown et al.) -- confirmed in context
- FMD improved by 65% at 30 mg and 88% at 270 mg (Wong et al.) -- dose-response is notably non-linear
- "31% decrease in risk of death" in mice on HFD (Baur et al.) -- this is from one study and should be verified in the original
- "nearly 20 RES-derived metabolites" described -- reasonable but exact count depends on classification
- SRT501 trial: "5 cases of renal failure" out of 24 patients -- verified from Popat et al. text in the review
