# src-002: Health Effects of Resveratrol: Results from Human Intervention Trials

**Citation:** Ramirez-Garza et al. (2018). *Nutrients*, 10(12), 1892. DOI: 10.3390/nu10121892. PMC6317057.
**Type:** Narrative review (peer-reviewed, open access)
**Venue:** Nutrients (MDPI)

## Core Findings

This review synthesizes human clinical trial data from approximately 2008-2018 on resveratrol bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and health effects across multiple disease domains. The authors conclude that resveratrol supplementation is safe up to 5 g/day, that bioavailability depends heavily on formulation/dose/food matrix/gut microbiota, and that clinical results are mixed -- positive effects are seen in some cardiovascular, metabolic, and neurological endpoints, but many null results exist, particularly for insulin sensitivity and low-dose interventions. The review recommends repeated moderate dosing (>450 mg sustained daily) over single high-dose administration.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Bioavailability and Pharmacokinetics (Q2)
- Resveratrol is rapidly metabolized after oral ingestion, mainly into glucuronide and sulfate conjugates (RV-3-O-sulfate has highest peak plasma concentration in most studies)
- Soluble formulation of trans-RV (40 mg) produced 8.8-fold higher Cmax vs dry powder capsules (n=15)
- Microparticulate RV (SRT501, <5 um particle size, 5 g/day x 14 days) produced higher peak plasma concentration than equivalent non-micronized dose
- High-fat breakfast reduced AUC by 45% and Cmax by 46% vs standard breakfast (n=8, 2000 mg twice daily x 7 days)
- Piperine (20 mg) co-supplementation with 250 mg trans-RV enhanced absorption and increased cerebral blood flow (n=23)
- Repeated dosing showed circadian variation: higher bioavailability at 8am and 12pm; 13th dose showed higher peak than 1st dose
- Half-life: 1-3 hours (single dose), 2-5 hours (repeated dosing)
- 77% of urinary excretion occurred within first 4 hours after lowest dose (0.5 g)
- Enterohepatic recirculation confirmed
- Up to 21 metabolites identified in urine

### Safety and Dosing (Q6)
- Safe and well tolerated across multiple dose levels (25 mg to 5 g/day)
- GI adverse events (nausea, diarrhea, flatulence, abdominal discomfort) reported at doses 2.5-5 g
- Authors conclude "safe and efficient dose is 1 g or more per day"
- In NAFLD patients (n=20), 3 g/day for 8 weeks increased ALT and AST (indicating hepatic stress)
- Potential negative interaction with metformin noted in T2D patients

### Human Clinical Trial Evidence by Domain (Q1)

**Neurological/Alzheimer's (Q3):**
- Phase II RCT in mild-moderate AD (n=119): 500 mg/day escalating to 1000 mg twice daily over 52 weeks; decreased Abeta40 and MMP9 in CSF, modulated neuroinflammation; brain volume decreased in RV group but cognitive deterioration was not indicated
- T2D patients (n=36): single 75 mg dose enhanced neurovascular coupling and cognitive performance
- Healthy adults (n=22): 250-500 mg improved cerebral blood flow but NOT cognitive performance
- Friedreich ataxia (n=24): 5 g/day for 12 weeks improved neurologic function and oxidative stress markers

**Diabetes/Metabolic (Q1, Q3):**
- Overweight/insulin resistant (n=10): 1-2 g/day x 4 weeks improved insulin sensitivity and postprandial glucose
- T2D (n=17): 150 mg/day x 30 days decreased intrahepatic lipid and systolic BP; but metformin interaction may negate insulin sensitivity benefits
- NAFLD (n=20): 3 g/day x 8 weeks -- NO improvement in insulin resistance, steatosis, lipids; increased liver enzymes
- Non-obese postmenopausal women (n=45): 75 mg/day x 12 weeks -- NO effect on inflammatory markers, body composition, insulin sensitivity
- Obese (n=11): 150 mg/day x 30 days mimicked calorie restriction effects (reduced sleeping metabolic rate, AMPK-SIRT1-PGC1alpha activation)

**Cancer (Q1):**
- Healthy volunteers (n=40): 0.5-5.0 g/day x 29 days decreased IGF-1 and IGFBP-3; optimal reduction at 2.5 g dose
- RV proposed as calorie-restriction mimetic with chemopreventive potential

**Cardiovascular (Q1):**
- Overweight/obese with borderline hypertension (n=19): single doses 30-270 mg; dose-dependent FMD improvement
- PREDIMED sub-analysis (n=1000): urinary RV metabolites associated with lower fasting glucose, triglycerides, heart rate
- Overweight/slightly obese (n=45): 150 mg/day x 4 weeks -- NO effect on cardiovascular risk markers, endothelial function, or inflammation

**Oxidative Stress/Inflammation (Q3):**
- Healthy (n=10): single 5 g dose increased TNF-alpha at 24h (pro-inflammatory acute response)
- Healthy (n=9): 1 g/day x 28 days increased circulating T cells, decreased pro-inflammatory cytokines, increased antioxidant capacity
- Peritoneal dialysis patients: 450 mg/day x 12 weeks improved ultrafiltration, decreased angiogenesis markers

### Mechanism Discussion (Q3, Q4)
- SIRT1 and AMPK activation described as key enzymatic regulators stimulated by RV in vivo
- Calorie-restriction mimetic properties noted, including reduced metabolic rate and AMPK-SIRT1-PGC1alpha axis activation in obese humans
- Authors note that determining the contribution of each antioxidant mechanism to overall decreased oxidative stress is "a complex task"
- RV acts as inhibitor of quinone reductase 2 (associated with neurological disorders)

### Null/Negative Results (Q5)
- NAFLD study (n=20, 3 g/day): no improvement, increased liver enzymes
- Non-obese postmenopausal women (n=45, 75 mg/day): no effect on any measured parameter
- Overweight/slightly obese (n=45, 150 mg/day): no effect on cardiovascular or inflammatory markers
- Healthy adults: improved cerebral blood flow but not cognitive performance
- T2D patients on metformin: no improvement in insulin sensitivity (possible drug interaction)
- Authors attribute mixed results to dose, gut microbiota, health status, formulation, and study design variability

## Methodology
- Narrative review of human clinical trials from ~2008-2018
- Inclusion criterion: studies that determined RV in biological samples (plasma, urine, feces)
- Organized into bioavailability/pharmacokinetics section and health effects section
- Tables provide structured summaries of all included trials with sample sizes, doses, and outcomes
- No systematic search strategy or quality assessment reported (limitation)

## Limitations
- Narrative review, not systematic; no PRISMA protocol, no quality assessment of included studies
- Relatively small sample sizes in most included trials (many n<20)
- Heterogeneous dosing regimens, populations, and outcome measures across studies
- Authors acknowledge contrasting results are difficult to reconcile due to multiple confounding variables
- Publication date range means more recent trials (post-2018) are not included

## Relevance Assessment
- **Q1 (Human clinical trial evidence):** Directly relevant -- comprehensive catalog of human intervention trials across multiple disease domains with specific doses, sample sizes, and outcomes
- **Q2 (Bioavailability/pharmacokinetics):** Highly relevant -- detailed pharmacokinetic data including metabolite identification, half-lives, formulation effects, food matrix effects, circadian variation
- **Q3 (Longevity/aging mechanisms):** Moderately relevant -- discusses SIRT1/AMPK/PGC1alpha pathway, calorie restriction mimetic effects in humans, neurological disease effects
- **Q4 (Expert disagreements on mechanism):** Moderately relevant -- acknowledges complexity of mechanisms, mentions multiple pathways but does not deeply engage with sirtuin controversy
- **Q5 (Evidence challenging claims):** Highly relevant -- documents multiple null results across diabetes, cardiovascular, and inflammation domains
- **Q6 (Dose-response, safety, drug interactions):** Highly relevant -- extensive safety data, dose-response discussion, metformin interaction flagged, GI adverse events documented at high doses

## Claims to Verify
- "8.8-fold higher Cmax for soluble formulation" -- from ref [29] (Amiot et al. 2013), n=15; reasonable but single study
- "45% and 46% decrease in AUC and Cmax with high-fat breakfast" -- from ref [34] (La Porte et al. 2010), n=8; very small sample
- PREDIMED sub-analysis n=1000 -- the 1000 figure in Table 2 refers to exploratory baseline analysis, not a full intervention; verify original study design
- "77% of urinary excretion within four hours" -- from ref [43] (Boocock et al. 2007), dose-escalation study with n=40 total but only 10 per dose level
