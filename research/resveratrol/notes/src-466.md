# src-466: Potential Adverse Effects of Resveratrol: A Literature Review

**Citation:** Shaito A, Posadino AM, Younes N, et al. *Int J Mol Sci.* 2020;21(6):2084. doi:10.3390/ijms21062084. PMCID: PMC7139620.

**Type:** Peer-reviewed narrative review (open access, MDPI)

**Quality Assessment:** Full text available from PMC. Substantive content with methods discussion, in vitro/in vivo evidence, and clinical trial data.

---

## Core Findings

1. Resveratrol (RE) exhibits a **hormetic (biphasic) dose-response**: beneficial antioxidant effects at low doses and toxic pro-oxidant effects at high doses. This dual behavior underpins many of the contradictory findings across the literature.

2. RE has **poor oral bioavailability** due to rapid sulfate conjugation in intestine/liver. Only trace amounts (<5 ng/mL) of unmetabolized RE appear in plasma after 25 mg oral dose, despite >70% intestinal absorption. However, RE metabolites have 10x higher half-life and plasma concentrations than native RE, and tissue accumulation (heart, liver, kidney) in rodents reaches ~10-30 uM, comparable to in vitro effective concentrations.

3. At high doses, RE acts as a **pro-oxidant**, inducing DNA damage, oxidative stress, and apoptosis. This is potentially therapeutic against cancer cells (which have elevated copper levels facilitating RE-copper DNA cleavage) but raises safety concerns for normal cells.

4. High-dose RE (1000 mg/day and above) inhibits cytochrome P450 isoenzymes (CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2D6) and interacts with numerous drugs including warfarin (increased bleeding risk), HIV protease inhibitors, statins, and immunosuppressants.

5. A **fatality occurred in a phase II trial** in refractory multiple myeloma patients receiving 5 g/day RE, attributed to nausea, diarrhea, fatigue, and renal toxicity.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Bioavailability (Q2)
- Oral 25 mg RE: only trace amounts (<5 ng/mL) of unmetabolized RE in plasma
- >70% absorbed by GI tract but rapidly metabolized via sulfate conjugation
- Low water solubility (<0.05 mg/mL)
- RE metabolite half-life and plasma concentrations are 10x higher than native RE
- Rodent tissue concentrations: ~10-30 uM in heart, liver, kidney (comparable to in vitro effective doses)
- Human colon cancer tissue: picomolar concentrations after 5 mg or 1 g dosing
- Plasma RE concentration of 137 uM reported 1 hour after 1 g pharmacological dose in humans
- PLGA nanoparticle formulation increased oral bioavailability by 335.7% in rats

### Adverse Effects and Safety (Q6)
- **450 mg/day** reported as safe dose for 60-kg person
- **1000 mg/day and above**: inhibits CYP3A4, CYP2C9, CYP2D6; activates CYP1A2; drug interactions
- **2-5 g/day**: mild diarrhea, nausea, hypersensitivity, anal pruritus
- **5 g/day**: phase II trial in multiple myeloma -- one patient death; renal toxicity reported
- 1000 mg/day elevated CVD risk biomarkers (ox-LDL, sE-selectin 1, sICAM-1, sVCAM-1, tPAI-1) in overweight older adults
- RE can reduce WBC counts, plasma IL-6, TNF levels; elevate ALT
- 200 mg/kg/day in rats and 600 mg/kg/day in dogs for 90 days: no adverse effects
- 1000 mg/kg/day and 3000 mg/kg/day in rats: renal toxicity
- Long-term intake can act as thyroid disruptor and goitrogen

### Mechanism Debates (Q4)
- **Hormesis** is the central explanatory framework: low-dose antioxidant vs. high-dose pro-oxidant
- RE pro-oxidant effects mediated by: cytochrome P450 (CYP2C9) ROS production, flavin oxidases, copper-mediated DNA cleavage
- Pro-oxidant effects counteractable by N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) and diphenyleneiodonium (DPI)
- Chronobiology matters: antioxidant effect during dark span, pro-oxidant during light span
- RE suppresses COX-1 and COX-2 expression (anti-inflammatory but may delay ulcer healing)

### Challenging Evidence (Q5)
- Human clinical trials have shown **controversial results**; reasons include patient characteristics, dose, duration, gut microbiota variability, genetic background
- RE in vivo results are highly variable: positive, negative, or neutral depending on dose, tumor model, species, sex, strain, timing
- Despite promising in vitro results, many in vivo studies failed to attribute beneficial effects to RE
- Lack of uniform study design is a major limitation across the field

### Clinical Trials (Q1)
- 244 human clinical trials with RE registered on clinicaltrials.gov (as of 2020)
- First phase I cancer trial published 2009: colorectal cancer, 20-80 mg/day RE, 14 days; decreased Wnt target gene expression in normal mucosa but not cancerous mucosa
- 2.5 g/day for 29 days: significantly reduced IGF1 and IGFBP3 in plasma (chemopreventive)
- 1 g/day for 4 weeks: favorably modulated lymphocyte counts and carcinogenesis enzymes with no significant adverse effects
- 300 mg/day: no cognitive function changes; 1000 mg/day: selective improvement only in psychomotor speed
- Clinical evidence for RE as effective cancer supplement in humans is **scarce**

### Longevity vs Disease (Q3)
- RE extends lifespan of organisms (referenced but not primary focus)
- Anti-aging properties attributed to sirtuin activation
- RE promotes mitochondrial function and cellular reprogramming
- The review focuses more on toxicity than longevity per se

## Methodology

- Narrative literature review synthesizing in vitro, in vivo (animal), and human clinical evidence
- Organized by mechanism (DNA damage, oxidative stress, drug interactions) and evidence type (in vitro, animal, human)
- No systematic search strategy described; not a formal systematic review or meta-analysis
- Covers literature up to early 2020

## Limitations

- Not a systematic review: no PRISMA protocol, no formal search strategy, no quality assessment of included studies
- Does not provide quantitative synthesis (no meta-analysis)
- Many cited doses are from in vitro studies with uncertain translation to in vivo relevance
- Published 2020; does not cover more recent clinical trial results
- The 244 clinical trials figure from clinicaltrials.gov is cited but the specific trials are not catalogued
