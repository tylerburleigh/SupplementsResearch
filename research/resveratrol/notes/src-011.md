# src-011: Novelle et al. (2015) — "Resveratrol supplementation, where are we now and where should we go?"

**Citation:** Novelle MG, Wahl D, Dieguez C, Bernier M, de Cabo R. Ageing Research Reviews, 2015 Jan;21:1-15. DOI: 10.1016/j.arr.2015.01.002. PMCID: PMC5835393.

**Quality:** ok — Full peer-reviewed review article with substantive text, methods, and analysis.

## Core Findings

This is a comprehensive review of clinical trials and preclinical evidence for resveratrol (RSV) supplementation in humans, covering cancer, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, neurodegeneration, obesity, and aging. The central conclusion is that RSV appears effective primarily in metabolically compromised individuals (diabetics, obese, CVD patients) but shows little to no benefit in healthy individuals. Most clinical trials to date have been limited by small sample sizes, and no lifespan extension has been demonstrated in animals on standard diets supplemented with RSV.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Clinical Trials Summary (Tables 1 & 2)
- Review catalogues ~35+ clinical trials spanning doses from 5 mg/day to 5 g/day.
- **Cancer:** Colorectal cancer trials show RSV inhibits Wnt signaling in normal mucosa but not cancerous mucosa (Nguyen et al., 2009, n=8). SRT501 (micronized RSV) at 5 g/day was well tolerated and showed increased caspase-3 activation in hepatic metastases (Howells et al., 2011, n=9). However, multiple myeloma patients on 5 g/day SRT501 experienced severe side effects including a possible treatment-related death (Popat et al., 2013).
- **Diabetes:** Meta-analyses show RSV significantly improves glucoregulation and insulin sensitivity in diabetic patients but NOT in healthy controls (Liu et al., 2014; Hausenblas et al., 2014). Bhatt et al. (2012, n=57): 250 mg/day for 3 months improved HbA1c, lipid profiles, systolic BP. Brasnyo et al. (2011, n=19): low dose (2x5 mg/day) improved insulin resistance.
- **CVD:** Meta-analysis showed no significant effect of RSV on plasma lipids (Sahebkar, 2013). RSV at >=150 mg/day decreased systolic BP without affecting diastolic BP (Liu et al., 2014b). Flow-mediated dilation improved significantly in multiple trials (Wong et al., 2011; Magyar et al., 2012).
- **Neurocognition:** RSV increased cerebral blood flow without cognitive changes in acute dosing (Kennedy et al., 2010). 26-week supplementation improved memory performance and hippocampal functional connectivity in overweight older adults (Witte et al., 2014, n=46).
- **Healthy individuals:** No significant effects on body composition, metabolic rate, or insulin sensitivity in non-obese healthy humans (Yoshino et al., 2012; Poulsen et al., 2013).

### Bioavailability
- Rapid and presystemic metabolism of RSV is a major challenge.
- Capsule form increased absorption 10x vs dry powder (Amiot et al., 2013).
- RSV metabolism shows considerable individual variation linked to gut microbiota differences (Bode et al., 2013).
- Food levels of RSV are well below concentrations needed for health effects; supplementation is required.

### Safety and Dosing
- Daily consumption of 450 mg RSV deemed safe for a 60-kg individual (Smoliga et al., 2012).
- Gastrointestinal tract is the most affected system for adverse effects.
- High doses inhibit cytochrome P450 isoenzymes, potentially affecting pharmacokinetics of many drugs.
- RSV exhibits biphasic dose-response: low doses cause weight gain in mice on high-fat diet; high doses cause weight loss.

### Mechanism Debates
- Earlier reports: RSV directly activates SIRT1 (Howitz et al., 2003).
- Later evidence: RSV indirectly modulates SIRT1 via inhibition of cAMP phosphodiesterases (Park et al., 2012).
- Current view: RSV activates SIRT1 through both direct and indirect actions (Canto et al., 2010; Um et al., 2010).
- Metabolic effects depend on acute AMPK activation via SIRT1-independent mechanism (Price et al., 2012).
- RSV extends lifespan in yeast, C. elegans, Drosophila, but NOT in mice on standard diet ad libitum (Miller et al., 2011; Pearson et al., 2008; Strong et al., 2013). Lifespan extension only seen in mice on high-fat diet (Baur et al., 2006).

### Exercise Interaction
- RSV may blunt exercise benefits: Gliemann et al. (2013) showed RSV hindered positive cardiovascular effects of exercise training in aged men.
- Scribbans et al. (2014): RSV did not augment training response from high-intensity interval training.
- Olesen et al. (2014): RSV negated positive impact of exercise training on metabolic and inflammatory status in aged men.

## Methodology
- Narrative review with systematic cataloguing of clinical trials in two tables (Table 1: patients with medical conditions; Table 2: healthy/obese participants).
- Covers only studies testing known quantities of RSV, not multi-compound formulations.
- Includes both human clinical trials and preclinical animal/cell data for context.
- Meta-analyses cited for diabetes (Liu et al., 2014), lipids (Sahebkar, 2013), and blood pressure (Liu et al., 2014b).

## Limitations
- Most reviewed clinical trials have small sample sizes (typically 8-57 participants).
- Heterogeneity in dosing (5 mg to 5 g/day), duration (single dose to 12 months), formulation, and patient populations.
- Few studies controlled for diet, concurrent medications, or gut microbiota variability.
- No direct meta-analysis performed by the authors; they rely on existing meta-analyses.
- Review published in 2015; does not cover more recent trials.

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Clinical Trials):** Directly relevant. Comprehensive catalogue of 35+ clinical trials with details on dosage, duration, outcomes, and timing. Key finding: benefits seen mainly in metabolically compromised individuals.
- **Q2 (Bioavailability):** Directly relevant. Discusses rapid presystemic metabolism, individual variation in gut microbiota metabolism, formulation challenges (capsule vs powder), and that food sources provide insufficient RSV.
- **Q3 (Longevity vs Disease):** Directly relevant. Key finding: RSV extends lifespan in lower organisms but NOT in mammals on standard diet; lifespan benefit only under high-fat diet metabolic stress. Benefits in disease prevention are context-dependent.
- **Q4 (Mechanism Debates):** Directly relevant. Reviews the SIRT1 direct vs indirect activation debate, AMPK-dependent and independent pathways, and biphasic dose-response.
- **Q5 (Challenging Evidence):** Directly relevant. Documents multiple negative trials (no effect in healthy individuals, blunting of exercise benefits, SRT501 safety concerns in multiple myeloma, and failures in NAFLD). Notes conflicting results on anti-inflammatory effects.
- **Q6 (Dosing/Safety):** Directly relevant. 450 mg/day deemed safe for 60-kg individual; GI side effects most common; cytochrome P450 inhibition at high doses; dose range across trials is 5 mg to 5 g/day.

## Claims to Verify
