## Core Findings

This is a comprehensive review of resveratrol's (RES) effects on cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), covering atherosclerosis, hypertension, stroke, myocardial ischemia, and heart failure. Preclinical studies consistently show cardioprotective effects via SIRT-1, AMPK, eNOS, and Nrf2 pathways, but clinical trials remain sparse, small, and often inconclusive. The author highlights the "resveratrol paradox" -- biological effects despite low plasma concentrations -- and notes that the gap between preclinical promise and clinical evidence remains large.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Bioavailability (Q2)
- After oral administration of 0.36 mg/kg, only ~2 uM RES detected in plasma within 30 min, far below EC50 values (5-100 uM) for pharmacologic effects.
- Boocock et al. pharmacokinetic study: peak plasma concentrations of 73-539 ng/mL (0.3-2.4 uM) after single doses of 0.5-5 g RES in healthy volunteers (10 per dose level). Metabolite RES-3-O-sulfate reached 1135-4294 ng/mL (3.7-14 uM).
- Wine consumption (~25 mg RES) yields plasma concentrations of 1-5 ng/mL; doses up to 5 g yield ~530 ng/mL.
- Rapid conjugation in intestine and liver (glucuronides, sulfates) limits oral availability. The "RES paradox" may involve metabolite activity or synergistic effects with other Mediterranean diet polyphenols.

### Clinical Trials (Q1)
- Only 26 publications in PubMed under "resveratrol" + "cardiovascular disease" as of 2016; of these, several were not actual RES supplementation trials.
- **Anti-atherosclerotic:** Meta-analysis of 7 clinical trials showed no effect on lipid profile. But 250-1000 mg/day lowered LDL-C in type 2 diabetics; 150 mg/day lowered triglycerides in obese men; 500 mg/day lowered triglycerides in healthy smokers.
- **Anti-hypertensive:** Meta-analysis of 6 RCTs (247 subjects) showed high-dose RES (>=150 mg/day) significantly decreased blood pressure; lower doses had no effect.
- **Myocardial ischemia:** Triple-blind RCT (n=75, stable angina/ACS) with GE+RES (8 mg/day then 16 mg/day for 1 year) increased adiponectin, decreased PAI-1. Post-MI trial (n=40, 10 mg/day for 3 months) improved diastolic function, endothelial function, lowered LDL-C. Militaru et al. trial (n=166, stable angina) showed RES+calcium fructoborate lowered NT-proBNP by 65.5% at 60 days.
- **Heart failure:** No clinical trials published as of 2016.
- **Stroke:** No clinical trials in stroke patients; RES shown to increase cerebral blood flow in healthy adults and postmenopausal women.

### Mechanism Debates (Q4)
- Key targets: SIRT-1, AMPK, eNOS, Nrf2, NF-kB, miRNAs.
- **SIRT-1 controversy:** Pacholec et al. provided evidence that RES is NOT a direct SIRT-1 activator. RES may activate AMPK through LKB1 independently of SIRT-1. Debate exists over whether SIRT-1 activation is cause or consequence of RES action.
- **Hormesis noted:** Beneficial effects at low doses, cytotoxic at higher doses (J-shaped or inverted U-shaped curve).
- **miRNA modulation:** RES modulated miR-21, miR-20b, miR-27a, miR-9, miR-328, miR-29 family in cardiac contexts.

### Challenging Evidence (Q5)
- Semba et al. (InCHIANTI study, n=783): Total urinary RES metabolite concentration was NOT associated with inflammatory markers, CVD, cancer, or all-cause mortality.
- Van der Made et al. (n=45 overweight/obese): RES 150 mg/day for 4 weeks did not change metabolic risk markers.
- Faghihzadeh et al. (n=55 NAFLD): RES 500 mg/day for 12 weeks had no beneficial effect on anthropometric measurements, insulin resistance, lipid profile, or blood pressure.
- Soare et al. (n=56 non-obese): Multi-ingredient supplement including 100 mg RES had no effect on arterial stiffness, endothelial function, blood pressure, or metabolic variables over 6 months.
- Inconclusive results attributed to small sample sizes, short-term studies, and protocol heterogeneity.

### Dosing and Safety (Q6)
- Doses studied clinically range from 8 mg/day (grape extract) to 1000+ mg/day supplements.
- RES inhibits CYP enzymes (notably CYP3A4 with IC50 1-5 uM), creating potential drug interactions with statins, calcium channel blockers, anti-arrhythmics, anti-platelet agents, anticoagulants.
- High doses (~1 g/day or above) may interact with co-administered drugs via intestinal CYP3A4 and/or P-glycoprotein inhibition.
- Low wine-level doses (~few mg/day) have low risk of drug interactions.
- Some studies showed dose-dependent effects that are non-linear: e.g., 2.5 mg/kg/day protected rat hearts but 25 or 100 mg/kg/day did not.
- Side effects in clinical trials: generally mild (GI discomfort in some studies); most trials reported no side effects.

## Limitations
- Review from 2016; data is now ~10 years old.
- Author notes that most preclinical studies administered RES as pretreatment before disease onset, limiting clinical translatability.
- Clinical trials are heterogeneous in dose, duration, population, and endpoints.
- Many clinical studies used RES-enriched grape extract rather than pure RES, confounding attribution of effects.
