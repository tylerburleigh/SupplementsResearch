# Source src-216: NAD+ Metabolism and Its Roles in Cellular Processes During Ageing

**Citation:** Covarrubias AJ, Perrone R, Grozio A, Verdin E. Nat Rev Mol Cell Biol. 2021 Feb;22(2):119-141. DOI: 10.1038/s41580-020-00313-x. PMID: 33353981, PMCID: PMC7963035.

**Type:** Comprehensive review article in a high-impact journal (Nature Reviews Molecular Cell Biology).

---

## Core Findings (2-3 sentences)

This review establishes that NAD+ levels decline by 10-65% during ageing across multiple tissues in rodents and humans, driven primarily by increased CD38 activity from proinflammatory macrophages and heightened PARP1 activation. Restoring NAD+ levels via precursors (NR, NMN) shows strong preclinical efficacy in mouse models of neurodegeneration, metabolic disease, and progeroid disorders, but human clinical trial results have been mixed. The authors identify key unresolved questions about long-term safety, optimal dosing, tissue specificity, and the potential for NAD+ boosting to inadvertently promote senescent cell inflammation.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### NAD+ Decline During Ageing
- Age-related NAD+ decline ranges from 10% to 65% depending on tissue and age (line 2243-2245).
- Major drivers: increased CD38 expression in tissues (particularly on M1-like macrophages activated by SASP from senescent cells), increased PARP1 activation from DNA damage, and decreased NAMPT expression (especially in obesity).
- PARP1 alone accounts for ~90% of total PARP activity; it outcompetes SIRT1 for NAD+ due to lower Km and higher Vmax (line 718-762).
- SIRT1 and SIRT2 account for about one third of total NAD+ consumption under basal conditions (line 607-611).

### NR-Specific Evidence

#### Mechanism and Pharmacokinetics
- NR enters cells via an unknown nucleoside transporter and is phosphorylated by NRK1 and NRK2 to form NMN, then converted to NAD+ by NMNAT1-3 (line 298-303).
- CD73 dephosphorylates extracellular NMN to NR, and NRK1 converts it back to NMN intracellularly -- making NRK1 a rate-limiting step (line 361-362).
- CD157 can consume NR as an alternative substrate, converting it to NAM and ribose (line 817-818). This may reduce NR bioavailability in certain tissues.
- A reduced form of NR, NRH, is more stable and potent than NR at increasing NAD+ through a different pathway via adenosine kinase (line 2418-2428).
- NR is found in daily foods such as milk, fruits, vegetables, and meat (line 353-354).

#### Preclinical Results
- NR restored muscle mass, force generation, endurance, and mitochondrial respiratory capacity in Nampt-knockout mice (line 1348-1360).
- NR showed a small but significant 5% increase in lifespan in old mice (line 2382-2383).
- NR prevented fatty liver and induced the mitochondrial unfolded protein response in high-fat diet-fed mice; effects diminished in SIRT1-knockout mice (line 1374-1385).
- NR improved glucose tolerance, reduced weight gain, and protected against diabetic neuropathy in high-fat diet-fed mice (line 1395-1402).
- NR decreased phosphorylated tau levels and increased neurogenesis, LTP, and cognitive function in 3xTgAD Alzheimer's mouse model (line 1433-1443).
- NR ameliorated mitochondrial function and increased autophagy/lysosomal function in iPSC-derived dopaminergic neurons from Parkinson's patients (line 1437-1444).
- NR partially rescued a multimorbidity syndrome in mice with dysfunctional mitochondria, reducing inflammation and senescent cell burden (line 1790-1792).
- In yeast and C. elegans, low micromolar concentrations of NR extend lifespan in a sirtuin-dependent manner (line 2316-2319).

#### Human Clinical Trials (NR-specific)
- Doses studied: 250-1200 mg daily for 6 weeks to 6 months.
- A crossover study in healthy adults aged 55-79: NR 500 mg twice daily for 6 weeks (NCT02921659).
- A randomized double-blind study in women aged 65-80: NR 1000 mg daily (500 mg every 12 hours) for 4.5-6 months (NCT03818802).
- A randomized double-blind study for blood pressure/arterial stiffness in adults aged 50-79: NR 1000 mg daily for 3 months (NCT03821623).
- A study in patients with MCI: NR titrated from 250 mg to 1 g daily (NCT02942888).
- A crossover study in MCI patients: NR 1200 mg for 8 weeks (NCT04078178).
- A study using Niagen NRPT (NR + pterostilbene): 250 mg NR + 50 mg pterostilbene once or twice daily for 8 weeks (NCT02678611).

### Clinical Trial Evidence Summary
- Phase I trials have primarily demonstrated that NMN and NR are safe and efficiently increase NAD+ levels in healthy volunteers (line 2280-2284).
- One trial showed short-term NR had beneficial effects in healthy elderly individuals; another showed positive results in ALS patients (line 2286-2289).
- Two randomized double-blind studies in overweight/obese individuals treated with NR for 6 and 12 weeks: NR increased NAD+ levels but showed no weight loss, insulin sensitivity improvement, or enhanced mitochondrial function (line 1185-1195).
- NR showed little to no effect in aged men with obesity (line 2294-2299).
- Several trials ongoing for neurological disorders, including cognitive decline and Parkinson disease.

### Safety Considerations
- Short-term NR/NMN administration proven safe in healthy participants (line 2617-2623).
- Long-term supplementation side effects are unknown.
- Treating senescent cells with NMN can heighten the SASP, potentially promoting chronic inflammation and inflammation-driven cancers (line 1747-1755).
- High-dose NAM (1-5 mM) reduces lifespan in yeast and C. elegans and can act as a feedback inhibitor of sirtuins and PARPs (line 2325-2332).
- High methylated NAM levels associated with type 2 diabetes, Parkinson disease, and cardiac diseases (line 2349-2351).
- NAM supplementation (at supraphysiologic doses) may deplete methyl groups needed for DNA methylation (line 2346-2350).
- NRH (reduced form of NR) has unknown potential side effects requiring further study (line 2426-2428).

## Methodology

This is a comprehensive narrative review covering approximately 5 years of NAD+ research. It synthesizes evidence from:
- In vitro and in vivo preclinical studies (yeast, C. elegans, Drosophila, rodent models)
- Human primary cell studies
- Clinical trials (Phase I and II)

### Key Preclinical Study Details (from Table 1)
- Mouse studies used C57BL/6 wild-type strains, various disease models (Alzheimer's 3xTgAD, muscular dystrophy mdx, high-fat diet obesity, Cockayne syndrome, ataxia telangiectasia).
- NR doses in mouse studies are not consistently reported in this review but referenced via primary literature.
- Long-term NMN feeding study: 1 year starting at 5 months of age in mice (line 2378-2379).

### Key Clinical Trial Details (from Table 2)
- NR clinical trials: sample sizes not provided in this review; doses range from 250-1200 mg/day; durations 6 weeks to 6 months.
- Populations: healthy elderly (55-80 years), MCI patients, ALS patients, overweight/obese individuals.
- NMN clinical trials in Japan (UMIN registry): doses 100-500 mg; durations single dose to 24 weeks.

## Critiques and Limitations

1. **Translation gap:** Strong preclinical results in rodents have not consistently translated to human clinical benefits, particularly in metabolic disease/obesity.
2. **Unknown long-term safety:** Whether long-term NAD+ precursor supplementation has adverse effects remains unknown.
3. **Senescence-SASP paradox:** NAD+ precursors may both reduce and enhance senescence-associated inflammation depending on context, creating uncertainty.
4. **Mechanistic uncertainty:** The specific cells and enzymes driving NAD+ decline in specific disorders remain unclear.
5. **Dose optimization:** Optimal therapeutic doses for different diseases are undetermined.
6. **Tissue specificity:** Whether NMN and NR have tissue/disease-specific effects is unknown.
7. **NR bioavailability questions:** CD157 and CD38 can degrade NR and NMN, potentially limiting efficacy.
8. **Potential cancer concerns:** NNMT inhibition may activate senescent cells; NAD+ boosting may theoretically fuel cancer cell metabolism.
9. **Controversies in the field:** Disagreement on whether PARP1 or CD38 is the primary NAD+ consumer during M1 macrophage polarization (line 1595-1598 vs 1598-1601).
10. **NMN neurotoxicity question:** NMN accumulation has been reported as potentially neurotoxic, promoting SARM1 activation and axonal degeneration, though this remains controversial (line 1904-1912).

This source is a **highly authoritative review** that directly addresses:
- Mechanisms of NAD+ decline during ageing (CD38, PARP1, reduced NAMPT)

## Claims to Verify

1. **"10% to 65%" NAD+ decline range** (line 2243-2245): This is cited from ref 130 but the specific studies underlying this range are not detailed in this review. The range is quite broad and should be verified against primary sources.
2. **"5% increase in lifespan"** with NR in old mice (line 2382-2383): Referenced to ref 198. A 5% increase is small and should be confirmed with sample size and statistical significance from the primary paper.
3. **"SIRT1 and SIRT2 responsible for about one third of total NAD+ consumption"** (line 607-611): Referenced to ref 5. This is a specific quantitative claim about enzyme-level NAD+ consumption that should be verified from the primary isotopic tracing study.
