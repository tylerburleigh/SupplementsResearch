# src-074: NAD+ Supplementation for Anti-Aging and Wellness: A PRISMA-Guided Systematic Review

**Source type:** Systematic Review (PubMed abstract; PMID 41655607)
**Published:** 2026, Elsevier B.V.
**Content quality:** Abstract-only (no full text retrieved). All findings below are from the abstract.

---

## Core Findings

This PRISMA-guided systematic review of 113 studies (33 human, 80 rodent) spanning January 2010 to October 2025 found that while NAD+ augmentation (NR, NMN) demonstrates clear biological activity and biochemical target engagement in humans, clinical effectiveness for anti-aging or wellness outcomes remains inconclusive. Functional outcomes in human trials were heterogeneous and often null. No evidence supports parenteral (IV/IM) NAD+ for anti-aging indications.

## 1. Review Methodology

- **Design:** PRISMA-guided systematic review
- **Scope:** Peer-reviewed human and rodent intervention studies evaluating NAD-related compounds (oral or parenteral), including NR, NMN, niacin, and nicotinamide
- **Date range:** January 2010 through October 2025
- **Eligible studies:** 113 total
  - 33 human intervention studies (28 randomized, 5 nonrandomized)
  - 80 rodent studies
- **Publication type:** Systematic Review (PubMed indexed)

## 2. Key Findings from Human Studies

- **Biochemical target engagement:** Oral NR and NMN consistently increased circulating (plasma/whole blood) and cellular (e.g., PBMC) NAD-related metabolites
- **Safety/tolerability:** Oral NR and NMN were generally well tolerated over weeks to months of supplementation
- **Functional outcomes:** Effects on functional, metabolic, vascular, and other healthspan-relevant outcomes were **heterogeneous and often null or endpoint-specific** -- not consistently beneficial
- This is a key finding: target engagement does not reliably translate to clinically meaningful outcomes

## 3. Key Findings from Rodent Studies

- NAD+ augmentation was **frequently associated** with improvements in:
  - Metabolic outcomes
  - Mitochondrial function
  - Inflammatory markers
  - Functional outcomes
- However, effects **varied across models and endpoints** (not uniformly positive)
- The translational gap between rodent and human results is notable

## 4. Parenteral NAD+ Approaches

- **No eligible outcomes trials** evaluated intravenous or intramuscular NAD+ for anti-aging or wellness indications
- One nonrandomized intravenous NMN study met inclusion criteria, contributing primarily short-term safety and biomarker information
- One intravenous NAD+ pharmacokinetic pilot was identified as contextual evidence only (lacked eligible clinical outcomes)
- **Conclusion:** The evidence base for parenteral NAD+ therapy is essentially nonexistent in terms of clinical outcomes

## 5. Overall Conclusions about Clinical Effectiveness

- NAD+ augmentation shows **clear biological activity** (confirmed target engagement)
- Clinical effectiveness for anti-aging or wellness outcomes remains **inconclusive**
- Larger, well-designed randomized trials are needed with:
  - Longer follow-up periods
  - Prespecified clinically meaningful endpoints
  - Particularly for parenteral approaches

## 6. Limitations and Gaps Identified

- **Abstract-only source:** Full text was not retrieved; detailed subgroup analyses, dose-response relationships, and individual study quality assessments are unavailable
- **Translational gap:** Rodent studies show frequent benefits but human studies show heterogeneous/null results on functional endpoints
- **Parenteral evidence gap:** No clinical outcomes data for IV/IM NAD+ despite its use in wellness clinics
- **Heterogeneity:** Effects varied across models, endpoints, and presumably dosing regimens
- **Duration uncertainty:** Human studies spanned "weeks to months" -- longer-term effects unknown
- **Conflict of interest note:** One author (Cory Gallagher) has a connection to an aesthetics clinic, though it does not offer NAD+ infusions or supplementation

## Relevance to Research Questions

This source is highly relevant as a comprehensive systematic review covering NR, NMN, and parenteral NAD+ approaches across both human and animal studies. It provides strong evidence that:
1. NR and NMN achieve biochemical target engagement in humans
2. Functional/clinical outcomes in humans are inconsistent
3. Parenteral NAD+ lacks clinical evidence entirely
4. The translational gap between rodent and human findings is substantial
