# Source src-036: Wang et al. (2015) - Carotene and Lycopene and Prostate Cancer Risk

**Citation:** Wang Y, Cui R, Xiao Y, Fang J, Xu Q. (2015). Effect of Carotene and Lycopene on the Risk of Prostate Cancer: A Systematic Review and Dose-Response Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies. *PLOS ONE* 10(9): e0137427. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0137427

## Core Findings

This systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of 34 observational studies (10 cohort, 11 nested case-control, 13 case-control; total 15,891 cases and 592,479 participants) found that both dietary lycopene intake and blood lycopene levels were inversely associated with prostate cancer risk, but lycopene could NOT lower the risk of advanced prostate cancer. The WCRF Continuous Update Project (2014) had downgraded the lycopene-PCa link from "strong evidence of decreased risk" to "no conclusion possible," due to inconsistent evidence when stratifying by cancer stage.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Overall Lycopene Results
- **Dietary lycopene intake and PCa:** Overall pooled RR = 0.88 (95% CI: 0.76-1.02; p=0.083), NOT statistically significant.
  - After sensitivity analysis removing the Jian et al. study (which had wide CIs): RR = 0.91 (95% CI: 0.83-1.00), borderline significant.
  - In 4 cohort studies specifically: RR = 0.87 (95% CI: 0.77-0.99), p=0.029 (significant).
  - Abstract reports: RR = 0.86 (95% CI: 0.75-0.98) -- this appears to be after sensitivity analysis adjustments.
- **Blood lycopene levels and PCa:** RR = 0.81 (95% CI: 0.69-0.96), p=0.015 (significant).
- **Blood lycopene and advanced PCa (4 studies):** RR = 0.75 (95% CI: 0.44-1.28; I2=63.2%), NOT significant.

### Dose-Response Analysis (Lycopene)
- 3% reduction in PCa risk per 1 mg/day increment in dietary lycopene intake (95% CI: 0.94-0.99).
- Nonlinear dose-response relationship observed.
- 7 studies eligible for dietary dose-response; 8 studies for blood concentration dose-response.

### Subgroup Findings (Lycopene)
- Inverse association with dietary lycopene was significant in cohort studies (RR=0.87) but not in case-control or nested case-control designs individually.
- Blood lycopene inverse association was significant in nested case-control studies (RR=0.82, p=0.02) and borderline in North American studies (RR=0.82, p=0.05).
- Inverse association was largely mediated through confounders: FHPC, BMI, education, smoking, physical activity.

### Alpha-Carotene and Beta-Carotene
- Dietary alpha-carotene intake inversely associated with PCa (RR=0.87, 95% CI: 0.76-0.99).
- Dose-response: 2% reduction per 0.2 mg/day alpha-carotene increment (95% CI: 0.96-0.99).
- Beta-carotene showed NO association with PCa risk (dietary RR=0.90, 95% CI: 0.81-1.01; blood RR=0.96, 95% CI: 0.81-1.14).

## Methodology

- **Design:** Systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis following PRISMA guidelines.
- **Search:** PubMed and Embase, up to January 2015.
- **Included:** 34 studies from 33 publications; 15,891 cases, 592,479 participants.
- **Study types:** 10 cohort (including 2 case-cohort), 11 nested case-control, 13 case-control.
- **Geographic spread:** 22 North America, 7 Europe, 2 Australia, 2 Asia, 1 Uruguay.
- **Quality assessment:** Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS); mean score 7.6 stars (range 4-9), indicating fair quality.
- **Statistical methods:** Random-effects models; Cochran Q test and I2 for heterogeneity; sensitivity analyses; subgroup analyses by study type, region, covariates; dose-response using generalized least squares with restricted cubic splines (3 knots).
- **All included studies used food frequency questionnaires** for dietary assessment.

## Limitations

1. **Observational only** -- all included studies are observational; no RCTs included. Cannot establish causation.
2. **Heterogeneity not fully explained** -- subgroup analyses did not account for all between-study variation.
3. **Confounding concerns** -- not all studies adjusted for important covariates (e.g., only 12/34 adjusted for FHPC, 14 for smoking, 3 for alcohol).
4. **Dietary assessment limitations** -- all studies used FFQs, which are subject to recall bias and measurement error.
5. **Advanced PCa evidence thin** -- only 4 studies for blood lycopene and advanced PCa, with high heterogeneity (I2=63.2%).
6. **Lycopene dietary intake overall result was NOT statistically significant** (p=0.083); significance only appeared after removing one outlier study or restricting to cohort design.
7. **WCRF CUP report (2014) downgraded** the lycopene-PCa evidence to "no conclusion possible," citing inconsistencies by cancer type/stage.

## Relevance to Research Questions

### Q1: Current state of evidence from large prospective cohort studies and RCTs on lycopene and prostate cancer
- **Highly relevant.** Meta-analysis of 34 observational studies with 592,479 participants. Shows modest inverse association for dietary lycopene (significant only in cohort studies, RR=0.87) and blood lycopene (RR=0.81). But overall dietary result not significant (p=0.083), and no effect on advanced PCa. WCRF downgraded evidence to "no conclusion possible."

### Q5: Evidence contradicting the popular narrative that lycopene reduces prostate cancer risk
- **Highly relevant.** Key contradicting evidence: (1) Overall pooled dietary lycopene-PCa association was NOT statistically significant (RR=0.88, p=0.083); (2) No significant effect on advanced PCa from blood lycopene (RR=0.75, 95% CI: 0.44-1.28); (3) WCRF CUP 2014 downgraded lycopene-PCa evidence; (4) Significance of dietary result depended on removing one outlier study; (5) Inverse associations largely mediated through confounders (FHPC, education, etc.).

### Q6: Mechanisms of action and alignment with clinical outcomes
- **Moderately relevant.** Discussion mentions lycopene mechanisms: attenuation via CDK7, BCL2, EGFR, IGF-1R gene expression modulation; PPARgamma-LXRalpha-ABCA1 pathway; reduced angiogenesis. However, notes that the mechanistic evidence does not align well with clinical outcomes, since no effect on advanced PCa was observed.

## Claims to Verify

1. **"15,891 cases and 592,479 participants"** -- These figures appear in the text. Cross-checking Table 1 data would confirm, but sample sizes in individual studies (e.g., 47,894 for Giovannucci 1995, 137,001 for Key 2007) are consistent with the total being in this range. Seems plausible.

2. **Abstract vs. body discrepancy on dietary lycopene RR:** Abstract reports RR=0.86 (95% CI: 0.75-0.98) for dietary lycopene; body text reports overall RR=0.88 (95% CI: 0.76-1.02, p=0.083). The abstract figure appears to reflect post-sensitivity-analysis or cohort-only results. This inconsistency should be noted.
