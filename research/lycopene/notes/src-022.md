# src-022: CAM Cancer Collaboration - Lycopene Summary

**Source:** CAM Cancer Collaboration (Lorenc A, Dennert G). "Lycopene." Nov 9, 2020.
**URL:** https://cam-cancer.org/lycopene
**Type:** Web-based evidence summary (non-peer-reviewed but systematic)
**Quality:** ok

## Core Findings

1. There is **insufficient high-quality evidence** to support lycopene supplementation for prostate cancer treatment or prevention, despite promising epidemiologic associations and biological plausibility from in vitro/animal studies.
2. Lycopene supplementation appears generally safe in men at supranutritional doses (~10 mg/day), with no pattern of adverse effects in clinical trials, though commercial supplement content varies by +/-38-42% from stated amounts.
3. Epidemiologic evidence shows associations between higher lycopene intake and reduced prostate cancer risk, but RCTs fail to confirm causality.

## Key Evidence

### Prostate Cancer Treatment
- **Haseen 2009 SR**: 8 intervention studies (2 RCTs, 1 non-randomized, 5 uncontrolled) -- all used PSA as surrogate. Only one RCT (Ansari 2003, n=54) examined clinical outcomes (metastatic prostate cancer, orchidectomy + lycopene 4 mg/day); found improved survival and bone metastasis response, but methodological shortcomings prevent generalization.
- **Paur 2016 RCT**: 79 prostate cancer patients randomized to tomato products (30 mg lycopene/day), tomato products + supplements, or control for 3 weeks. No PSA difference overall; sub-analysis suggested benefit in intermediate-risk patients only.

### Prostate Cancer Prevention
- **Cochrane SR (Ilic 2011)**: 3 RCTs -- concluded "insufficient evidence to either support or refute" lycopene for prevention. Mohanty 2005 (n=40) reported 10% vs 30% cancer rate but very small and unclear bias.
- **Cui 2017 SR (n=13)**: Meta-analysis of HGPIN studies -- lycopene decreased prostate cancer risk but not significantly.
- **Beynon 2018 RCT**: 128 men with raised PSA; tomato products vs tomato + supplements vs standard care. No PSA differences except subgroup of intermediate-risk patients.

### Mechanisms of Action
- In vitro: growth inhibition, apoptosis induction, G0/G1 cell cycle arrest, MMP-7 inhibition, decreased genomic instability, reduced angiogenesis
- SR by Applegate 2019 supports biological plausibility that lycopene interacts with androgen axis in prostate cancer
- Lycopene linked to androgen metabolism, lowered testosterone in mice (Ford 2012)
- Key caveat: "much of this data is from in vitro studies, and the metabolism and biological effects of lycopene are still not fully understood"
- Some studies found NO effect: lycopene at physiological levels did not significantly affect proliferation of normal and malignant cells (Burgess 2008); lycopene did not reduce carcinogenesis in TRAMP mice (Rowles 2020, Conlon 2015)

### Bioavailability
- Absorption higher with dietary lipids and from processed tomato products vs raw tomatoes
- Peak plasma levels at 0.5-6 hours post-ingestion; elimination half-life 2-5 days
- Detectable in skin for up to 42 days (Ross 2011)
- Novel delivery systems (nanoparticles, lipid-based) improved bioavailability in animal studies

### Cardiovascular Evidence
- Cheng 2017 SR: epidemiologic evidence associates higher lycopene/tomato intake with reduced CVD risk (noted briefly, not a primary focus)

### Safety
- Generally safe in men; no adverse effect pattern across trials
- One RCT in pregnant women showed higher rates of preterm labor and low birth weight (Banerjee 2009)
- Tomato products (but not lycopene alone) may inhibit CYP3A4 -- caution with drugs metabolized by this enzyme
- Commercial supplement content varies +/-38-42% from label (Feifer 2002)

## Methodology
This is a structured evidence summary by the CAM Cancer Collaboration, covering systematic reviews, RCTs, and observational studies. It synthesizes evidence across cancer treatment, prevention, and supportive care using a narrative review approach with evidence tables. Not itself a systematic review, but draws on Cochrane and other SRs.

## Limitations
- Not a peer-reviewed publication; an online evidence summary last updated November 2020
- No explicit search methodology or inclusion/exclusion criteria reported
- Does not provide detailed quantitative results (effect sizes, confidence intervals) for most studies
- Cardiovascular and mortality outcomes mentioned only in passing

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (cohort studies and RCTs on lycopene and prostate cancer):** Highly relevant. Reviews the state of RCT evidence (Haseen 2009 SR, Paur 2016, Beynon 2018, Cochrane Ilic 2011) and concludes evidence is insufficient. Notes epidemiologic associations exist but RCTs do not confirm causality.
- **Q2 (bioavailability supplements vs whole food):** Relevant. Notes absorption is higher from processed tomato products than raw tomatoes; higher in presence of dietary lipids. Provides pharmacokinetic data (half-life, peak levels).
- **Q4 (tradeoffs supplements vs whole food):** Relevant. Notes supplement content varies +/-38-42% from stated amounts; tomato products but not lycopene alone may inhibit CYP3A4 (suggesting whole-food matrix effects).
- **Q5 (contradicting evidence on prostate cancer):** Relevant. Highlights that RCTs fail to confirm epidemiologic associations; Cochrane concluded insufficient evidence; some animal studies found no protective effect.
- **Q6 (mechanisms):** Relevant but indirect. Lists proposed mechanisms (antioxidant, anti-proliferative, androgen axis interaction) but notes much evidence is in vitro and results are discrepant. Key point: mechanisms are biologically plausible but do not clearly align with clinical outcomes.

## Claims to Verify
- Supplement content varies +/-38-42%: attributed to Feifer 2002, a single study; may be outdated for current products.
- Mohanty 2005 n=40 with 10% vs 30% cancer rate: very small trial, needs corroboration of exact numbers from original source.
