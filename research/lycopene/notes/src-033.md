# src-033: Lycopene/Tomato Consumption and the Risk of Prostate Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Prospective Studies

**Authors:** Chen J, Song Y, Zhang L  
**Year:** 2013  
**Venue:** Journal of Nutritional Science and Vitaminology  
**Citations:** 79  
**DOI:** 10.3177/jnsv.59.213  
**Quality:** ok (full text available, 43K chars, 366 sentences)

## Core Findings

This meta-analysis of 17 prospective studies (6 cohort + 11 nested case-control, published 1989-2011) found that all inverse associations between lycopene/tomato intake and prostate cancer were suggestive but statistically non-significant. The authors conclude that tomato may play a "modest role" in prostate cancer prevention, but the evidence is not strong enough to recommend lycopene supplementation.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Pooled ORs (highest vs. lowest intake, random-effects model):
- **Raw tomato intake:** OR 0.81 (95% CI 0.59-1.10), I-squared=74%, p=0.03 for heterogeneity
- **Cooked tomato intake (tomato sauce):** OR 0.85 (95% CI 0.69-1.06), I-squared=64%, p=0.09
- **Lycopene intake:** OR 0.93 (95% CI 0.86-1.01), I-squared=18%, p=0.30 -- most homogeneous
- **Serum lycopene:** OR 0.97 (95% CI 0.88-1.08), I-squared=0%, p=0.52 -- no effect

### Advanced prostate cancer subgroup:
- **Lycopene intake (4 studies):** OR 1.03 (95% CI 0.83-1.26), I-squared=4% -- no protective effect
- **Serum lycopene (4 studies):** OR 0.77 (95% CI 0.49-1.20), I-squared=71% -- suggestive but NS with high heterogeneity
- **Combined tomato/cooked tomato (HPFC only):** RR 0.65 (95% CI 0.44-0.95) for >10 vs <1.5 servings/week -- single study

### Sensitivity analysis for lycopene intake:
- Excluding Kristal 2010: OR 0.88 (95% CI 0.80-0.98) -- becomes significant
- Excluding Giovannucci 2002: OR 0.98 (95% CI 0.88-1.08) -- no effect

## Methodology

- **Search:** OVID MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, Springer, CNKI, CENTRAL; studies published before January 2012; no language restrictions
- **Included:** 11 cohort studies and 6 nested case-control studies (total 17); one excluded for insufficient data
- **Quality assessment:** STROBE guidelines; two independent reviewers
- **Analysis:** Fixed-effect and random-effects models using Review Manager 5.0
- **Heterogeneity:** Chi-squared and I-squared statistics; publication bias assessed via funnel plots

## Key Limitations

1. **Significant heterogeneity** for raw tomato (I-squared=74%) and cooked tomato (I-squared=64%) analyses
2. **FFQ variability:** Studies used FFQs ranging from 20 items to 736 items, complicating cross-study comparisons
3. **Single serum measurement** in blood-based studies may misclassify long-term exposure
4. **Recall bias** in dietary studies
5. **Residual confounding** -- few studies adjusted for all important factors (e.g., alcohol)
6. **Cooked tomato analysis** based primarily on tomato sauce data due to lack of standardized serving definitions
7. **No universal consensus** for measuring lycopene intake or developing qualified FFQs
8. **Small study counts** in subgroup analyses (3-4 studies each)

## Notable Contextual Points

- The FDA found no credible evidence supporting lycopene intake association with reduced prostate cancer risk (2004 review of qualified health claims)
- The World Cancer Research Fund (2007) suggested high fruit/vegetable intake (including lycopene) may be beneficial for prostate cancer
- The authors explicitly state: "existing evidence is not overwhelming enough to recommend the use of lycopene supplements in the prevention of prostate cancer"
- Cooked tomato did NOT show a stronger protective effect than raw tomato, contradicting the bioavailability hypothesis
