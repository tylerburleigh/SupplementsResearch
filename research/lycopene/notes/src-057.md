# src-057: The Mediterranean Diet Reduces the Risk and Mortality of Prostate Cancer: A Narrative Review

**Authors:** Capurso C, Vendemiale G (2017)
**Venue:** Frontiers in Nutrition
**Citations:** 49
**Quality:** ok -- full text available, substantive narrative review

## Core Findings

This narrative review synthesizes observational and case-control evidence on how Mediterranean Diet (MD) adherence reduces prostate cancer risk and mortality. The authors conclude that the MD's protective effect is attributable to its whole dietary pattern rather than any single component, though they highlight olive oil, lycopene (from cooked tomato sauce), and polyphenol-rich fruits and vegetables as key contributors. For prostate cancer specifically, the strongest evidence they cite is a meta-analysis (Schwingshackl & Hoffmann 2014) showing 4% risk reduction with high MD adherence (RR 0.96, 95% CI 0.92-0.99, p=0.03) based on 29,867 prostate cancer cases from 1,431,461 subjects.

## Key Evidence on Lycopene and Prostate Cancer

1. **Gann et al. (1999) - Physicians' Health Study:** Case-control analysis showing aggressive prostate cancer risk was significantly reduced with high lycopene concentration (5th vs 1st quintile: unadjusted OR=0.56, 95% CI 0.34-0.92, p=0.02). For highest vs lowest plasma lycopene levels: OR=0.40, 95% CI 0.19-0.84, p=0.006.

2. **Key et al. (2015) - Pooled analysis of 15 studies (11,239 cases, 18,541 controls):**
   - Advanced prostate cancer: 80% increase in lycopene OR=0.73, 95% CI 0.54-0.99, p=0.036
   - Aggressive prostate cancer: 80% increase in lycopene OR=0.72, 95% CI 0.53-0.97, p=0.025
   - Alpha-tocopherol showed similar protective effects

3. **Bioavailability note:** Plasma lycopene significantly increased after consumption of tomato meals cooked in olive oil vs tomatoes cooked without olive oil (citing Fielding et al. 2005). Cooking activates lycopene and improves intestinal absorption, especially with fats.

## Mechanisms Discussed

- Lycopene exerts antioxidant properties via modulation of downregulation mechanisms of the inflammatory response
- Phenolic compounds in olive oil have chemopreventive effects via antioxidant actions and effects on cancer cell signaling, cell cycle progression, and proliferation
- MD components collectively suppress spontaneous DNA mutations, regulate cell proliferation, induce DNA methylation and apoptosis
- Pilot study (n=20, Bishop et al. 2015): 3-month MD adherence showed inverse association with DNA damage (p=0.013), protective against peroxide-induced DNA damage (p=0.009)
- Green tea and legume consumption were protective against DNA damage; red meat was associated with DNA damage

## Broader Context on Diet and Prostate Cancer

- **BMI:** Dual effect -- decreased risk for localized prostate cancer (RR 0.94 per 5 kg/m2 increase) but increased risk for advanced prostate cancer (RR 1.09 per 5 kg/m2 increase) (Discacciati et al.)
- **Red/processed meat:** NIH-AARP study (175,343 men): red meat HR=1.12 for total prostate cancer; processed meat HR=1.32 for advanced prostate cancer. However, pooled analysis of 15 cohorts (842,149 men) found no significant association for unprocessed or processed red meat.
- **Dairy:** Meta-analysis of 32 prospective studies (Aune et al.): total dairy RR=1.07 per 400 g/day; dietary calcium RR=1.05 per 400 mg/day
- **Fish consumption:** Meta-analysis of 12 cohort studies (445,820 men) found no association with prostate cancer incidence (RR 1.01, p=0.83), but significant reduction in prostate cancer mortality from 4 pooled cohort studies (RR 0.37, 95% CI 0.18-0.74, p=0.005)

## MD and Cancer Mortality

- Trichopoulou et al. (2003): 2-point increment in MD score associated with 25% reduction in total mortality (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.64-0.87) and 24% reduction in cancer mortality (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.59-0.98)
- Schwingshackl & Hoffmann meta-analysis: high MD adherence associated with 10% reduction in both cancer mortality and incidence (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.86-0.95)

## Methodology

Narrative review (not systematic). Analyzed observational studies (cohort and case-control) on overweight, dietary components, and MD adherence effects on prostate cancer risk. No formal systematic search strategy or risk-of-bias assessment described. Includes summary tables of key studies.

## Limitations

1. **Narrative review, not systematic** -- no predefined search strategy, inclusion criteria, or quality assessment; susceptible to selective citation
2. **Published 2017** -- does not include more recent RCTs or large cohort analyses
3. **Lycopene evidence is secondary** -- the paper is primarily about the MD as a whole dietary pattern, not lycopene specifically
4. **No RCT evidence for lycopene** -- all cited lycopene-prostate cancer evidence is observational (case-control, pooled analyses of observational studies)
5. **Potential confounding** -- MD adherers may differ systematically in health behaviors; the authors acknowledge protective effects appear due to the whole food pattern rather than isolated components

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (prospective cohort/RCTs):** Moderate relevance. Cites Key et al. 2015 pooled analysis of 15 studies and Gann et al. 1999 Physicians' Health Study data on lycopene and prostate cancer. No RCT evidence discussed. Also provides broader context of dietary pattern evidence.
- **Q5 (contradicting evidence):** Low relevance. This paper supports the lycopene-prostate cancer benefit narrative rather than contradicting it.
- **Q6 (mechanisms):** Moderate relevance. Discusses antioxidant properties, modulation of inflammatory response, and DNA damage protection, but mechanisms are described at a general level without detailed molecular pathways.
- **Q2 (bioavailability):** Low-to-moderate relevance. Notes that cooking with olive oil improves lycopene bioavailability (citing Fielding et al. 2005), but does not compare supplements vs whole-food sources.

## Claims to Verify

- "The protective effect of the MD against the prostate cancer is also due to the high consumption of tomato sauce" -- this is an editorial conclusion, not directly tested in the reviewed studies
- Schwingshackl & Hoffmann 4% prostate cancer risk reduction (RR 0.96) is statistically significant but clinically modest; the CI (0.92-0.99) barely excludes null
- The Gann et al. 1999 findings (OR=0.40 for aggressive prostate cancer with highest lycopene) are based on a nested case-control within the Physicians' Health Study with 578 cases -- should verify whether this has been replicated in more recent analyses
