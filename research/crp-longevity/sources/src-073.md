INTRODUCTION 24 
C-reactive protein (CRP) is a widely used as a clinical parameter of to measure inflammatory 25 
status, and numerous observational studies have reported associations between elevated CRP levels and 26 
the risk of colorectal cancer (CRC) incidence risk [1,2], population mortality due to CRC  [3,4], and 27 
the clinical prognosis of colorectal cancer ( CRC) patients, including death  [5,6], metastasis [5,7], 28 
postoperative complications [8,9], and recurrence [5,10,11]. For the prognosis of CRC, three Three 29 
studies of systematic reviews and meta-analyseis have updated provided up-to-date information on the 30 
association between elevated CRP levels and a poor prognosis of CRC  [10,12,13]. For various other 31 
solid tumors, as well as CRC, CRP is is one of thea variables that must be included when constructing 32 
a prognostic prediction model [10], including the Glasgow Prognostic Score (GPS), which is known to 33 
effectively predict the prognosis of CRC patients  [14]. However, in these observational studies, it is 34 
difficult to differentiate between the deterioration of cancer -related inflammation and the clinical 35 
impact of elevated CRP levels themselves, because most studies used theanalyzed peri-treatment CRP 36 
levels. The best timing for the evaluation ofevaluating CRP-related markers is stillremains unclear [6]. 37 
In addition, since the association between cancer progression and an immune response is often 38 
bidirectional and multifactorial, it is difficult to avoid reverse causality and residual confounding effects 39 
in observational studies [15]. To overcome these limitations and clarify the true causality, Mendelian 40 
randomization (MR) using genotypes as instrumental variables has been widely applied. Because 41 
genetic va riants are randomly allocated by Mendel’s law, an MR study using genetic variants as 42 
instrumental variables can be independent of potential confounders and can exclude the possibility of 43 
reverse causality. However, constructing large data sets with interme diate phenotypes and genetic 44 
instruments is challenging due to the high cost of measurement s and/or the lack of suitable biological 45 
specimens. On the other hand In this context , two2-sample MR can evaluate the association between 46 
exposure and outcome using two 2 independent existing genome-wide association studies ( GWASs), 47 
and the recent proportion of two 2-sample MR is steadily becoming more common increasing in 48 
studiesin research using MR analysis [16] 49

Sensitivity analyses 163 
Mendelian randomization MR reliesd on three 3 assumptions. First, genetic variants are 164 
associated with the exposure (CRP). Second, genetic variants are not associated with potential 165 
confounders. Third, genetic variants are not directly associated with  the outcome (death), except 166 
through the exposure (a lack of horizontal pleiotropic effects) . In our study,  the selected SNPs were 167 
validated in the CRP GWAS and were independent of each other (not in linkage disequilibriumLD). In 168 
addition, using Phenoscanner, a database of GWAS results, the pleiotropic effects of the selected SNPs 169 
was confirmedwere checked [38], and the an association of rs429358 near APOE with the blood lipid 170 
profile was reported [39,40]. SoTherefore, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis excluding 171 
rs429358. 172 
For two2-sample MR, three 3 sensitivity analyses (simple and weighted median, and MR -173 
Egger regression) were performed. Although the IVW method is sensitive to violation s of the 174 
assumption on regarding pleiotropy, the results from the simple and weighted median are consistent 175 
even when up to 50% of the information comes from invalid instrumental variables [41]. The intercept 176 
estimated from the MR-Egger regression provides an estimate of the horizontal pleiotropic effect [42]. 177

Regarding the association between CRP levels and CRC survival, in contrast contrary to our 249

Chronic inflammation induces cancer invasion, progression, and metastasis, and influences the 268 
efficacy of chemotherapy and immunotherapy [50]. CRP, which is is synthesized in the liver, and is an 269 
acute-phase protein that reflects inflammation. However, we did no t find an effect of genetically 270 
predicted CRP levels on CRC survival as a systemic inflammatory mediator, unlike many previous 271 
case–control studies [5,7,15], and prospective studies [3,4], and meta-analyseis [10,12,13]. However, 272 
since the potential causal role of CRP was not confirmed in previous Mendelian studies, the observed 273 
effect of CRP elevation on CRC mortality may be due to residual effects or reverse causality. 274 
Our study had several limitations. First, since inf ormation on recurrence was not available, 275 
disease-free survival was not included in the analysis. Second, the batch -effect may not have been 276 
excluded in CRP and CRC GWAS.  Therefore, to minimize the batch  -effect, we evaluated the 277

association between CRP  levels and mortality in CRC patients, future studies with higher statistical 283

by by the genetic instruments should be discussed in terms of to discuss the biological effects of selected 285 
SNPs or CRP levels on cancer mortality to compensate fory, because of the lack of functional analysis 286 
of SNPs. 287

the overall or CRC -specific survival of CRC patients. Therefore, our results suggest that genetically 289 
predisposed circulating CRP levels do not play a causal role in the prognosis of CRC.  290
