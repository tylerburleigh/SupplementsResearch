Abstract

Objectives

Negative self-perceptions of aging (SPA) and an older subjective age are associated with a greater likelihood of cardiovascular disease. We predicted that C-reactive protein (CRP), a marker of stress-related inflammation, would mediate this relation.
Method

Data from up to 6 602 participants ages 50–101 in the Health and Retirement Study (2008–2018) were analyzed using logistic regressions with mediation. Subjective aging was assessed in 2008 or 2010, CRP was assessed 2 years later, and diagnosis of heart disease or stroke was assessed in the years from CRP measurement to 2018.
Results

Significant indirect effects occurred in all models, indicating that negative SPA and older subjective age are associated with a greater likelihood of heart disease and stroke through elevated levels of the inflammatory marker CRP.
Discussion

These findings suggest that psychological factors such as subjective aging impact cardiovascular health through physiological mechanisms, specifically markers of inflammation.
Keywords:
Cardiovascular disease, Inflammation, Stroke, Views on aging

Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of mortality in the United States, accounting for over 20% of deaths in 2020 (
Ahmad & Anderson, 2021
). In older adulthood, ischemic heart disease and stroke are the greatest contributors to disease burden (
Global Burden of Cardiovascular Diseases Collaboration, 2018
). Although many predictors of cardiovascular health are biological (e.g., hypertension) or behavioral (e.g., cigarette smoking), a biopsychosocial perspective suggests that psychological constructs might also be impactful.

One phenomenon associated with a variety of health outcomes in later life is subjective aging and its constructs of self-perceptions of aging (SPA) and subjective age (
Westerhof & Wurm, 2018
). Stereotype embodiment theory proposes that cultural norms and beliefs about aging are assimilated into adults’ beliefs about their own aging processes (
Levy, 2009
). Stereotype embodiment theory and past research suggest that, when negative, older adults’ SPA (i.e., evaluations and experiences of one’s own aging process) can induce stress and worsen health (
Levy, 2009
). Similarly, evidence suggests that an older subjective age, or feeling older than one’s chronological age, predicts negative health outcomes (
Westerhof & Wurm, 2018
) and does so to a stronger degree than the reverse (health predicting subjective age;
Spuling et al., 2013
).

Recent studies have shown that older subjective age and negative SPA are longitudinally associated with a heightened risk of cardiovascular events (
Stephan et al., 2020
) and cross-sectionally with higher levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), a protein associated with inflammation (
Stephan et al., 2015
). Because inflammation is deleterious to cardiovascular health (
Cushman et al., 2005
), elevated levels of CRP might mediate the association between both older subjective age and more negative SPA and cardiovascular problems. Indeed,
Levy and Bavishi (2018)
found that positive SPA was associated with increased longevity via lower levels of CRP in adults aged 50–99 participating in the Health and Retirement Study (HRS). That study was the first to test for a biological mechanism linking SPA and survival prospectively. Therefore, the purpose of the present study was to extend
Levy and Bavishi (2018)
and examine the association between SPA and cardiovascular problems (i.e., heart disease and stroke) over time, and to determine whether elevated levels of CRP mediate this relation. Additionally, we expand the conceptualization of subjective aging by including both SPA and subjective age in the association with CRP and subsequent development of cardiovascular problems.

Method

Participants

HRS is a nationally representative survey of middle-aged and older adults and their spouses (
Sonnega et al., 2014
). The core HRS measures are assessed biennially and a rotating 50% of the sample may complete additional measures, including biomarker and psychosocial data, every four years. To maximize sample size, we collapsed two waves of data for our baseline assessment, drawing our sample from participants aged 50–101 who completed demographic and psychosocial measures in 2008 or 2010 (T1), biomarker measures two years later (T2), and were free of heart disease (in the heart disease models) or stroke (in the stroke models) at T2. We measured incidence of cardiovascular disease as occurring in the years from biomarker collection to 2018 (T3), a six-to-eight-year span, depending on the year of T2 data collection. Due to variations in missing data and disease diagnoses, sample sizes differ between models: SPA with heart disease,
n
= 6 151; subjective age with heart disease,
n
= 5 939; SPA with stroke,
n
= 6 602; subjective age with stroke,
n
= 6 284.
Measures

Dependent variables

Respondents reported whether a physician has ever diagnosed them with
heart disease
(i.e., “heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems”) or
stroke
every two years. Responses were dichotomized (0 =
no
, 1 =
yes
) and represent whether participants were diagnosed with heart disease or stroke from T2 to T3.
Independent variables

SPA
was assessed with the mean score of eight items (e.g., “So far, I am satisfied with the way I am aging”;
Lawton, 1975
;
Liang & Bollen, 1983
). We reverse-coded positive items to create a scale in which higher scores represent less favorable (i.e., more negative) SPA. This scale had acceptable reliability (heart disease sample α = 0.809; stroke sample α = 0.811).

Subjective age
was assessed with the item: “What age do you feel?” to which respondents provided an age in years. Like others (
Stephan et al., 2020
), we calculated a discrepancy proportion by subtracting participants’ chronological age from their felt age, then dividing by the chronological age. Per this calculation, although the raw difference between two participants’ chronological and subjective ages may be identical (e.g., five years), the younger participant has a higher discrepancy score than the older participant (
Westerhof et al., 2014
). Thus, an older subjective age is weighted more strongly for younger participants than their older counterparts. We removed outliers by setting responses more than three standard deviations from the mean to missing, resulting in a removal of 11 cases (0.2%) from the heart disease sample and 76 cases (1.2%) from the stroke sample. In the heart disease sample, most excluded participants reported feeling older than their chronological age, whereas in the stroke sample, equal numbers of excluded participants reported feeling younger as older.
Mediating variable

Evidence shows that CRP is a reliable predictor of cardiovascular health over time (
Danesh et al., 2004
). Furthermore, it is more stable and has a longer half-life than other inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., interleukin 1 and 6), making it more easily detectable and appropriate for longitudinal analyses (
Danesh et al., 2004
;
Libby & Ridker, 2004
). CRP was assessed with blood samples. Participants provided dried blood spots by pressing a pricked finger on paper, which was then analyzed by the University of Washington (
Crimmins et al., 2015
;
Crimmins et al., 2017
). Results of dried blood samples are similar to those from intravenous methods (
Crimmins et al., 2014
). Following HRS recommendations, we used the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) equivalent value from the HRS for comparability with whole blood samples. We log transformed scores to reduce skewness and kurtosis.
Covariates

Following
Stephan et al. (2020)
, we included several covariates that may be related to cardiovascular disease. Age and education were recorded in years and included as continuous variables. Gender (0 =
men
, 1 =
women
), race (0 =
White
, 1 =
non-White
), ethnicity (0 =
non-Hispanic,
1 =
Hispanic
), smoking history (0 =
never
, 1 =
former/current
), baseline hypertension (0 =
no
, 1 =
yes
), and baseline diabetes (0 =
no
, 1 =
yes
) were included as binary variables. Physical inactivity was included as the mean of two measures: frequency of vigorous and moderate physical activity (1 =
every day
, 5 =
never
). Depressive symptoms were assessed with the eight-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D;
Radloff, 1977
) scale in which higher scores indicate greater depressive symptoms in the past week (range 0–8).
Analytic Strategy

Because the analytic sample included some respondents nested within households (e.g., married spouses; heart problems:
n
= 1 970 [subjective age] to 2 066 [SPA]; stroke:
n
= 2 684 [subjective age] to
n
= 2 886 [SPA]), we first tested for nonindependence in the outcome variables with Cohen’s kappa (
Kenny et al., 2006
). Results were not significant, indicating no interdependence between coresiding respondents on likelihood of heart disease (SPA
κ
= −0.001,
p
= .972; subjective age
κ
= −0.010,
p
= .670) or stroke (SPA
κ
= 0.012,
p

κ

Next, we tested the primary hypotheses using logistic regression models with mediation in Mplus Version 8.5 (
Muthén & Muthén, 1998–2017
). Analyzing SPA and subjective age separately for each outcome, we conducted four fully adjusted models with bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals.

Discussion

Our indirect effects hypotheses were supported, such that older adults who perceive their aging process negatively (i.e., negative SPA) and/or feel older than their chronological age (i.e., higher subjective age) at T1 had higher CRP levels at T2 and, in turn, a greater likelihood of being diagnosed with heart disease or stroke between T2 and T3. To date, most research on the mechanisms of subjective aging has addressed behaviors (
Westerhof & Wurm, 2018
), yet our analyses provide evidence that the subjective evaluation and experience of aging may also impact health outcomes via physiological pathways. To the extent that older adults evaluate themselves as feeling older than their actual age and have negative perceptions of how well they are aging, they may experience heightened stress reactivity that in turn promotes elevated levels of circulating inflammatory markers such as CRP. These elevated levels of CRP are subsequently linked to the likelihood of developing heart disease or stroke in later adulthood.

Our findings add to a growing body of literature demonstrating that psychological processes influence older adults’ health outcomes via biological mechanisms such as inflammation over time. Despite the contributions of our study (e.g., longitudinal design, biomarker measures, and nationally representative data), there are important limitations. First, the cardiovascular health measures only account for physician diagnoses; some participants may have undiagnosed diseases or severe risks at the threshold of a cardiovascular event. Second, future studies should include a measure of stress response. We theorized that negative subjective aging may induce greater stress reactivity, raising inflammation, and heightening the risk of cardiovascular events. Past work has documented the association between views on aging and perceived stress (
Witzel et al., 2022
), as well as biological indicators of stress (i.e., cortisol;
Levy et al., 2016
). It may be fruitful to estimate a model incorporating both inflammation and stress, which could determine whether one’s stress response links psychological processes like subjective aging to inflammatory responses. It is likely that biological pathways of subjective aging work in conjunction with behavioral pathways such as physical activity and engaging in preventative healthcare; future work should incorporate both pathways to estimate cumulative effects. Further, as biomarker data becomes more accessible, pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin 6 which stimulates CRP production (
Libby & Ridker, 2004
), should be explored. Future research should also continue to explore what influences older adults’ perceptions of their aging process, as such work might point to additional points of intervention (e.g., reducing ageism in the media;
Boeder et al., 2021
) that could positively impact health in later life. Taken together, the findings of the current study underscore the importance of considering psychological processes (i.e., older adults’ views of aging) alongside biological processes for optimizing understanding of cardiovascular health in later life.
