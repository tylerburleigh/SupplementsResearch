Abstract  8 
Mendelian randomization (MR) is an established epidemiological technique which 9 
uses genetic variants to strengthen causal inference regarding modifiable exposures. 10 
Non-linear MR is an extension to MR which aims to estimate whether the effect 11 
differs across the level of the exposure. Many applications of non-linear MR have 12 
focused on Vitamin D as an exposure.  Using this technique, the study sample is 13 
divided into strata, and separate estimates are calculated in each stratum to estimate 14 
causal effects at different levels of the exposure (e.g. Vitamin D). For example, a 15 
recent study which applied this method identified an apparent protective effect of 16 
Vitamin D on C-reactive protein (CRP) levels for those with poor Vitamin D status. 17 
However, recent work has highlighted that the commonly used non-linear MR 18 
approaches are susceptible to serious bias, suggesting that further methodological 19 
development incorporating extensive simulation and empirical investigation is 20 
required. In this paper, we provide a commentary on the sources of bias in non-linear 21 
MR methods with a re-examination of the relationship between Vitamin D and CRP 22 
as an applied example. We highlight the role of negative controls and non-collider 23

Introduction  26 
Mendelian randomization (MR) is an established epidemiological technique 27 
which uses genetic variants to strengthen causal inference regarding modifiable 28 
exposures in the presence of unmeasured confounding (1, 2). MR analyses test the 29 
overall exposure-outcome relationship in the population by incorporating genetic 30 
variants found to reliably associate with the exposure into an instrumental variable 31 
(IV) analysis (2). Non-linear MR is an extension to MR which aims to estimate 32 
whether the effect differs across different levels of the exposure. In non-linear MR 33 
analyses the study sample is divided into strata, and separate IV estimates are 34 
calculated in each stratum to estimate causal effects across levels of the exposure 35 
(3, 4). This approach has the potential to answer important questions around the 36 
shape of an exposure-outcome relationship. However recent work has highlighted 37 
that the current analytical framework is highly susceptible to bias, suggesting there 38 
should be a pause in publication until these issues are addressed (5, 6).  39 
  The first developed non-linear MR method is known as the “residual” method,  40 
the stratification procedure for which is based on the residuals of the regression of 41 
the IV on the exposure (3, 4), thus representing the “IV-free” exposure. MR analyses 42 
are then run within each stratum and a non-linear curve is estimated by parametric 43 
methods. This strategy aims to avoid collider bias which would be induced by 44 
stratifying on the exposure directly (7). The method is dependent on the strict 45 
assumption that the genetic effect on the exposure is constant across levels of the 46 
exposure. This method has been widely applied (8-14), however results produced by 47

the required assumption that genetic effects are constant across different levels of 49 
the exposure (15, 16). For example, the residual method was applied to investigate 50 
putative non-linear effects of Vitamin D on coronary heart disease and all-cause 51 
mortality (14). The results of this investigation attributed a protective effect to Vitamin 52 
D on all-cause mortality in individuals with low Vitamin D status. Violations of the 53 
assumptions of MR are evidenced by a precisely estimated null effect in the overall 54 
relationship, but the results of non-linear MR suggesting directionally protective 55 
effects of elevating Vitamin D across all strata with substantial benefit in the lowest 56 
strata (14). The publication has now been formally retracted (17), due to clearly 57 
spurious findings (18, 19) and with the authors stating that their original results were 58 
“a logical impossibility” (20). A second paper sharing a similar research question and 59 
data as the previous example, describes the relationship between Vitamin D and 60 
cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk using the residual non-linear MR method (21). 61 
This analysis produced similarly spurious results, where effects in the protective 62 
direction were reported for increasing Vitamin D across all strata on CVD risk, but a 63 
null effect in the overall population and the study was subsequently retracted (17, 21, 64 
22).  65 
The developers of the original method have since proposed the alternative 66 
“doubly-ranked” method which aims to more robustly estimate non-linear exposure-67 
outcome relationships (23). The doubly-ranked method uses a more complex 68 
stratification procedure to derive strata with different average levels of the exposure 69 
independent of the genetic IV. This is achieved via two-steps. First the population is 70 
ranked and assigned into “pre-strata” based on level of the genetic IV. Then within 71 
pre-stratum, individuals with the lowest level of the exposure are assigned to 72

stratum-2, and so on. This approach relies on the “rank preservation assumption”, 74 
i.e. that the relative ranking of individuals by their exposure would be preserved 75 
across levels of the genetic IV. This is a strictly weaker parametric assumption 76 
regarding the IV and exposure compared to the residual method, with the 77 
stratification process being described in more detail in the paper introducing the 78 
method (23). While this has been shown in simulations to be less sensitive to 79 
heterogeneity in the genetic effect across strata (23), the performance of this 80 
methodology has been shown to be highly sensitive to other sources of bias (5, 6, 81 
16).  82 
In this paper we comprehensively interrogate methods that can detect bias in 83 
non-linear MR analyses (5, 6). We demonstrate empirically in a re-examination of the 84 
relationship between serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D concentration (i.e. Vitamin D) and 85 
C-reactive protein (CRP) as described by Zhou et al (9) how sensitivity tests can be 86 
applied to detect potential sources of bias in the non-linear association. Further, we 87 
demonstrate a stratification approach based on a non-collider variable (Townsend 88 
deprivation index) as a powerful sensitivity test for the detection of true non-linear 89 
associations. This is based on stratification by a variable which is not itself plausibly 90 
influenced by the exposure and outcome, but where sub-groups exhibit different 91 
average levels of the exposure. Finally, we advocate that until there is a more 92 
reliable demonstration of a non-linear method which is robust to bias, publications 93 
applying this method should be paused, or at the very least be viewed with 94 
considerable caution. 95

Methods  96 
UK Biobank cohort  97 
UK Biobank is a population-based health research resource consisting of 98 
approximately 500,000 people, aged between 38 years and 73 years, who were 99 
recruited between the years 2006 and 2010 from across the UK (24). Particularly 100 
focused on identifying determinants of human diseases in middle-aged and older 101 
individuals, participants provided a range of information (such as demographics, 102 
health status, lifestyle measures, cognitive testing, personality self-report, and 103 
physical and mental health measures) via questionnaires and interviews; 104 
anthropometric measures, BP readings and samples of blood, urine and saliva were 105 
also taken (data available at ww.ukbiobank.ac.uk). A full description of the study 106 
design, participants and quality 107 
control (QC) methods have been described in detail previously (25).  108 
We restricted the sample to individuals of white British ancestry who self-109 
report as “White British” and who have very similar ancestral backgrounds according 110 
to the PCA (n=409,703), as described by Bycroft (25). All analyses were conducted 111 
on our in-house quality-controlled UKB dataset, with recommended exclusions 112 
applied (e.g. genetic outliers, individuals with a high degree of relatedness, minimal 113 
relatedness and individuals who have withdrawn consent were excluded from the 114 
sample). A full description of all QC applied to the data has been described in detail 115 
previously (26). Following exclusions, the total sample size used in downstream 116 
analyses was  N= 336, 839 individuals of White British ancestry with measured 117 
phenotypic data for selected variables. UK Biobank received ethical approval from 118 
the Research Ethics Committee (REC reference for UK Biobank is 11/NW/0382). 119

Phenotypic measures  120 
Vitamin D (nmol/L) blood biochemistry assays were conducted in the UKB by 121 
chemiluminescent immunoassay on the DiaSorin LIAISON XL analyzer and provided 122 
in data-field: 30890. C-reactive protein (CRP) (mg/L) blood biochemistry measures 123 
were assayed on a Beckman Coulter AU5800 analyzer and provided in data-field: 124 
30710.  The distribution of CRP measures in the data is highly skewed and as such 125 
values were log-transformed prior to inclusion in analyses. Townsend deprivation 126 
index (TDI) in the UKB was calculated immediately prior to participant joining and is 127 
based on the preceding national census output areas. Individual participants were 128 
assigned a TDI score corresponding to their postcode at recruitment (provided in 129 
data-field: 22189), with higher scores corresponding to higher deprivation.  130 
Additional phenotypes were extracted to examine evidence for an interaction 131 
effect between an individual’s general health status and the IV-exposure relationship. 132 
This was achieved by generating an illness summary score comprising the following 133 
traits: Cystatin C (data-field: 30720), forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) 134 
(data-field: 3063), Albumin (data-field: 30600), cholesterol (data-field: 30690), 135 
HbA1C (data-field: 30750) and history of cancer (self-reported diagnoses by doctor) 136 
(data-field: 2453). All phenotypic data was accessed via the UKB Research Access 137 
Platform (RAP) under UKB application id: 81499.  138

Mendelian randomization analyses  152 
Overall exposure-outcome relationships were estimated by standard MR 153 
analyses in an individual level setting by two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression 154 
using the ‘ivreg’ R package (29). Covariates included sex, age at assessment, 155 
assessment centre, month of assessment, fasting time, genotype chip and the first 156 
10 genetic principal components. Non-linear MR analyses were conducted sing the 157 
‘SUMnlmr’ R package using the standard settings and Gaussian distribution for 158 
continuous outcomes.  We selected to use 10 strata for analyses. Non-linear MR 159 
analyses using both the “residual” and “ranked” methods were conducted separately.   160

For example, considering Vitamin D as the exposure, an individual’s general health 164 
status may plausibly interact with the effect of the genetic IV on measured levels of 165 
the exposure. A test for evidence of an interaction effect on the IV-exposure 166 
relationship was previously demonstrated by Hamilton et al (6), using a proxy 167

MR in participants stratified by a non-collider  190 
We selected TDI as an example of a non-collider because Vitamin D is not 191 
expected to influence geographical location or TDI. Further, differences in mean 192 
Vitamin D are observed across socio-economic class as indicated by TDI in the UKB. 193 
The UKB sample were stratified according to quartiles of TDI, and by season 194 
(Summer: April-September; Winter: October-March). Detail of all sample sizes, mean 195 
Vitamin D measures and proportion of Vitamin D deficiency across these groupings 196 
is provided (Table S2). Individual level MR analyses of the relationship between 197 
Vitamin D and CRP were performed for each group using the ‘ivreg’ R package as 198 
described above. Further sensitivity tests included individual level MR analysis of 199 
Vitamin D on TDI in the overall sample, and both non-linear MR methods.  200 
Secondly, we performed an additional analysis of the effect of Vitamin D on 201 
CRP in participants in the UKB stratified by recruitment centre and by season. In this 202 
analysis we followed the strategy presented in a recent preprint by Burgess et al 203 
(31). The UKB enrolled participants across 22 assessment centres. Participants who 204 
attended Stockport and Wrexham assessment centres were dropped due to very low 205 
sample sizes (<500 participants). Similarly, participants who attended the Swansea 206 
assessment centre in Winter were dropped due to low sample size (<350 207 
participants). As before, individual level MR analyses of the relationship between 208 
Vitamin D and CRP were performed for each group using the ‘ivreg’ R package with 209 
adjustment for age, sex, month of assessment fasting time and the first 10 genetic 210 
principal components. Sub-group derived MR estimates tested for heterogeneity 211 
using Cochran’s Q. Evidence for heterogeneity or non-linearity in the effect was 212 
further assessed by fixed effects meta-regression using the ‘Metafor’ package in R.  213

Estimation of the causal effect of Vitamin D on CRP by MR  215 
  We first estimate the causal effect of Vitamin D on CRP in an individual level 216 
setting, incorporating a focused PRS for Vitamin D as the genetic IV for Vitamin D. 217 
The variance in the trait explained by the focused Vitamin D PRS in the present 218 
analysis was estimated at ~3.9%, aligning with previous studies (14). A precise null 219 
effect was observed in the MR analysis of the overall effect of Vitamin D on CRP 220 
(Beta= -0.0002, standard error (SE)= 0.0005, P=0.72). The F-statistic for this 221 
analysis was large (F=13,727) indicating the result is not likely to be influenced by 222 
weak instrument bias. Further detail of the results provided in Figure 1 and Table 223 
S3.  224

The Vitamin D IV-exposure relationship is modified by health status  257 
Genetic effect heterogeneity and effect modification of the IV-exposure 258 
relationship together are all that is required to generate bias in methods for 259 
conducting non-linear MR as presented originally by Small (30) and expanded upon 260 
by Hamilton (6). While unmeasured confounding of the exposure and outcome can 261 
be assumed for nearly all practical applications of MR, heterogeneity in the genetic 262 
effect via any GxE or GxG effect modification may thus induce bias, with the 263 
magnitude of the bias being  relative to the size of the interaction (6). A variable 264 
which could plausibly interact with the genotype effect on an exposure is a person’s 265 
general health status, or ‘ill-health’. However, ill-health in population studies is 266

Discussion  342 
Non-linear MR is a potentially powerful approach in genetic epidemiology to 343 
help gain insight on the shape of exposure-outcome relationships. However, recent 344 
work has highlighted outstanding challenges due to bias induced by the stratification 345 
procedures used by both the residual and doubly-ranked methods. We examine and 346 
summarise via applied demonstrations the detection of bias in non-linear MR 347 
methodology which has recently been raised via simulation and empirical analysis 348 
(5, 6, 30). In our exploration of potential sources of bias, we decided to focus on the 349 
relationship between Vitamin D and CRP as exemplar. The aim of this was to re-350 
examine the non-linear relationship between Vitamin D and CRP which was 351 
described using the residual method (9) considering recent developments in the non-352 
linear MR methodology and literature.  We have shown by applied example how the 353

Violations of the constant genetic effect and rank preservation assumptions 359 
have been demonstrated previously to occur in the presence of unmeasured 360 
confounding, with the degree of bias relating to the size of the interaction effect (6). 361 
All that is required is an interaction with another variable which can lead to 362 
heterogeneity in the genetic effect to induce bias which is unpredictable in direction 363 
and effect (6, 15). In our example, strong evidence for an interaction is observed 364 
between a proxy-score for ill-health on the IV-Vitamin D relationship which may be 365 
contributing to the inconsistent estimates produced via both residual and doubly-366 
ranked methods. However, ill-health is only one of many plausible interacting 367 
variables for commonly examined exposures.  A primary advantage of conventional 368 
MR analysis is to strengthen insight on exposure-outcome relationships in the 369 
presence of unmeasured confounding. As such, we highlight the need to address the 370 
presence of ubiquitous confounding as an essential area of future development of 371 
stratification-based non-linear MR methods.  372 
While the current non-linear MR methodology is heavily reliant on the 373 
assumptions underlying the stratification procedure, non-linear associations have 374 
been investigated in populations where differences in exposure arise due to a non-375 
collider variable, hence allowing for less strict assumptions about the causal 376 
framework. A prominent example of this is highlighted by the evaluation of the effect 377 
of alcohol on cardiovascular disease risk factors and events by MR (32-34). This 378

prominent when separated by Summer and Winter months. This method of 385 
stratification offers a powerful means to sensitivity test putative non-linear 386 
relationships within sub-groups with different average levels of the exposure, but 387 
which is not itself influenced by the exposure or the IV.  Null estimates were 388 
observed for the effect of Vitamin D on CRP by standard MR across all TDI strata 389 
derived in the present analysis, aligning with the null estimate observed overall. 390 
Further, secondary analyses where this was extended to stratification by recruitment 391 
centre followed by heterogeneity testing were similarly consistent with the null (31).  392 
The recent progress in the development of non-linear MR methods has 393 
highlighted both the importance of and challenges to accurately estimating non-linear 394 
exposure-outcome relationships. We advocate that future development of non-linear 395 
MR methods should consider the careful selection of controls and, when feasible, 396 
non-collider variable-based stratification as a means of sensitivity checking putative 397 
bias. In conclusion, we recommend that strategies to mitigate the sources of bias in 398 
the current non-linear MR techniques need to be implemented before further 399 
applications of the methods. 400
