C-reactive protein and prospective cardiometabolic risk: observational and Mendelian randomization study of ischemic stroke and all-cause death

Download PDF

Abstract
Background and objectives
Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) is a well-established marker of low-grade systemic inflammation often accompanying cardiometabolic diseases like type 2 diabetes. We sought to determine if plasma CRP concentrations can be used as a predictive marker of risk for ischemic stroke and all-cause death in the general population, and to investigate whether elevated plasma CRP has a causal effect on ischemic stroke and all-cause death.
Methods
Observational and one-sample Mendelian randomization analyses were performed in 113,491 individuals from the Copenhagen City Heart Study and the Copenhagen General Population Study. Two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses were performed in up to 575,531 individuals with publicly available data from the CHARGE CIWG, UKBB, FinnGen, and MEGASTROKE.
Results
Observationally in the Copenhagen studies, higher CRP concentrations associated with stepwise higher risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death with the highest hazard ratios of 1.51(95% confidence interval: 1.34, 1.71) and 1.69(1.60, 1.79), respectively. The cumulative incidence of ischemic stroke at age 72 was 57% higher and the cumulative incidence of all-cause death at age 80 was 62% higher in individuals with a plasma CRP ≥ 2 mg/L compared to individuals with a plasma CRP < 2 mg/L. One- and two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses did not support a causal effect of CRP on risk of ischemic stroke, nor of CRP on risk of all-cause death, with odds ratios in studies combined of 1.01(0.98, 1.05) and 1.01(0.98, 1.05), respectively.
Conclusion
In this observational and one- and two-sample Mendelian randomization study, we found that elevated CRP concentrations above the population median of 1.4 mg/L predicted risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death. There was no causal genetic effect of C-reactive protein on risk of stroke or all-cause death.

Research insights
What is currently known about this topic?

Elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) are a well-established prognostic indicator for cardiovascular diseases.

CRP has a strong observational association with an increased risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death.

Clinicians use CRP as a powerful predictive biomarker to identify individuals at higher risk of adverse outcomes.

What is the key research question?

Is the observed association between elevated plasma C-reactive protein and the risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death a causal relationship?

What is new?

Utilizing Mendelian Randomization, we establish robust causal evidence of genetically predicted CRP's effect on ischemic stroke and all-cause death risk.

It provides novel genetic evidence that, despite a strong observational link, CRP does not have a causal effect on ischemic stroke or all-cause death.

Our findings clearly distinguish between CRP's role as a predictive biomarker versus a direct causal factor.

How might this study influence clinical practice?

The study reinforces CRP's use in risk stratification but suggests that lowering CRP may not prevent these outcomes.

Introduction
Worldwide, more than 12.2 million strokes occur every year leading to more than 6.5 million deaths, placing stroke as the second leading cause of death globally [
1
]. Of these, 7.6 million are ischemic strokes causing over 3.2 million deaths [
1
]. High systolic blood pressure, body mass index(BMI), and hyperglycemia are some of the known major risk factors [
1
]. Low-grade inflammation and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) has also been associated with risk of ischemic stroke, as well as risk of recurrent stroke and a more severe prognosis following discharge from hospital after a stroke event [
2
,
3
,
4
,

,

,

]. Elevated CRP is increasingly used in risk prediction of cardiovascular disease [
8
]. However, its role in risk prediction of ischemic stroke in primary prevention remains uncertain [
9
]. Additionally, the potential causal contribution of low-grade inflammation to the risk of ischemic stroke and stroke subtype is not well established. Anti-inflammatory therapies, such as colchicine and canakinumab, has proven beneficial in secondary prevention in patients with coronary heart disease, reducing the risk of cardiovascular events. Whether these agents also reduce the risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death remains unclear [
10
,

,

,

,

,

].
Mendelian randomization (MR) is an epidemiological approach combining traditional epidemiological methods with information on individuals’ genotypes. The result is a less confounded estimate compared to an observational association [
16
]. The method makes use of the random and independent segregation of alleles from parents to offspring at conception. By dividing the population into subgroups by genotype, the subgroups will differ only by a specific phenotype and confounding factors will be largely evenly distributed [
16
]. Also, as alleles are randomized at conception Mendelian randomization is not susceptible to reverse causation.
We hypothesized that if the observational association is causal, genetic variants associated with lifelong elevated CRP would similarly be associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death. We first investigated the observational association between CRP and risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death in the Copenhagen City Heart Study (CCHS) and the Copenhagen General Population Study(CGPS). Second, we investigated the causal genetic effect of CRP, a marker of low-grade inflammation, on risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death using one-sample Mendelian randomization in the Copenhagen studies and two-sample Mendelian randomization using the Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology, Inflammation Working Group (CHARGE CIWG) [
17
] as the exposure cohort(CRP), and the FinnGen study [
18
] and the MEGASTROKE consortium [
19
] as outcome cohorts (stroke and all-cause death). In the two-sample analyses, we also investigated the causal genetic effect of CRP on risk of stroke subtypes. The study design is shown in Fig.

Methods
Study population
The Copenhagen studies
We included 113,491 individuals for observational and one-sample Mendelian randomization analyses from the Copenhagen City Heart Study (CCHS) and the Copenhagen General Population Study (CGPS), combined called the Copenhagen studies. The Copenhagen studies are prospective population-based studies conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki and with written informed consent obtained from all participants. Danish ethics committees have approved both studies (approval number: H-KF-01–144/01, KF-100.2039/91, KF-01–144/01). The CCHS was initiated in 1976 to 1978 with follow-up examinations in 1981 to 1983, 1991 to 1994 and 2001 to 2003. The CGPS was initiated in 2003 with enrolment until 2015 and with ongoing follow-up examinations [
14
]. The participants were selected at random from the national Danish Civil Registration System. All individuals were white and of Danish descent and none were included in more than one of the studies [
20
]. The participants were aged from 20 to 100 + years. The follow-up time was up to 40 years due to the link to national Danish health registries. All participants completed a questionnaire which was reviewed at the day of attendance by an investigator. On this day they also had a physical examination and a blood sample drawn. In total, 3,519 participants (3.0% of the cohort) were excluded from the present study due to missing values of plasma CRP or genotype.
CHARGE CIWG, UK Biobank, FinnGen, and MEGASTROKE consortia
We included publicly available summary data for selected genotypes for two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses. From the Cohorts for Heart and Aging Research in Genomic Epidemiology, Inflammation Working Group (CHARGE CIWG) [
17
], we used the beta-coefficients for the association between genotypes and CRP concentrations in 204,402 individuals of European ancestry. From the UK Biobank (UKBB) we used the beta-coefficients for the association between genotypes and CRP concentrations in 575,531 individuals of European ancestry, where 427,367 individuals were only in the UKBB, 148,164 individuals were also included in the CHARGE CIW G [
21
].
From the FinnGen study, we used the beta-coefficients for the association between genotypes and stroke outcomes in 342,499 individuals of Finnish ancestry [
18
]. From the MEGASTROKE consortium, we used beta-coefficients for stroke outcomes in 446,696 individuals of European ancestry [
19
]. Some of the cohorts included in the CHARGE CIWG was also included in MEGASTROKE. The overlap between the two consortia is shown in Supplementary Table 1.
Consent to participate
All participants have given written consent to participate in the studies.
Genotypes
As genetic instrument for CRP, we selected three genetic variants in the
CRP
gene known to be associated with CRP concentrations in genome wide association studies and previously used in Mendelian randomization studies of both CRP as well as other relevant biomarkers: rs1205, rs3093077, and rs1130864 [
14
,
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
]. The specific variants were selected to minimize risk of pleiotropy as CRP is part of a cascade. Detailed information regarding genotypes is shown in Supplementary Table 2. The Copenhagen studies were not part of these original GWASes. An ABI PRISM 7900HT Sequence Detection System (Applied Biosystems Inc. Foster City CA) and TaqMan-based assays were used for genotyping in the Copenhagen studies. The three variants were not in linkage disequilibrium with the highest R
2
of 0.24. The genetic variants were combined into a weighted allele score weighted by effect size on plasma CRP concentration, and allele frequency in the Copenhagen studies. The allele score was divided into quintile categories. Genotypes were available in 108,636 individuals (Supplementary Table 3).
C-reactive protein and covariates
Plasma CRP concentrations were measured using high sensitivity turbidimetry (Dako) or nephelometry (Dade Behring) assays according to manufacturer’s protocols. Plasma concentrations of triglycerides and high- and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol were measured using standard hospital assays. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by measured weight in kilograms divided by measured height in meters squared. Information on alcohol consumption in units per week, smoking, physical activity, and use of lipid-lowering medication was self-reported. Smoked pack-years were calculated as accumulated time of tobacco smoking and consumed tobacco per day in current and former smokers. Physical activity was categorized as low, medium, and high activity during work and leisure time. Blood pressure was measured with automated equipment at the study site.
Ischemic stroke, ischemic stroke subtypes, and death
In the Copenhagen studies, diagnoses of ischemic stroke were as defined by the World Health Organisation (WHO) International Classification of Disease(ICD)-10 and 8 codes (Supplementary Table 4) and all-cause death were collected from the national Danish Civil Registration System, the national Danish Patient Registry and the national Danish registry of causes of death, from January 1st, 1977, through December 13th, 2018. ICD9 was not used as this revision of ICD was never introduced in Denmark, and ICD10 directly replaced ICD8 as of January 1st, 1994. All diagnoses in the national Danish Patient Registry are recorded by physicians in compliance with national law. As all individuals living in Denmark have a Danish Civil Registration System number linked to all national Danish health registries, we did not lose a single person to follow-up; those who emigrated (n = 608) had their follow-up truncated at the day of emigration. Ischemic stroke is categorized into subtypes by the TOAST (Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) classification [
26
]: (1) large-artery atherosclerosis, (2) cardioembolism, (3) small-vessel occlusion, (4) stroke of other determined etiology, and (5) stroke of undetermined etiology. In the present study, any ischemic stroke and ischemic stroke subtypes 1–3 were investigated. The CCHS and CGPS included data on any ischemic stroke and all-cause death. The FinnGen study included data on ischemic stroke, cardioembolic stroke, and all-cause death. The MEGASTROKE consortium included data on any ischemic stroke and ischemic stroke subtypes 1–3 (Supplementary Table 4).
Statistical analyses
We used STATA SE 17.0 for Windows (StataCorp) and investigated deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium using chi-square tests. Trends for significance across ordered categories of CRP were tested using the non-parametric Cuzick’s extension of a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. CRP concentrations were log-transformed before incorporation into regression models, as they were non-normally distributed. Because plasma CRP in the general population is highly right-skewed, with a long tail toward higher values, this transformation was necessary to approximate a normal distribution and improve model fit.
The observational associations between CRP and risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death were examined with CRP on a continuous scale using restricted cubic splines with three knots, selected using Akaike’s information criterion [
27
] to balance best fit and overfitting, and incorporated in a Cox proportional hazards model. The population median CRP concentration of 1.4 mg/L was used as the reference in continuous analyses. To test the observational association between CRP concentrations in quintiles and risk of stroke we used a Cox proportional hazards model with age as timescale (age adjusted). Both models were adjusted for known risk factors for stroke: sex, body mass index (BMI), physical activity, alcohol and tobacco consumption, hypertension, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, and use of lipid-lowering drugs.
The cumulative incidence of ischemic stroke and all-cause death as a function of age was plotted using the Kaplan–Meier estimator approach. The population was divided in two groups stratified by CRP concentration (CRP < or ≥ 2 mg/L). The 2 mg/L cut-off applied in categorical analyses is commonly used in the existing literature of risk prediction in cardiovascular disease [
28
], while the 1.4 mg/L cut-off applied in continuous analyses represents the median CRP value in our study population. This distinction between cut-off values ensured both alignment with established research and relevance to our specific cohort. Differences between groups were tested by log-rank tests.
For the genetic analyses in the Copenhagen studies, we examined the association between the selected genetic variants, the weighted allele score, and CRP concentrations. To estimate a causal effect of plasma CRP on risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death in one-sample Mendelian randomization(MR) analyses we used the publicly available, user-written
ivreg2
and
ivpois
commands [
29
]. As the weighed allele score is largely unconfounded, the genetic analyses were unadjusted. We also performed two-sample MR analyses using inverse-variance weighted regression with publicly available summary-data from the CHARGE CIWG, the UKBB, the FinnGen, and the MEGASTROKE consortia, using the same three genetic variants as in the one-sample MR analyses. Two-sample MR analyses, using CHARGE CIWG and UKBB as exposure cohorts and FinnGen as outcome cohort, were performed on any ischemic stroke, embolic stroke, and all-cause death. Two-sample MR analyses, using CHARGE CIWG and UKBB as exposure cohorts and MEGASTROKE as outcome cohort, were performed on any ischemic stroke, embolic stroke, large artery stroke, and small vessel stroke. To account for direct pleiotropic effects and for up to 50% of information coming from invalid or weak instruments, we performed sensitivity analyses using the publicly available, user-written
mregger
and
mrmedian
commands [
30
,
31
]. Combined causal MR estimates for any ischemic stroke (Copenhagen studies, MEGASTROKE and FinnGen), embolic stroke (MEGASTROKE and FinnGen), and all-cause death (Copenhagen studies and FinnGen) were estimated by meta-analyses using the
metaan
command. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed by I
2
-statistics. A fixed effects model was chosen as between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate (I
2
= 0–16%)(Supplementary Table 5) [
32
]. The outcomes large artery stroke and small vessel stroke had only one outcome-cohort, MEGASTROKE, and was therefore not subject to meta-analysis. The power of our genetic estimates was calculated for each analysis, showing that a sample size of 204,402 individuals and 34,217 events had an estimated power of 90.69% to detect an causal odds ratio of 1.10 at a significance level of 0.05, assuming that the genetic variants explain a total of 5% of the variation in CRP based on previous estimates [
33

34
]. Power-calculations for all analyses is shown in Supplementary Table 6.

Discussion
In this prospective cohort study of the general population, we found an observational association between higher C-reactive protein (CRP) concentrations and risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death. Individuals with plasma CRP concentrations above versus below the population median of 1.4 mg/L had a higher risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death. However, one and two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses did not support causality between high CRP concentrations and risk of any ischemic stroke, subtypes of ischemic stroke, or all-cause death.
Mechanistically, CRP is an acute-phase reactant and a downstream marker of systemic inflammation, synthesized in the liver following inflammation, infection, ischemia, or other types of stress in tissues [
37
,
38
]. Low-grade inflammation, characterized by moderately elevated CRP, is associated with several chronic diseases and has also been suggested to be a risk marker of all-cause death [
39
]. Elevated CRP concentrations are highly prevalent in patients with metabolic dysfunction, including those with pre-diabetes and type 2 diabetes, often serving as a key indicator of heightened cardiometabolic risk in this population [
40
]. CRP is increasingly used in risk stratification of cardiovascular disease and has been associated with the development and progression of atherosclerosis, one of the major causes of ischemic stroke [
37
,
41
,
42
,
43
]. Systemic low-grade inflammation often include inflammation in the arterial wall, which may destabilize the atherosclerotic plaques, potentially leading to plaque rupture and ischemic stroke [
27
].
Like the current study, several previous observational studies have found an association between high CRP and high risk of stroke in the general population [
44
,
45
,
46
]. Also, it has been suggested that CRP may be used as a predictive marker of risk of recurrent ischemic stroke and a predictor of survival after ischemic stroke [
6
,
47
,
48
]. However, CRP did not improve risk prediction of ischemic stroke when added to other conventional cardiovascular risk factors such as sex, age, blood pressure, smoking, and previous cardiovascular disease in a primary prevention setting [
9
,
49
].
The observational associations between elevated CRP and risk of all-cause death reported in this study align with findings from several other studies. The Northern Manhattan study [
15
] showed a higher risk of death in individuals with CRP > 3 mg/L (HR = 1.54, 95%CI:1.22, 1.95) compared to those with CRP < 1 mg/L. Similarly, a large hospital-based cohort study(n = 274,515) found that CRP was a predictor of mortality from both cancer and cardiovascular causes [
50
]. However, this study used low-sensitivity CRP measurements, which were unable to detect CRP concentrations < 5 mg/L, making direct comparisons with the present study challenging. The Whitehall study (n = 5,360) further supported an association between hsCRP and both vascular and non-vascular mortality. In that study, male participants with no prior disease had a higher risk of death (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.33, 1.99) per 2 standard deviations increase in CRP [
51
]. Finally, studies investigating the relationship between elevated CRP and risk of cardiovascular mortality, as well as all-cause mortality, have reported strong observational associations but found no evidence of genetic causality [
24
,
52
]. The apparent discrepancy between the strong observational hazard ratios and our null findings causal genetic examinations is instructive, reinforcing that these observational associations are likely driven by unmeasured confounding or reverse causation, which Mendelian randomization is specifically designed to circumvent. Although the subtype analyses were based on smaller case numbers than the overall ischemic stroke analysis, the calculated power (Supplementary Table 6) was sufficient to detect clinically meaningful effect sizes if there had been any effect. Therefore, the absence of associations across stroke subtypes is unlikely to be explained by limited statistical power.
The current study does not support a causal relationship between elevated CRP and high risk of ischemic stroke or all-cause death, suggesting that drugs reducing CRP concentrations per se may not reduce the risk of ischemic stroke or death.
The CANTOS trial, a randomized controlled trial investigating the effect of canakinumab in individuals with elevated CRP and a history of cardiovascular disease, found a reduction in both CRP concentrations and overall cardiovascular event risk. However, there was no effect on stroke risk or all-cause death in individuals treated with canakinumab compared to placebo [
10
]. This lack of effect may be attributable to insufficient statistical power to detect differences in these secondary endpoints. In contrast, in the COLCOT and LoDoCo2 trials, treatment with colchicine reduced risk of cardiovascular events and ischemic stroke in individuals with a history of cardiovascular disease compared to placebo. However, no effect was seen on risk of cardiovascular or all-cause mortality [
11
,
53
,
54
]. Notably, CRP concentrations was not routinely measured in the LoDoCo2 trial and were assessed only in a subgroup of patients in the COLCOT trial. In this subgroup, no change in CRP concentrations was observed after 6 months of colchicine treatment compared to placebo [
53
]. Canakinumab is a monoclonal antibody specifically targeting interleukine(IL)1β, upstream of IL-6 and CRP [
10
,
55
]. Colchicine inhibits the NLRP3 inflammasome, indirectly reducing IL1β activation, but also has broader anti-inflammatory effects, including alteration of leukocyte responsiveness [
56
]. A recent trial comparing colchicine with placebo following acute myocardial infarction found no reduction in the incidence of cardiovascular mortality, recurrent myocardial infarction, stroke, or unplanned ischemia-driven coronary revascularization [
57
].
A recent study [
58
] reported a potential causal association between CRP and large-artery atherosclerotic stroke, which contrasts with our null findings for overall ischemic stroke and its subtypes. However, this study relied on a large genome-wide set of genetic variants, which increases statistical power but also the risk of horizontal pleiotropy [
59
]. In contrast, we used only three genetic variants with known functions in the
CRP
gene, reducing risk of pleiotropy and providing a more specific assessment of CRP’s effects. While this approach may modestly reduce power for subtype analyses, it offers a more accurate estimate of the causal influence of the CRP gene itself. This is important as CRP is a downstream marker of many processes. From a clinical perspective in the setting of cardiometabolic disease, our results confirm that while CRP may not be the direct mechanism of vascular damage, it remains an essential and easily measurable plasma marker for identifying individuals, particularly those with concurrent metabolic risk factors, who are at high and immediate risk of fatal and non-fatal cardiovascular events.
As stated above, the findings of the current study do not support a causal relationship between high plasma CRP concentrations and risk of ischemic stroke, suggesting that lowering CRP per se with anti-inflammatory therapies may not have a direct effect on ischemic stroke risk. The results were consistently negative across stroke subtypes and we found no causal effect of CRP concentrations on risk of embolic stroke, large artery stroke, and small vessel stroke. However, further studies are needed to elucidate whether targeting low-grade inflammation by other pathways might have a beneficial effect. A Mendelian randomization study investigating the causal effect of interleukin-6(IL-6) on the risk of stroke and stroke subtypes found that genetic variation in the
IL6R
gene
,
selected to associate with lower CRP, was also associated with lower risk of ischemic stroke. These findings suggest that specific components of the inflammatory pathways upstream of CRP may play a causal role in stroke pathogenesis [
52
]. Specifically for stroke subtypes, the study found a lower risk for large artery stroke and small vessel stroke, but no effect on risk of embolic stroke. Whether pharmacological IL-6-inhibition influences risk of ischemic stroke and stroke subtypes remains to be explored [
60
].
The current study did not support causality between CRP and all-cause mortality, in accordance with a previous study [
52
]. While the observational association is strong, and consistent with the findings in other studies [
50
,
51
], the genetic results indicate that lowering CRP per se would not have a direct effect on all-cause mortality.
Strengths and limitations
The strengths of the present study include the comprehensive observational analyses and the one- and two-sample MR design. Examining ischemic stroke and all-cause mortality in both the same cohort and with summary data from three consortia, the risk of confounding and pleiotropy is reduced, strengthening the results.
Potential limitations to our study may include population stratification bias, weak instrument bias, and pleiotropic effects [
61
,
62
]. The observational cohorts used, the CCHS and the CGPS, were both ethnically homogenous, as invited individuals were white of Danish descent. The four consortia, CHARGE CIWG, UKBB, FinnGen, and MEGASTROKE, included cohorts with individuals of European descent, hereby reducing the risk of population stratification bias; however, the results may therefore not necessarily be applicable to other ethnicities. Between the consortia, there was an up to 8.8% overlap among cases used for the summary statistics, which might bias the results toward the observational and one-sample MR estimates. However, the risk of a null finding being biased because of overlapping data in two-sample analyses is generally low.
Another notable limitation of this study is that the MR analyses were based on a limited number of CRP-associated variants, which may reduce power. However, the use of a broader genome-wide instrument, while more powerful, has been shown to introduce greater heterogeneity and pleiotropy [
59
], which could bias causal estimates in the direction of a significant finding. One of the genetic variants used in the analysis(rs1205) has been reported to be associate with heart rate variability and COVID-19 severity [
63
] which might indicate potential pleiotropic effects. Although these associations are unlikely to fully account for the null findings, residual pleiotropy cannot be excluded and should be considered a limitation of the present study. The F statistics indicated sufficient instrument strength of the genetic instrument in one-sample MR analyses. Two-sample MR analyses and MR Egger regression showed no indication of pleiotropic effects.
In conclusion, in this observational, and one- and two-sample Mendelian randomization study, we found that CRP concentrations above the population median of 1.4 mg/L was associated with increased risk of ischemic stroke and all-cause death. However, we found no genetic association between CRP concentrations and ischemic stroke or all-cause death, suggesting that these associations are not causal. The lack of a genetic association for CRP does not exclude a causal role of upstream inflammatory mediators such as IL-6, which should be examined in future research.
