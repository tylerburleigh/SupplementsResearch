# src-092: Inflammation -- Nature's Way to Efficiently Respond to All Types of Challenges

**Authors:** Bennett JM, Reeves G, Billman GE, Sturmberg JP
**Year:** 2018 | **Venue:** Frontiers in Medicine | **Citations:** 414
**DOI:** 10.3389/fmed.2018.00316 | **Quality:** ok

## Core Findings

This review argues that inflammation is the body's universal, adaptive response mechanism to all forms of challenge -- infectious, traumatic, metabolic, and psychological -- and that chronic diseases arise not from inflammation per se but from its dysregulation. The authors propose a systems-level framework in which chronic low-grade inflammation (not acute inflammation) drives disease progression across multiple organ systems simultaneously, challenging the prevailing siloed view of diseases as discrete entities. They advocate for anti-inflammatory lifestyle interventions (diet, exercise, stress management) rather than blanket pharmacological suppression of inflammatory markers.

## Arguments About Protective Functions of Inflammation

1. **Universal adaptive response:** Regardless of cause (trauma, infection, autoimmune, stress), the body mounts the same fundamental cascade: increased blood supply, white cell recruitment, phagocytic activity, and down-regulation leading to healing. This is presented as evidence of system design efficiency -- one mechanism handles all threats (lines 55-66).

2. **IL-6 dual role:** The authors explicitly note that IL-6, commonly labeled pro-inflammatory, can also have anti-inflammatory effects and may signal natural tissue repair processes. They emphasize that context is critical when interpreting inflammatory markers (lines 350-356).

3. **Sickness behavior as adaptive:** Pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, TNF-alpha, IFN-gamma, IL-6) trigger sickness behavior (fever, fatigue, social withdrawal, poor concentration), which prompts reduced activity and rest to better cope with infection (lines 376-381).

4. **Autoimmune potential vs. disease:** Autoantibodies and autoimmune cells can exist in healthy individuals without disease; they may play adaptive roles in "mopping up" tissue destruction products, and autoimmune T cells may assist with immune regulation (lines 799-808).

5. **Balanced pro/anti-inflammatory regulation:** The finely tuned balance between pro- and anti-inflammatory activities is presented as "one of nature's principles at work -- system design and efficiency" (lines 659-663).

## Why Suppressing Inflammation May Have Costs

1. **Paradoxical effects of anti-inflammatory drugs:** NSAIDs and corticosteroids reduced peri-operative atrial fibrillation risk but increased AF incidence in other populations (lines 989-997). This demonstrates context-dependent effects that challenge blanket suppression.

2. **Immunosuppression trade-offs:** Immunosuppressive agents (corticosteroids, DMARDs, biologics) carry significant toxicities. Even targeted anticytokine therapies (biologics) have infection as a "recognized and common problem" (lines 1844-1865). The authors call immunosuppression the "bluntest" approach.

3. **Glucocorticoid insensitivity from chronic suppression:** Chronic elevation of cortisol (whether from stress or exogenous sources) leads to downregulation of glucocorticoid receptor expression, paradoxically resulting in unregulated immune cell activity and excessive pro-inflammatory cytokine production (lines 500-505).

4. **Loss of parasympathetic anti-inflammatory pathway:** Chronic stress shifts autonomic balance toward sympathetic dominance, withdrawing the cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway (vagal-mediated), which is the body's natural brake on inflammation (lines 548-550, 1322-1335).

5. **Infection risk from immune modulation:** The "Hygiene hypothesis" is discussed -- reduced microbe exposure may paradoxically increase allergic and autoimmune conditions, suggesting that some inflammatory stimulation is necessary for proper immune calibration (lines 1766-1780).

## Implications for CRP-Lowering Interventions

1. **CRP as imperfect proxy:** CRP is presented as a clinical marker of the acute-phase response that outperforms ESR but has important limitations: it can be elevated in obesity, renal dysfunction, CVD, and many chronic diseases without acute infection, while it may be absent during hepatic failure and SLE flares (lines 1448-1462).

2. **CRP cut-offs provided:** The paper includes a clinical reference table: <1 mg/L = low CVD risk, 1-3 mg/L = moderate, 3-10 mg/L = high, >10 mg/L = very high or acute illness (lines 1487-1500).

3. **Inflammaging and CRP:** People over 50 show 2-4-fold elevations in CRP (along with IL-6, IL-18, TNF-alpha) compared to younger individuals, driven by DAMP accumulation rather than any acute process. This suggests age-related CRP elevation may be partly physiological rather than purely pathological (lines 1277-1280).

4. **Context-dependent interpretation:** The paper explicitly argues that context is critical when examining inflammation levels. The same CRP value may indicate active tissue repair in one setting and pathological inflammation in another. This directly challenges the logic of targeting CRP reduction as a therapeutic goal without addressing the underlying drivers.

5. **CRP and mortality data:** Individuals with IL-6 levels at or above 3.19 pg/mL were 2x more likely to die than those with levels below 1.46 pg/mL. CRP was associated with sudden cardiac death risk in a 17-year follow-up, but this association did not persist after adjustment for baseline risk factors (unlike IL-6) (lines 964-972, 1470-1472).

## Methodology

- Narrative review / hypothesis and theory article (not a systematic review or meta-analysis)
- Organized in three parts: (1) immune system overview and neuroendocrine-immune feedback loops, (2) inflammatory mechanisms in common diseases (atopy, autoimmunity, CAD, arrhythmias, obesity, diabetes, depression, osteoarthritis, aging), (3) clinical practice implications
- Heavily referenced (250+ citations)
- Includes original schematic figures illustrating inflammatory pathways

## Limitations

- Not a systematic review; selection of diseases and evidence may reflect author bias
- No original data; synthesizes existing literature through a specific theoretical lens (complex adaptive systems)
- Some claims about inflammation as the universal disease mechanism are stated more strongly than the evidence warrants
- Does not directly address clinical trial evidence for or against CRP-lowering interventions
- The paper does not discuss canakinumab or other specific CRP/IL-1beta-lowering trials (CANTOS, etc.)

## Relevance to Research Question

**Directly relevant** to questions about whether CRP is causal, protective, or epiphenomenal. This paper provides strong conceptual arguments that inflammation (and by extension inflammatory markers like CRP) serves essential adaptive functions, and that context determines whether elevated markers indicate pathology or normal repair processes. The inflammaging section and the CRP limitations discussion are particularly relevant to whether lowering CRP per se is beneficial. However, the paper does not provide direct evidence from CRP-lowering intervention trials.
