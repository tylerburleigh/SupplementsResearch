# src-100: Ergothioneine as a Natural Antioxidant Against Oxidative Stress-Related Diseases

**Authors:** Fu, T-T. and Shen, L. (2022)
**Venue:** Frontiers in Pharmacology (Mini Review)
**Citations:** 71
**DOI:** 10.3389/fphar.2022.850813
**Quality:** ok -- full text available from PDF extraction

## Core Findings

This mini-review comprehensively surveys EGT's antioxidant properties, its tissue distribution via the OCTN1 transporter, and its protective roles across multiple oxidative stress-related disease domains (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, liver disease, neurodegenerative diseases, inflammatory conditions). The review argues EGT is a superior antioxidant compared to GSH and ascorbic acid due to its stability, low metabolism, high tissue accumulation, and lack of toxicity, supporting its potential as a therapeutic and preventive agent.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Safety and Dosing
- EFSA recommends synthetic EGT as safe food supplement at 30 mg/day (adults), 20 mg/day (children) (Turck et al., 2016, 2017)
- No genotoxicity at 5,000 ug/ml in Chinese hamster lung cells
- No adverse effects in rats at maximum oral dose of 1,600 mg/kg/day
- No reproductive/developmental toxicity in 90-day Sprague-Dawley rat studies at 0.9% concentration
- Combined intake estimates: 1.7 mg/kg bw/day (adults), 3.7 mg/kg bw/day (children)

### Bioavailability and Pharmacokinetics
- EGT is transported specifically by OCTN1 (SLC22A4, also called ETT) on cell membranes
- OCTN1 knockout/overexpression studies demonstrate complete dependence on this transporter
- Tissue concentrations reach ~0.1-1 mM in organs susceptible to oxidative stress (liver, erythrocytes, brain, kidney, spleen, lung, eye, intestines, semen, testis, bone marrow)
- Cheah et al. (2017): Oral EGT in 45 healthy male volunteers showed strong absorption and retention, with significant increase in plasma/whole blood EGT and decreases in oxidative damage and inflammatory biomarkers

### Cardiovascular Disease
- Smith et al. (2020): Long-term follow-up of 3,236 participants; among 112 plasma metabolites, EGT showed the most significant positive association with healthy food patterns; high EGT levels associated with reduced CVD mortality and lower risk of cardiometabolic disease

### Diabetes
- Calvo et al. (2016): 37 early diabetes patients eating white button mushrooms (EGT 3.2 mg/100 g) daily for 16 weeks showed reduced systemic oxidative stress and inflammatory markers
- EGT improves endothelial cell senescence and vascular relaxation damage from hyperglycemia via upregulation of sirtuin 1/6, downregulation of p66Shc and NF-kB, and GSH modulation

### Neurodegenerative Diseases
- EGT crosses the blood-brain barrier via OCTN1
- Cheah et al. (2016a): Elderly patients with mild cognitive impairment show significantly reduced plasma EGT; blood EGT decreases with age in middle-aged and elderly
- Feng et al. (2019): Community-based cross-sectional study in Singapore (663 volunteers, age 60+); mushroom consumption >2 times/week associated with reduced odds of mild cognitive impairment
- EGT promotes neuronal cell proliferation/differentiation and relieves depressive symptoms in mice at 120 mg EGT/100 g diet (Nakamichi et al., 2016)
- Multiple neuroprotective mechanisms: inhibition of A-beta accumulation, maintenance of GSH/GSSG ratios, restoration of acetylcholinesterase activity, prevention of peroxynitrite formation

### Advantages Over Other Antioxidants
- Exists primarily in thione form under physiological conditions -- ideal thermal and pH stability
- Does not auto-oxidize; does not promote Fenton reaction
- Forms redox-inactive metal complexes (chelates Fe, Cu, Zn, Ni, Co)
- Low metabolism, high tissue accumulation compared to rapid metabolism of GSH and ascorbic acid

## Methodology

This is a narrative mini-review (not a systematic review or meta-analysis). The authors surveyed the literature on EGT's biological sources, in vivo distribution, antioxidant mechanisms, and relationships with oxidative stress-related diseases. They included a summary table (Table 1) of human survey studies on EGT/mushroom consumption and health outcomes. The review draws on cell culture, animal model, and human observational/clinical evidence.

## Limitations

1. **Narrative review without systematic methodology** -- no PRISMA protocol, no formal quality assessment of included studies
2. **Few randomized controlled human trials** -- most human evidence comes from observational/cross-sectional studies or small pilot studies
3. **Mushroom confounders** -- many studies use whole mushrooms rather than isolated EGT, making it difficult to attribute effects to EGT alone; mushrooms contain other bioactive compounds with antioxidant/anti-inflammatory activity
4. **Dose-response gap** -- the authors explicitly note there are "no substantial research on the correlation between the therapeutic dose of EGT and diseases"
5. **Potential adverse effects in specific genotypes** -- CD patients with OCTN1 L503F variant allele showed adverse effects from mushroom intake (Petermann et al., 2009), suggesting dose and genotype matter
6. **Limited gut microbiome data** -- the influence of EGT on gut microbiota, and vice versa, remains largely unstudied
7. **Animal model bias** -- many protective mechanisms are demonstrated in animal/cell models with limited human translational evidence

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Supplementation protocols):** Directly relevant. Provides EFSA-recommended dosing (30 mg/day adults), human pharmacokinetic data from Cheah et al. (2017), and extensive preclinical safety data. Notes the absence of therapeutic dose-response studies.
- **Q2 (Evidence for health benefits):** Highly relevant. Summarizes evidence across diabetes, cardiovascular, liver, neurodegenerative, and inflammatory diseases. Human evidence includes the Smith et al. (2020) 3,236-participant cardiovascular study, the Feng et al. (2019) cognitive study (n=663), and the Calvo et al. (2016) diabetes study (n=37).
- **Q3 (Safety/side effects):** Directly relevant. Reviews extensive safety data including EFSA assessments, genotoxicity studies, and high-dose rat studies showing no adverse effects up to 1,600 mg/kg/day. Notes the Petermann et al. finding of potential adverse effects in CD patients with specific OCTN1 variants.
- **Q4 (Mechanisms of action):** Highly relevant. Reviews antioxidant mechanisms (free radical scavenging, metal chelation, interaction with GSH/antioxidant pathways, mitochondrial protection), tissue-specific mechanisms, and disease-specific pathways.
- **Q5 (Age-related decline):** Relevant. Documents age-related decline in blood EGT levels, association with cognitive impairment, and the hypothesis that EGT deficiency increases risk of aging-related oxidative stress diseases.

## Claims to Verify

- The claim that mushrooms account for "about 95% of dietary intake" of EGT (line 86-87) appears to come from Ito et al. (2011) and Weigand-Heller et al. (2012) but the exact percentage should be cross-checked against the primary sources.
- The porcini EGT content of "7.27 mg/g dw" (line 100-102) is attributed to Kalaras et al. (2017); this is a notably high value and worth verifying against the original.
- The sample size of 45 for Cheah et al. (2017) should be confirmed from the original paper; the review describes them as "healthy young male volunteers."
