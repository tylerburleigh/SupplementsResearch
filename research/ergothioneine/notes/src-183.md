# src-183: Association of mushroom consumption with all-cause and cause-specific mortality (NHANES III)

**Authors:** Ba DM, Gao X, Muscat J, Al-Shaar L, Chinchilli V, Zhang X, Ssentongo P, Beelman RB, Richie JP Jr.
**Journal:** Nutrition Journal (2021) 20:38
**DOI:** 10.1186/s12937-021-00691-8
**Design:** Prospective cohort study (NHANES III, 1988-1994, follow-up through 2015)

---

## Core Findings

Mushroom consumption was associated with a 16% lower risk of all-cause mortality in a nationally representative sample of 15,546 US adults followed for a mean of 19.5 years (adjusted HR = 0.84; 95% CI: 0.73-0.98), independent of demographics, lifestyle factors, diet quality, and total energy intake. A significant dose-response relationship was observed (P-trend = 0.03). Substituting one daily serving of mushrooms for red/processed meat was associated with 35% lower all-cause mortality (HR = 0.65; 95% CI: 0.50-0.84). No significant cause-specific mortality associations were found, likely due to limited statistical power.

## Methodology

- **Data source:** NHANES III (1988-1994), linked to National Death Index mortality data through December 31, 2015
- **Sample:** 15,546 adults aged 18+ (from 19,598 eligible; excluded implausible energy intake and missing dietary data)
- **Exposure assessment:** Single 24-h dietary recall; mushrooms identified via USDA food codes for recipe foods and FCID commodity codes for mixed dishes
- **Dose categories:** None (0 g/d, n=15,002), lowest (median 10 g/d, n=346), middle (median 35 g/d, n=104), high (median 72 g/d, n=94)
- **Outcome:** All-cause and cause-specific mortality (CVD, cancer, Alzheimer's, diabetes, other)
- **Analysis:** Cox proportional hazards regression with survey weights, adjusting sequentially for demographics (model 1), socioeconomic factors (model 2), and lifestyle/dietary factors including HEI-2000 (model 3)
- **Substitution analysis:** Mushroom replacing red/processed meat using continuous variables in the same model
- **Sensitivity analyses:** Propensity score adjustment, exclusion of early deaths (first 2 years), exclusion of participants with major chronic diseases (n=4,826), interaction tests

## Key Evidence

| Analysis | Adjusted HR | 95% CI | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mushroom vs. no mushroom (model 1) | 0.79 | 0.67-0.92 | Significant |
| Mushroom vs. no mushroom (model 3, fully adjusted) | 0.84 | 0.73-0.98 | Significant |
| Dose-response trend | -- | -- | P = 0.03 |
| Substitution: mushroom for red/processed meat | 0.65 | 0.50-0.84 | Significant |
| Propensity score adjusted | 0.86 | 0.74-0.99 | Significant |
| Excluding chronic disease participants | 0.83 | 0.65-1.06 | Not significant |
| CVD mortality | 0.82 | 0.56-1.21 | Not significant |

| Alzheimer's mortality | 0.90 | 0.31-2.60 | Not significant |

## Ergothioneine Connection

The paper discusses ergothioneine as a potential biological mechanism for the observed mortality benefit. Key points:
- Ergothioneine is described as a proposed "longevity vitamin" with antioxidant, cytoprotective, and anti-aging functions (citing Ames 2018)
- A plasma metabolomics study (Smith et al., Heart 2020) identified ergothioneine as the major metabolite associated with reduced cardiometabolic disease and mortality risk
- Ergothioneine levels differ by mushroom type: shiitake, oyster, and maitake are higher in ergothioneine than Agaricus bisporus (white button, crimini, portabella)
- US has the lowest estimated average ergothioneine consumption compared to Italy, Ireland, France, and Finland
- The NHANES III database does not contain ergothioneine intake data, so it could not be included as a variable

## Limitations

1. **Single 24-h recall** at baseline only -- no long-term pattern data; likely underestimates true association due to nondifferential measurement error
2. **No mushroom type data** -- ergothioneine content varies significantly by species
3. **No ergothioneine or glutathione data** in NHANES III nutrient database
4. **Observational design** -- cannot establish causation
5. **Likely exposure misclassification** -- mushrooms in mixed foods may be misclassified as vegetables
6. **Small number of mushroom consumers** (n=544 out of 15,546), limiting statistical power for dose-response and subgroup analyses
7. **Loss of significance** when excluding participants with major chronic diseases suggests possible reverse causation or confounding by indication
8. **Residual confounding** possible despite extensive covariate adjustment

## Claims to Verify

- The dose-response analysis had only 94 participants in the high intake group and 104 in the middle group, which is quite small for detecting meaningful differences. The P-trend of 0.03 should be interpreted cautiously given these sample sizes.
- The non-significant finding after excluding chronic disease participants (HR=0.83, 95% CI: 0.65-1.06) raises questions about whether the main finding is confounded by baseline health status. The point estimate barely changed but the CI widened substantially due to sample size reduction.

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Ergothioneine and longevity/mortality (Q5):** Directly relevant as one of the few large-scale prospective studies linking mushroom consumption (the primary dietary source of ergothioneine) to lower all-cause mortality. The dose-response relationship strengthens the evidence, though the study cannot isolate ergothioneine from other mushroom constituents.
- **Mechanisms of ergothioneine action (Q7):** Discusses ergothioneine as a plausible mechanism (antioxidant, "longevity vitamin") but provides no direct evidence -- the study measured mushroom intake, not ergothioneine levels. Cites supporting evidence from plasma metabolomics (Smith et al. 2020) and the Ames (2018) longevity vitamin hypothesis.
- **Supplementation relevance:** The study supports the general premise that ergothioneine-rich foods are associated with longevity but does not directly address supplementation. The low US ergothioneine intake cited suggests a potential supplementation rationale.
