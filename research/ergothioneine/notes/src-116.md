# src-116: Diet-Derived Antioxidants: The Special Case of Ergothioneine

**Authors:** Halliwell B, Tang RMY, Cheah IK
**Year:** 2023
**Venue:** Annual Review of Food Science and Technology, Vol. 14, pp. 323-345
**DOI:** 10.1146/annurev-food-060822-122236
**Citations:** 80
**Quality:** ok

## Core Findings

This is a comprehensive review by one of the leading groups in ergothioneine (ET) research, organized as a series of seven key questions about ET biology. The central thesis is that ET is a diet-derived compound avidly retained via the specific OCTN1 transporter, but its primary in vivo function remains uncertain. While antioxidant properties are well-established in vitro, in vivo evidence for systemic antioxidant effects is weak. The authors propose ET acts as an "adaptive antioxidant," coming into play only under conditions of tissue stress or injury. Multiple epidemiological correlations link low ET levels to age-related diseases, but causation is not established and no deficiency disease has been identified.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Transport and Pharmacokinetics
- OCTN1 (encoded by SLC22A4) is the major or sole ET transporter, confirmed by CRISPR-Cas9 knockout in mice eliminating ET and all metabolites from all tissues even at 70 mg/kg supplementation doses (lines 273-280).
- In humans, 25 mg/day pure ET for 7 days raised whole-blood ET from ~100 uM to ~125 uM, plasma from ~1 uM to ~3 uM, with only a few percent excreted in urine (lines 320-408).
- Mushrooms contain the highest ET levels among foods: Boletus edulis at 1,812 ug/g dry weight, with most other foods containing far less (Table 1, lines 60-122).
- Dietary ET intakes estimated at 0.051 (France), 0.255 (Italy), and 0.153 (US) mg per kg body weight per day (lines 204-207).

### Antioxidant Function (Question 6)
- ET scavenges hydroxyl radical, hypochlorous acid, singlet oxygen, and peroxynitrite in vitro, but reacts slowly with H2O2 and superoxide (lines 702-716).
- In young healthy humans, 25 mg/day ET for 7 days showed only non-significant trends toward reduced oxidative damage biomarkers (8-OHdG, allantoin, F2-isoprostanes, protein carbonyls) (lines 728-733).
- The authors argue ET is likely an "adaptive antioxidant" that comes into play only during tissue injury or disease stress, not as a primary defense (lines 734-739, Figure 3).

### Aging and Neurodegeneration (Question 4)
- Low plasma/whole-blood ET correlates with frailty, macular degeneration, CVD, preeclampsia, MCI, dementia, and PD across multiple cohorts (lines 477-547).
- A hypothetical plasma ET threshold of ~810 nM was proposed to distinguish healthy vs. at-risk individuals (Figure 2, lines 533-547).
- Low plasma ET is a predictor of subsequent cognitive decline (Wu et al. 2022); lower brain ET levels found in AD patients (Novotny et al. 2022).
- Mushroom consumption is associated with lower risk of some neurodegenerative diseases, but mushrooms contain many bioactive compounds beyond ET (lines 539-542).
- Ames (2018) proposed ET as a "longevity vitamin," but no deficiency disease has been demonstrated (lines 543-546).
- ET protects against AD and PD in animal models and enhances memory in animal studies (lines 558-567).

### Safety and Regulatory
- ET declared safe as dietary supplement by EFSA (Turck et al. 2016, 2017) and GRAS by US FDA in 2018 (lines 51-54).

### Longevity
- ET was reported to increase lifespan in Drosophila (Pan et al. 2022), but the authors' own Drosophila studies did NOT replicate this finding (lines 781-783).
- ET improved exercise performance in female mice (Fovet et al. 2022) (line 781).

### Comparison with Other Dietary Antioxidants
- Vitamin E (alpha-tocopherol) has demonstrated in vivo antioxidant effects via F2-isoprostane biomarkers (lines 630-642).
- Vitamin C, despite excellent in vitro antioxidant activity, shows little in vivo antioxidant effect in most human biomarker studies; its enzyme cofactor roles are likely more important (lines 643-670).
- Polyphenols/flavonoids are unlikely to exert systemic antioxidant effects in vivo due to rapid metabolism and low tissue levels (lines 671-697).

## Methodology

This is a narrative review structured around seven key questions about ET biology. The authors integrate findings from their own laboratory (including unpublished data from OCTN1 knockout mice and Drosophila models) with the broader literature. The review critically evaluates the evidence quality for various proposed functions of ET, rather than simply cataloging positive findings. Key methodological contributions from the authors' lab include:
- CRISPR-Cas9 OCTN1 knockout mouse data (Tables 2-3)
- Human pharmacokinetic study with pure ET supplementation (Cheah et al. 2017)
- Biomarker analysis of oxidative damage in supplemented humans

## Limitations

1. **No human clinical trial data:** The authors repeatedly emphasize that no double-blind placebo-controlled human clinical trials of ET supplementation for disease prevention or treatment have been conducted (lines 566-568).
2. **Correlation vs. causation:** The epidemiological associations between low ET and disease cannot establish causation; low ET could be a consequence rather than cause of disease (lines 548-557).
3. **No deficiency disease identified:** ET cannot be formally classified as a vitamin because no specific deficiency disease has been demonstrated in humans or knockout animals (lines 544-546).
4. **In vivo antioxidant evidence is weak:** The strongest argument for antioxidant function comes from in vitro studies; in vivo biomarker data in healthy humans showed no significant effects (lines 728-733).
5. **Unresolved mechanisms:** The precise mechanisms of ET's protective effects in animal disease models remain unclear (lines 793-794).
6. **Conflicting longevity data:** Drosophila lifespan extension by ET was not replicated by the authors (line 783).
7. **Much reliance on unpublished data:** Several key claims reference "in preparation" manuscripts from the authors' own group (lines 232, 269, 466, 559, 770, 783).

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Supplementation effects):** Provides detailed human pharmacokinetic data (25 mg/day dose, plasma/whole-blood levels, retention) and confirms safety/GRAS status. Critically notes that no clinical trials have been conducted.
- **Q2 (Clinical evidence):** Summarizes epidemiological correlations between low ET and multiple age-related diseases. Proposes ~810 nM plasma threshold. Emphasizes the absence of interventional clinical trial data.
- **Q3 (Dose-response):** Provides tissue distribution data from mice at 70 mg/kg, human dosing data at 25 mg/day. Notes species differences in OCTN1 expression (human liver has lower expression than rodent liver).
- **Q4 (Longevity):** Ames proposed ET as a "longevity vitamin" but no deficiency disease demonstrated. Drosophila lifespan extension was not replicated by these authors. Animal models show neuroprotection.
- **Q5 (Mechanism):** Proposes the "adaptive antioxidant" hypothesis where ET is not a primary defense but activates during tissue injury. Evidence for in vivo systemic antioxidant effects is weak. ET may act via Nrf2 pathway activation, metal ion binding, singlet oxygen scavenging, or other non-antioxidant mechanisms.
- **Q6 (Deficiency risks):** OCTN1 knockout mice showed no obvious phenotypic abnormalities. No deficiency disease identified. Low ET correlates with disease but causation unproven.
- **Q7 (Food sources):** Comprehensive Table 1 of ET content across food categories. Mushrooms are by far the richest source. Notes modern agricultural practices may reduce ET in food supply.

## Claims to Verify

1. **Plasma threshold of ~810 nM:** This figure appears to derive from the authors' own unpublished data compilation (Figure 2). The methodology for deriving this threshold is not described in detail in the text. Worth verifying in the original source data.
2. **Dietary intake estimates (0.051-0.255 mg/kg/day):** These come from Ramirez-Martinez et al. 2016 and should be verified in the primary source.
3. **Human supplementation data (whole-blood ~100 to ~125 uM, plasma ~1 to ~3 uM):** These specific numbers come from Cheah et al. 2017. The baseline whole-blood level of ~100 uM seems high and should be cross-checked with the primary study's Methods section to confirm the measurement approach and participant characteristics.
