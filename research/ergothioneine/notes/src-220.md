# src-220: Is ergothioneine a 'longevity vitamin' limited in the American diet?

**Citation:** Beelman RB, Kalaras MD, Phillips AT, Richie JP. (2020). *Journal of Nutritional Science*, 9, e52. doi:10.1017/jns.2020.44

**Type:** Perspective/review article (not a primary research study)

**Quality:** ok -- full text available, substantive content across 5 pages

---

## Core Findings

This perspective paper argues that ergothioneine (ERGO) should be considered a "longevity vitamin" whose deficiency in the American diet may contribute to chronic diseases of aging. The authors synthesize epidemiological, metabolomic, and food-composition evidence to build the case that ERGO intake is limited in the US compared to European countries and that this gap may have health consequences. The paper is a narrative review/perspective rather than a primary research study; it does not present new experimental data but re-analyzes WHO mortality data against estimated ERGO intakes across countries.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Dietary intake disparities
- Estimated US ERGO intake: **1.1 mg/day**
- Estimated Italian intake: up to **4.6 mg/day** (highest among four European countries studied)
- Lower US intake coincides with higher prevalence of neurological diseases of aging and lower life expectancy (ecological/correlational analysis using WHO 2010 data)

### Mortality and disease associations
- Swedish longitudinal metabolomic study (n > 3,200 adults, 21.4-year follow-up): plasma ERGO was the **most strongly associated** metabolite (of 112 measured) with decreased CVD risk and reduced overall mortality
- ERGO blood levels decline with age; decline is faster in cognitively impaired individuals vs. age-matched controls
- Blood ERGO levels are lower in Parkinson's Disease patients vs. age-matched controls
- ERGO was lower in frail vs. non-frail elderly hospital patients and in those with impaired cognition (n=131 metabolites evaluated)

### Food composition data (mg/kg dry weight)
| Food | ERGO (mg/kg d.w.) |
|------|-------------------|
| White button mushrooms (Agaricus bisporus) | 630 |
| Gray oyster mushrooms (Pleurotus ostreatus) | 1,310 |
| Tempeh | 201 |
| Chicken livers | 47 |
| Kidney beans | 2.1 |
| Oats | 1.8 |

- In the US, ~97% of mushroom production is Agaricus bisporus (lower ERGO than oyster mushrooms)

### Biological transport mechanisms
- SLC22A4 gene encodes a highly specific ERGO transporter (ETT), evolutionarily conserved
- ETT expressed in bone marrow, small intestine, trachea, kidney, cerebellum, lung, monocytes
- ERGO crosses the blood-brain barrier
- ERGO taken up into erythrocytes within one hour of consumption

### Longevity vitamin concept
- Bruce Ames (2018) proposed ERGO as a "longevity vitamin" under Triage Theory
- Paul and Snyder (2010) earlier suggested ERGO merits designation as a vitamin
- ERGO does not meet classic vitamin definition (no overt deficiency disease in short timeframe)

## Methodology

This is a **narrative perspective paper**, not a primary research study. Methods include:
- Synthesis of published literature on ERGO health effects and dietary sources
- Re-analysis of WHO 2010 mortality data plotted against estimated ERGO consumption by country (ecological comparison)
- No new experimental data or systematic review protocol

## Limitations

1. **Ecological fallacy risk:** Cross-country correlations between estimated ERGO intake and mortality/neurological disease rates are ecological in nature -- they cannot establish causation and are subject to confounding.
2. **No original data:** The paper presents no new experimental or observational data; it synthesizes prior studies.
3. **Estimated intake data:** ERGO intake figures come from limited assessments (references 14, 15) and may not reflect current dietary patterns.
4. **Speculative agricultural link:** The hypothesis that conventional agriculture reduces ERGO in crops via mycorrhizal disruption is plausible but not directly tested with data in this paper.
5. **Perspective/bias risk:** As a perspective piece arguing for a specific hypothesis, the authors may selectively cite supportive evidence.

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Supplementation/dietary intake):** Directly relevant -- provides food composition data and estimated intake levels across countries. Documents that mushrooms are by far the primary dietary source.
- **Q2 (Pharmacokinetics/bioavailability):** Relevant -- describes ETT transporter specificity, tissue distribution, rapid erythrocyte uptake (within 1 hour), and blood-brain barrier crossing.
- **Q3 (Health outcomes/mortality):** Highly relevant -- cites the Smith et al. 2020 Swedish metabolomic study showing ERGO as top mortality-associated metabolite; also discusses neurodegeneration and frailty associations.
- **Q4 (Agricultural/food supply):** Relevant -- discusses how conventional tillage may disrupt mycorrhizal fungi that supply ERGO to crops, proposing regenerative agriculture as a solution.
- **Q5 (Optimal dosing):** Indirectly relevant -- provides intake estimates (1.1 mg/day US vs. up to 4.6 mg/day Italy) that could inform dosing discussions, but does not propose specific supplementation doses.

## Claims to Verify

- The Swedish metabolomic study sample size ">3200" and follow-up "21.4 years" -- these come from Smith et al. 2020 (ref 17) and should be verified against the primary source.
- US ERGO intake estimate of "1.1 mg/day" -- comes from Ramirez-Martinez et al. 2016 (ref 14), a dietary intake assessment that may have methodology limitations worth checking.
- Food composition values (especially 630 and 1310 mg/kg d.w. for mushrooms) -- from Kalaras et al. 2017 (ref 29); these are representative values selected by the authors and actual values can vary substantially.
