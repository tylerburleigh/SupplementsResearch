Science is bridging a wide chasm
when it comes to curcumin

T
      his is an exciting time in the world of supplements. What is
      happening with curcumin—where current clinical research is
      uncovering the complex chemistry behind the compound’s
well-known mechanisms of action, previously not elucidated in folk
medicine—is particularly emblematic.
  As with many historically tried-and-true remedies, the gap between the utility of a substance and
our knowledge of how it actually affects the human system is narrowing, as an emphasis on the use of
natural and complementary approaches in Western medicine draws the attention of researchers.
  Curcumin—the nutritional compound located within the rhizome, or rootstalk, of the turmeric
(Curcuma longa) plant—continues to play a leading role in India’s ancient medical system of ayurveda.
And the range of uses in the current version of the practice is fairly astonishing.
  Medical dressings made of turmeric paste still are used to treat common eye infections, and to dress
wounds, treat bites, burns, acne and various skin diseases. Powdered turmeric is taken with boiled milk
to cure cough and related respiratory ailments, and roasted turmeric is an ingredient used as a medicine
against dysentery in children, according to the book “Major medicinal plants of India,” published by the
Central Institute of Medicinal and Aromatic Plants in Lucknow, India.
  In some parts of India, women are given a tonic of fresh turmeric paste with powder of dried ginger
roots and honey in a glass of hot milk to drink twice daily after childbirth. Dressings of turmeric paste are
also applied to the perineum to help heal lacerations in the birth canal (Trees Life J. 2005;1).
  More wide-ranging uses of turmeric in ayurveda include applications in dentistry and digestion. In
another creative if not fully understood mechanism of action, it’s shown to alleviate the hallucinatory
effects of hashish and other psychotropic drugs (Phytother Res. 2004;18:798-804).
  While the issue that unfolds on the following pages won’t fully explain the functionality behind all of
these folk remedies and uses, it does lay out with specificity some of the strides science is making
to uncover the mysteries of curcumin’s history, especially related to gut dyspepsia, joint and muscle
protection, and cognitive function.
  Balanced against the functional as well as market challenges that are further described, this issue
hopefully serves as a small journey of discovery, mixed with some real-world supplement business
application. Enjoy this modest dose of enlightenment!

The benefits and potential
of curcumin
by Rachel Adams




C
           urcumin is a chemical compound isolated from Curcuma longa L.
           (turmeric) plants that is known in the nutrition community for its potential
           anti-inflammatory, anticancer and antimicrobial properties.1
  With a global market share of more than US$58 million in 2020, and a projected compound annual
growth rate (CAGR) of more than 16% from 2020 to 2028—according to a report from Grand View
Research—this compound is a shooting star in the natural products industry that continues to draw
attention from researchers and consumers alike.
  Fueling the growth of curcumin is clinical research, which connects consumers to the health benefits
of this nutritional player. Emerging studies are extending the reach of curcumin, opening doors to new
health avenues that bring opportunities for natural products marketers and manufacturers, as well as the
customers who take stock in their products.

A healthy gut
   Functional dyspepsia is a condition characterized by recurring symptoms of indigestion, such as upper
abdomen discomfort, bloating, belching and nausea. According to N. Kalyanam, Ph.D., president of
research and development (R&D) at Sabinsa Corp., the ailment can also be compounded with H. pylori
infection. “Functional dyspepsia and gut-related problems affect a good fraction of the population and
lower the quality of life, especially due to their persistence or recurrence,” he explained.
   Research this year suggests curcumin may provide support for those dealing with functional dyspepsia.
In a one month, triple-blinded clinical study of 75 subjects with functional dyspepsia, 500 mg daily
standardized turmeric extract with 95% curcuminoids (as Curcumin C3 Complex with Bioperine,
from Sabinsa) as an adjuvant to a standard therapy decreased various indicators of upper abdominal
discomfort in a statistically significant fashion, as compared to the group receiving only standard therapy.2
The positive outcomes were recorded upon completion of the treatment, and during follow-up a month
later. Results included a higher eradication rate of H. pylori in the curcumin group as well.
   Curcumin further supported the gut in a double-blind, placebo-controlled, randomized digestive health
study of 80 subjects with self-reported digestive and mood complaints.3 Participants were administered
either a turmeric-oleoresin extract with 50% curcuminoids (500 mg as Curcugen, from DolCas Biotech) or
placebo for eight weeks. Changes to abdominal pain, reflux, diarrhea, constipation and other symptoms
were monitored using a gastrointestinal symptom rating score (GSRS) inventory. The Curcugen group
scored significantly better in the reduction of abdominal pain, compared to placebo (28% vs. 18%), with
the placebo group getting worse during weeks four through eight.

  What’s more, researchers noted a significant reduction in
anxiety scores in the Curcugen group (52%), versus 16% in

  Curcumin may also support a healthy gut microbiome. In
a double-blind, placebo-controlled study, an inter-university
team in the U.S. examined the effects of placebo, turmeric
powder and C3 Complex+Bioperine on the gut microbial
population, finding curcuminoids played a “prebiotic-like” role
in the promotion of healthy gut microbial population.4

Enduring cognition
  “Since the onset of the global pandemic, consumers have
more frequently sought out natural solutions to cognitive
well-being, mood, emotional and mental health, and many
other subcategories in the brain and cognitive health space,”
explained Kristen Marshall, digital marketing manager,
Verdure Sciences. “In addition to increased interest driving
demand for cognitive and brain solutions, there is increased
demand from an aging population looking for natural
solutions in a proactive approach to their health.”
  Curcumin is answering the call, offering researched benefits
to mood, memory, fatigue and overall cognitive function.
   Supporting curcumin’s role in cognitive preservation and function is a new study showing a C. longa
extract with >90% curcuminoids (as HydroCurc, from Pharmako Biotechnologies) supplemented with
iron increased serum brain-derived neurotropic factor (BDNF), a protein found in the brain and key for
maintenance of normal neuronal function and energy homeostasis.5 BDNF is also suggested to impact
cognitive function, including learning and memory, according to Eric Meppem, commercial director, and
George Kokkinis, technical director, Pharmako Biotechnologies.
  In the six-week study of 155 participants, HydroCurc co-administered with iron demonstrated a 26%
increase in BDNF in the low-dose iron arm (18 mg) and a 35% increase in BDNF in the high-dose iron
arm (65 mg).
  “There is significant impact for both people concerned with cognitive decline, and people looking to
improve cognitive performance (for example students, executives and e-gamers),” the duo said of the results.
   One study found 400 mg (80 mg free curcumin) of solid lipid curcumin particle extract (as Longvida,
from Verdure Sciences) improved working memory, sustained attention, spatial memory and learning, and
mood in healthy, older adults over a 28-day period.6 A later partial replication study sought to extend the
findings of the earlier trial by including a 12-week assessment in addition to a 28-day testing session.7
  “The primary objective of this new clinical trial was to evaluate whether the effects of the four-week trial
could be replicated following 12 weeks supplementation,” Marshall explained.
  Findings showed curcumin (as Longvida at 400 mg/d) improved measures of mood and cognition, such
as decreased fatigue, lowered tension and anxiety, improved working memory, sustained attention, and
improved spatial memory and learning.

Athletic performance and recovery
  For athletes, curcumin has the capacity to speed recovery and soothe sore muscles.
  Kalyanam pointed to a 2021 study showing pre- and post-exercise curcuminoid supplementation
attenuated muscle soreness while retaining muscle power in 22 adults.10 Participants were given 500
mg curcumin or placebo for 10 days: Six days pre-workout, the day of the workout and three days
post-workout. Findings showed greater soreness in the placebo group compared to the curcumin group
48 hours and 72 hours post-exercise and a decrease in vertical jump over time in the placebo group,
which was not seen in the curcumin group.
  A 2021 double-blind, randomized clinical trial measured muscle pain following lower limb resistance
exercise in 24 young, healthy males who were given placebo or curcumin dispersed in water (as
HydroCurc) pre- and post-workout.11 Results showed a significant reduction in delayed onset muscle
soreness (DOMS) pain, positive activation of interleukin (IL)-6 and IL-10 inflammatory markers, reduced
capillary lactate, and reduced thigh circumference at 24 hours and 48 hours in the HydroCurc group,
compared to placebo.
   Another study, presented during a 2020 meeting of the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN),
evaluated the potential anti-inflammatory and pain-relieving role of curcumin (a single 500 mg/d dose of
Curcugen) in participants who completed intense exercise.12 For the double-blind, placebo-controlled,
randomized study, eccentric exercises created muscle damage in both groups, who were monitored
over the next 72 hours. Findings showed a significant reduction in pain-to-pressure threshold (a measure
of soreness) at hours 48 and 72, a reduction of high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP, a marker of
inflammation) at 72 hours, and a return to a normal range of motion at 72 hours in the Curcugen group,
compared to placebo.
  Adding to the growing body of evidence supporting curcumin for athletes is a 2017 randomized,
controlled trial showing curcumin supplementation is effective in recovery following exercise-induced
muscle damage.13 “The authors stated that the supplementation could attenuate muscle damage before it
occurs,” Kalyanam reported.

Youthful joints
  Curcumin’s benefits to joints are well documented. Despite an extensive repertoire of research, new
studies evaluating curcumin’s role in joint health are emerging.
  In a new unpublished, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study, researchers investigated the
efficacy of curcumin (440 mg/d as HydroCurc) for alleviating joint pain and improving quality of life. Results
showed a significant difference in participants’ recorded pain value according to the visual analogue scale
(VAS) over two weeks.
  The impact of this study, according to Meppem and Kokkinis, derives from its demonstration of “the clinical
benefits of HydroCurc at a one-a-day capsule dose, and over a relatively (14 days) short period of time.”
  A randomized, placebo-controlled clinical study explored the effects of 500 mg/d or 1,000 mg/d of a
turmeric extract standardized to patented Turmerosaccharides (as Turmacin, from Natural Remedies) in
90 healthy volunteers feeling knee joint discomfort or pain of at least 40 mm on VAS during 10 minutes
of a brisk walk on treadmill equipment.14 Findings of the 12-week study revealed Turmacin significantly
decreased the final pain scores from the first follow-up of day five, compared to placebo. A maximum
decrease of 1.7 units on the VAS scale from baseline was seen in the Turmacin group on days 56 and
84, a clinically significant difference. The product was also effective in improving joint range of motion and
preserving muscle function during relative physical inactivity at the end of the study, compared to placebo.

Boosted immunity
  Immune health has been at the forefront of the ongoing global health crisis. Curcumin’s possible
benefits to immunity are well-researched, with emerging evidence bolstering the compound’s reputation.
  In a 2021 double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trial of 40 healthy volunteers (60 years and
older) 90 mg of amorphous solid dispersion of curcumin twice daily (as curcuRouge, from Robertet)
for four weeks significantly decreased white blood cell count, neutrophil count and neutrophil ratio,
and significantly increased lymphocyte ratio, resulting in a significant 11.3% decrease in neutrophil/
lymphocyte ratio (NLR), compared to placebo.15
   “Elevated NLR has been reported as a sensitive marker for predicting poor prognosis in chronic
inflammation-based diseases such as stroke, heart failure, colorectal cancer and diabetes, as well as acute
inflammatory diseases such as bacterial and viral infections,” conveyed Laure Egoumenides, product
manager at Robertet. “NLR is also known to increase with age and is considered to be an aging marker.”

  A key player affecting immune function is iron, with research showing a link between iron homeostasis
and the immune system.16
  Curcumin (as HydroCurc) was not only shown to have no negative impact on iron absorption in a 2021
publication, but the study showed the ability of curcumin to significantly elevate whole blood hemoglobin
(Hgb) levels at 180 minutes compared to baseline in the group receiving the high-iron dose of 65 mg
ferrous sulphate with HydroCurc.17
  These findings, according to Meppem and Kokkinis, could encourage iron supplementation for those
who need it. “The pro-oxidant effect of oral ferrous iron (the most cost-effective form) is well-known to
induce inflammation, causing side effects,” the duo said, referencing the study publication. “These lead to
poor compliance, with the end result of no health outcome improvements. This study demonstrates that
those challenges can be overcome.”

Topical relief
  As curcumin research evolves, new areas such as skin health come into view. Atopic dermatitis, also known
as eczema, is a condition characterized by red, itchy patches on the surface of the skin. According to the
National Eczema Association, approximately 16.5 million U.S. adults have atopic dermatitis, with nearly
40% affected by moderate or severe disease. More than half (62.8%) of adults with moderate to severe
disease report itching at least 12 hours per day.
  Oral use of a phytosome curcuminoid complex (as Meriva, from Indena) in subjects experiencing atopic
dermatitis and treated with moisturizers and ceramide creams impacted skin reactions to internal and
external stimulations.18 After 90 days, subjects using Meriva benefited from a more consistent control in
skin discomforts and in the occurrence of skin breaks (from 100% to 63.6%). Individuals using Meriva
also reported a greater reduction in oxidative stress, improvement in skin oxygenation and flux, and
improvement of skin properties, such as oil and water content, hydration and elasticity.

Antidiabetic benefits
  Type 2 diabetes causes the body to develop insulin resistance and, ultimately,
results in elevated blood glucose levels that, if unchecked, can lead to severe
health issues including kidney disease, vision impairment and heart disease.
  Type 2 diabetes affects more than 30 million Americans, according to the CDC,
underlining the need for solutions.

                                      COVID-19 recovery
                                        More than a year into the COVID-19 pandemic, medical
                                      professionals and health regulators are still looking for effective
                                      treatments for the virus that has taken the lives of more than
                                      5 million people around the globe, according to the World
                                      Health Organization (WHO).
                                         Dietary supplements—and curcumin, specifically—could
                                      answer the call for COVID-19 support. One 2021 clinical trial
                                      showed supplementation with 525 mg curcumin with 2.5 mg
                                      piperine twice daily could speed the recovery of patients with
                                      COVID-19.20 Those in the curcumin group, compared to
                                      control, experienced early recovery from symptoms such as
                                      fever, cough, sore throat and breathlessness; less deterioration;
                                      better ability to maintain oxygen saturation; and better clinical
                                      outcomes. Other possible benefits of curcumin/piperine
                                      treatment include reduced duration of hospitalization in patients
                                      with moderate to severe symptoms, and fewer deaths.
                                        Researchers attributed these effects to the anti-inflammatory
                                      and antithrombotic properties of curcumin.

                                      and used by the body. Low bioavailability (attributed to water
                                      insolubility) and rapid metabolism to inactive metabolites are
                                      hurdles that stand in the way of curcumin’s effectiveness.21

  For this reason, some ingredient developers have worked hard to ensure the curcumin ingredients they
supply will provide therapeutic effects to end users. These efforts are evidenced by a wealth of studies
evaluating the bioavailability and efficacy of the ingredients.
  In a single-dose, double-blind, two-way, crossover study of 12 volunteers, 225 mg curcuRouge
(90 mg curcumin) demonstrated a Cmax (peak serum concentration) and AUC0–8h (concentration from
administration to eight hours after) 5.4- and 3.4-fold higher, respectively, than those of another curcumin
product (Theracurmin).22
  “Since native curcumin has extremely low bioavailability in humans and almost doesn’t show any
dose-dependency, a comparative study with products of the market seems like an interesting way to
assess bioavailability improvement,” Egoumenides said of the findings.
  According to Rachela Mohr, manager for technical marketing and scientific management of
bioingredients at WACKER, the bioavailability of curcumin can be boosted with the aid of gamma-
cyclodextrin. She cited a 2018 study showing the curcuminoids of WACKER’s cyclodextrin complex
CAVACURMIN were absorbed around 40 times more efficiently in the body than those of a standard
curcumin extract.23
  Findings of a randomized, double-blind, 2021 study found that the metabolic bioavailability of
tetrahydrocurcumin is also around 40 times higher following ingestion of CAVACURMIN, compared to a
standard curcumin extract.24
  Tetrahydrocurcumin is a curcumin metabolite credited with additional health-promoting properties,
Mohr explained. “Another finding of the long-term study is that, after only four weeks, daily ingestion of
CAVACURMIN (approximately 340 mg curcumin each time) leads to a consistently high blood concentration
of curcumin and tetrahydrocurcumin,” she said. “This effect can be measured after 12 weeks, as well.”
  According to in vitro studies, Indena’s curcumin formulation with lecithin has shown a favorable effect on
the natural biotransformation of curcuminoids by intestinal microbiota with a more efficient production of
curcuminoids metabolites,25 “opening new perspectives in investigation of curcuminoids metabolism and
bioavailability,” Antonella Riva, product research manager, R&D, Indena S.p.A., said. “Research completed
with Meriva continues to demonstrate its benefits as an antioxidant and for low chronic inflammation that
are important to consumers.”
  Pharmako Biotechnologies’ HydroCurc uses LipiSperse technology, which
protects curcumin from hydrochloric acid during transit through the stomach
to address curcumin’s poor bioavailability, limited biodistribution and poor
gastrointestinal (GI) stability. In a 2019 study, researchers found HydroCurc
consistently outperformed the standard C. longa extract, demonstrated
drastic improvement on the solubility of curcuminoids, impacted the
dissolution profile, and assisted in the permeation of curcumin across a
CaCo2 cellular monolayer to show a 1.5-fold increase in the transport of
curcumin across the CaCo2 monolayer.26
  A newly launched curcumin ingredient from OmniActive (Curcuwin
Ultra+) is formulated using a specialized process to better protect and
deliver more curcuminoids than standard curcumin, per unpublished
research. In the randomized, double-blind, single-dose, crossover,
pharmacokinetic study, Curcuwin Ultra+ demonstrated 40% faster absorption

Bioavailability and curcumin: An imperfect union
   Rachela Mohr, manager for technical marketing and scientific
management of bioingredients at WACKER, noted her
company’s concern with curcumin’s bioavailability prompted
the organization to focus on increasing it. Since the ingredient
doesn’t “readily dissolve” in water, she explained, the body
eliminates up to 90% of curcumin taken orally.4
  “Several approaches have been utilized to enhance
bioavailability and bioactivity,” Monheit stated. “This includes
the addition of black pepper extract or other strategies to
deliver more active. The challenge with bioavailability is on the
one hand, it acknowledges this ingredient’s challenge, and
secondly, there are so many competing strategies and tools
to measure bioavailability—both in vitro and in vivo. It’s very
difficult to get expert consensus on the best technology and
approach and so we end up with an ‘arms race’ mentality.”
  Majeed and Antony insisted metabolism must be
considered to accurately measure the bioavailability of curcumin.
  “Bioavailability is to be considered comparing the absorption of free curcumin, which is responsible for
the beneficial aspects of curcumin,” Antony said. “The process of metabolism also releases curcumin
metabolites, which are inactive.” Arjuna’s BCM-95, a 100% pure turmeric extract, evaluated free
curcumin, but Antony explained a lot of companies either don’t mention the number or opt for “an easier
method to consider curcumin metabolites.”
  The results lead to the consumer getting shortchanged, Majeed held. “Many brands claim several
tens of times enhanced bioavailability without a proportionate advantage in health effects, exposing the
hollowness in their claims,” he revealed. “Bioavailability is often measured with the wrong methodology.
Incorrectly claimed enhanced bioavailability helps to market suboptimal amounts of curcumin.
Intestinal effects of curcumin are often ignored, including their beneficial impact on microbiota.5 The
biotransformation of curcumin into reductive metabolites is invariably glossed over.”

purpose/claim,” Monheit detailed. “We’ve all got to be properly crediting and attributing the science to

better, as it will be on the exact in-bottle formulation.”

   Curcumin has a long history in both Chinese and Indian traditional medicine practices. The Curcuma

  Another comparison surfaced in 2021, this time an analysis of retail-purchased curcumin finished
products. In this case, NOW Foods purchased curcumin/turmeric products from Amazon in June
2021 and blind-tested for potency, heavy metals, labeling accuracy and potential addition of synthetic
curcumin. Of the 23 purchased brand products and two NOW products analyzed, only one clearly failed
potency testing and four others tested very low, but without any specific label claim.
  The labeling of products was an issue, with many saying “Turmeric Curcumin 1,650 mg” on the front of
the label, but listing breakdown amounts on the back or side from various sources such as turmeric root,
turmeric extract and piperine. This effectively meant the 1,650 mg was not entirely curcumin as indicated
on the front. “This can be perceived as deceptive since many customers do not know the difference
between turmeric, turmeric extract, curcumin extract, and standardized 95% extract,” said Dan Richard,
NOW’s vice president of global sales and marketing.
  NOW also tested the products for heavy metals, including arsenic, cadmium, lead and mercury. On
average, total heavy metals were 525% higher in the other purchased brands than in the two NOW
products, with only one product testing lower than NOW’s. The company noted two products that turned
out to be B’Leaf Nature and Eagle brands had more than 20 times higher heavy metals than NOW’s
products, and they also had lead levels above California’s Prop 65 limits. Two others, Farm Haven and
BioEmblem brands, had cadmium levels above 100 ppb (parts per billion).
  Lastly, samples of the products were sent to the University of Georgia’s Center for Applied Isotope
Studies for independent radiocarbon testing to look for possible adulteration, a problem highlighted by
