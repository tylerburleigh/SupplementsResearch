# Source src-163: WebMD Taurine Overview

**Source type:** Web (WebMD vitamin/supplement database entry)
**Quality assessment:** Ok -- substantive content with effectiveness ratings, dosing, interactions, and extensive references
**Peer-reviewed:** No (tertiary evidence summary, licensed from Therapeutic Research Center)

## Core Findings

1. Taurine is rated as **"possibly effective"** for congestive heart failure (CHF) -- improving heart function, reducing symptoms, and increasing exercise capacity -- and for **hepatitis** (improving liver function).
2. Taurine is rated as **"possibly ineffective"** for obesity; it does not reduce body weight in overweight/obese people.
3. For all other claimed uses (diabetes, athletic performance, fatigue, etc.), there is **"not enough reliable information"** to determine efficacy.

## Key Evidence / Effectiveness Ratings

- **Possibly Effective:**
  - Congestive heart failure (CHF): multiple RCTs cited (Azuma et al. 1983, 1985, 1992; Beyranvand et al. 2011)
  - Hepatitis: clinical studies cited (Matsuyama et al. 1983; Hu et al. 2008)
- **Possibly Ineffective:**
  - Obesity: no weight reduction benefit demonstrated
- **Insufficient Evidence:** Diabetes, athletic performance, fatigue, and all other uses

## Practical Dosing Guidance

- **Typical dietary intake:** 40-400 mg/day from Western diet (meat, seafood, eggs)
- **Medicinal dose:** Most studies use **6 grams/day by mouth**, used for up to one year in adults
- **Safety:** Possibly safe as medicine for up to 3 months; commonly consumed in foods
- **Children:** Possibly safe for up to 12 weeks as medicine

## Safety and Interactions

- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding:** Only food amounts recommended; insufficient safety data for medicinal doses
- **Moderate interactions:**
  - Antihypertensive drugs: additive blood pressure lowering; monitor closely
  - Lithium: may slow lithium excretion, increasing serum levels; dose adjustment may be needed

## Key Study Citations (selected from ~250 references)

- **CHF:** Azuma et al. 1983, 1985, 1992 (double-blind crossover trial; clinical trials)
- **Blood pressure:** Sun et al. 2016 (RCT: taurine lowers BP in prehypertension); Fujita et al. 1987
- **Exercise performance:** Waldron et al. 2018 (meta-analysis); Rutherford et al. 2010; Balshaw et al. 2013
- **Diabetes/metabolic:** Maleki et al. 2020 (RCT: glycemic control, lipids in T2DM); Brons et al. 2004; Chauncey et al. 2003
- **Cardiovascular risk:** Wojcik et al. 2010 (coronary heart disease protection); Moloney et al. 2010 (endothelial function in T1DM)
- **Meta-analysis:** Guan & Miao 2020 (meta-analysis of RCTs on obesity, BP, lipids)
- **Liver:** Schwarzer et al. 2018 (RCT: 6g/day for portal hypertension)
- **Neurological:** El Idrissi & Trenkner 1999; O'Donnell et al. 2016 (first-episode psychosis RCT)

## Methodology

This is a tertiary evidence summary (WebMD/Therapeutic Research Center), not a primary study. It synthesizes clinical evidence using a standardized rating system (Possibly Effective, Possibly Ineffective, Insufficient Evidence). References span from 1959 to 2023, covering both primary RCTs and reviews.

## Limitations

- Not peer-reviewed; commercial health information platform
- No detailed methodology for how evidence ratings were assigned
- Does not provide effect sizes, confidence intervals, or detailed study quality assessments
- Lacks systematic review methodology
- Many references are old (1970s-1980s) and may be superseded by newer evidence
- Some references appear to be for other supplements or are tangentially related (e.g., Gymnema sylvestre, parenteral nutrition contexts)

## Relevance to Research Questions

**Q4 (Cardiovascular effects):** Moderate relevance. Confirms CHF benefit rating, lists key BP studies. Notably rates CHF as the strongest evidence base for taurine. Cites Sun et al. 2016 showing BP reduction in prehypertension.

**Q6 (Practical dosing):** Strong relevance. Provides the standard medicinal dose of 6g/day, dietary intake range of 40-400 mg/day, safety duration (up to 3 months as medicine, up to 1 year in some studies), and drug interaction warnings. Key practical guidance for supplementation recommendations.

## Claims to Verify

- **6 grams/day as most common study dose:** This is a tertiary summary claim; individual studies should be checked for actual doses used, as they may vary by condition.
- **40-400 mg/day typical Western diet intake:** This range is commonly cited but should be cross-referenced with primary dietary survey data.
- **"Possibly safe for up to 3 months":** This may be conservative; some cited studies used taurine for up to 1 year. The duration rating may reflect limited long-term safety data rather than known harm at 3+ months.
