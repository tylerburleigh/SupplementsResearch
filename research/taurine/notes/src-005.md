## Core Findings

NIH/NIA researchers found that circulating taurine levels **did not decline with age** in longitudinal data from humans, rhesus monkeys, and mice. Instead, taurine levels **increased or remained constant** with age, contradicting the premise that taurine decline is a universal hallmark of aging. The study concludes taurine is unlikely to be a reliable aging biomarker.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

1. **Longitudinal cohorts studied:**
   - Baltimore Longitudinal Study of Aging (humans aged 26-100)
   - Rhesus monkeys (aged 3-32 years)
   - Mice (aged 9-27 months)

2. **Cross-sectional cohorts (geographically distinct):**
   - Balearic Islands Study of Aging (aged 20-85), Mallorca
   - Predictive Medicine Research cohort (aged 20-68), Atlanta, Georgia
   - Study of Longitudinal Aging in Mice (cross-sectional arm)

3. **Direction of effect:** Taurine concentrations **increased with age** in all groups except male mice, where levels remained unchanged. This is the opposite of what the "taurine deficiency" hypothesis would predict.

4. **Within-individual variability:** Longitudinal analysis showed that within-individual differences in taurine levels often exceeded age-related changes, meaning natural fluctuation swamps any age signal.

5. **Inconsistent health outcome associations:** The relationship between taurine and muscle strength or body weight was inconsistent. Gross motor function analyses showed that low motor performance could be associated with either high or low taurine concentrations, and in some cases no relationship was found at all.

6. **Cross-species and cross-cohort inconsistency:** Taurine levels were inconsistently associated with health outcomes across age, species, and cohorts.

## Methodology

- Longitudinal blood sampling across three species (humans, monkeys, mice).
- Cross-sectional replication in two geographically distinct human populations.
- Measurement of circulating taurine concentration.
- Analysis of associations with functional outcomes (muscle strength, body weight, gross motor function).
- Funded by NIH/NIA in-house research program.

## Expert Quotes

- **Rafael de Cabo, Ph.D.** (study co-author, chief of Translational Gerontology Branch, NIA): "A recent research article on taurine led us to evaluate this molecule as a potential biomarker of aging in multiple species."

- **Maria Emilia Fernandez, Ph.D.** (study co-author, postdoctoral fellow, NIA): "We used longitudinal, cross-species data across the lifespan under normal conditions aimed to clarify how taurine levels change with age as a biomarker for aging, a key advance for aging research."

- **Luigi Ferrucci, M.D., Ph.D.** (study co-author, scientific director, NIA): "Identifying reliable biomarkers to predict the onset and progression of aging and functional decline would be a major breakthrough, enabling more effective, personalized strategies to maintain health and independence into old age."

## Context for Supplementation Debate

The release explicitly notes that taurine gained popularity as a supplement due to prior research showing supplementation improved age-related traits and extended lifespan in model organisms (worms, mice). However, it states: **"there is no solid clinical data that shows its supplementation benefits humans."**

## Implications

1. The foundational premise that taurine declines with age -- which motivated supplementation research -- is not supported by longitudinal data in normal/healthy populations.
2. Taurine's impact may depend on individual physiological contexts shaped by genetic, nutritional, and environmental factors, rather than being a universal aging mechanism.
3. The finding undermines the rationale for universal taurine supplementation for anti-aging purposes, though it does not rule out benefit in specific deficiency states.

## Limitations

- This is a press release, not the full paper. Detailed statistical results, effect sizes, and p-values are not reported here.
- The release does not address whether taurine supplementation might still have therapeutic effects even if endogenous levels do not decline.
- The study examined "normal conditions" -- whether disease states or stressors alter the age-taurine relationship is not addressed.
