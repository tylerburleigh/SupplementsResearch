**Author:** Guoyao Wu (Texas A&M University)
**Venue:** Amino Acids (2020) 52:329-360
**Citations:** 407 (as of fetch date)
**Type:** Invited review (comprehensive)
**Quality note:** Content is substantive full-text. Funded by National Cattlemen's Beef Association -- author notes no conflict of interest, but the pro-beef framing is notable throughout.

## Core Findings

Taurine, creatine, carnosine, anserine, and 4-hydroxyproline are abundant in red meat (especially beef) but absent from plants. These nutrients play critical roles in anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory defense, neurological function, cardiovascular health, retinal integrity, immune function, and metabolic regulation. The review argues that regular consumption of red meat (as little as 30 g dry beef/day) can fully meet daily taurine and carnosine requirements for a 70-kg adult, while also providing substantial creatine, anserine, and 4-hydroxyproline. Taurine supplementation at 0.4-6 g/day has demonstrated cardiovascular, metabolic, retinal, and muscular benefits in human clinical studies.

## Key Evidence / Data Points

### Dietary Sources of Taurine (Q1)
- **Beef taurine content** (mg/g dry weight): Chuck 2.34, Round 2.78, Loin 2.92 (Table 1, from authors' own analyses of US commercial beef)
- **Taurine is absent from all plants** tested: corn, potato, soybean, wheat flour, white rice all = 0.00 mg/g
- Taurine is also present in human milk (0.4 mM), mouse milk (0.8 mM), cat milk (2.8 mM)
- A 70-kg person has approximately 70 g total body taurine; skeletal muscle stores ~70% of this
- Human skeletal muscle: 15-20 mM taurine; heart: 28-40 mM; retina: 20-35 mM; placenta: 20-35 mM

### Taurine Synthesis in Humans (Q1)
- Humans have very low endogenous taurine synthesis capacity: hepatic cysteinesulfinate decarboxylase activity is ~1000x lower than in rats
- A healthy adult synthesizes only 50-125 mg taurine/day from methionine and cysteine
- Infants and children cannot synthesize sufficient taurine even with adequate precursors
- Vegetarians/vegans are at increased risk for taurine deficiency

### Physiological Roles Relevant to Aging (Q3)
Eight major functions identified:
1. Conjugates bile acids to form bile salts (lipid digestion, cholesterol elimination)
2. Major antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic factor
3. Physiological stabilizer of cell membranes
4. Regulator of Ca2+ signaling, fluid homeostasis, retinal photoreceptor activity
5. Contributor to osmoregulation
6. Key component of nerve and muscle conduction networks
7. Stimulator of neurological development
8. Inhibitory neurotransmitter in CNS

Aging-relevant mechanisms:
- Protects cells/tissues from ROS toxicity, excess metals, and ammonia
- Maintains integrity of plasma and organelle (especially mitochondrial) membranes
- Through N-chlorotaurine production by activated granulocytes and monocytes, contributes to killing of bacteria, fungi, parasites, and viruses

### Human Clinical Evidence (Q6)

**Table 2 -- Taurine supplementation trials in humans:**
- **1.5-2.25 g/day for 12 weeks**: Prevented electroretinographic abnormalities in children on long-term parenteral nutrition without taurine (Geggel et al. 1985)
- **0.4 or 1.6 g/day for 2 weeks**: Decreased platelet aggregation and thromboxane release in healthy adults (Xu et al. 2008)
- **1.5 g/day for 90 days**: Increased platelet taurine, decreased platelet aggregation in IDDM patients (Xu et al. 2008)
- **3 g/day for 7 weeks**: Decreased body weight, plasma TAGs, and atherogenic index in overweight/obese adults (Xu et al. 2008)
- **3 g/day for 30-45 days**: Decreased blood pressure in adults with mild/borderline hypertension (Xu et al. 2008)
- **3 g/day for 6 weeks**: Improved cardiac function in CHF patients (Xu et al. 2008)
- **6 g/day for 3 weeks**: Decreased total cholesterol, LDL in healthy adults on high-fat diet (Xu et al. 2008)
- **6 g/day for 1 week**: Decreased blood pressure in hypertensive patients (Militante and Lombardini 2002)
- **2 g/day for 4 weeks**: Ameliorated muscle cramps in chronic liver disease patients (Vidot et al. 2018)
- **9-12 g/day for 52 weeks**: Reduced recurrence of stroke-like episodes in MELAS patients (Ohsawa et al. 2019)
- **1-6 g/day for up to 2 weeks**: Improved endurance exercise performance in healthy adults (Waldron et al. 2018 meta-analysis)

**Diabetic patients**: Have 25% lower plasma taurine than normal subjects (Sak et al. 2019)
**Cancer patients on TPN**: Have 50% lower plasma taurine than healthy subjects (Gray et al. 1994)

### Recommended Intake and Safety (Q6)

**Estimated physiological requirements (70-kg adult):**
| Nutrient | Requirement (mg/day) | mg/kg BW/day | From 30g dry beef | % of daily need met |
|----------|---------------------|--------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Taurine | 75 | 1.07 | 80.4 mg | 107% |
| Creatine | 1,700 | 24.3 | 303 mg | 18% |
| Carnosine | 606 | 8.66 | 608 mg | 100% |

- Taurine requirement estimated from urinary excretion of 72 mg/day (Laidlaw et al. 1988)
- No official dietary requirements established by IOM for taurine, creatine, carnosine, anserine, or hydroxyproline
- **Safety**: "There were no side effects for the ingestion of taurine, creatine, carnosine, anserine and hydroxyproline at the indicated dosages on all the studies" (Table 2 note)
- Doses tested in humans range from 0.4 g/day to 12 g/day, with duration from 1 week to 52 weeks, all without reported side effects

### Other Nutrients Covered
- **Creatine**: 120 g total in 70-kg adult; 95% in skeletal muscle; 3 g/day supplementation improves muscle creatine saturation; beneficial for sarcopenia, neurodegenerative diseases
- **Carnosine**: 5-10 mM in human skeletal muscle; antioxidant; benefits for Parkinson's, schizophrenia, autism, diabetes, heart failure
- **Anserine**: Not naturally present in humans but dietary intake improves insulin sensitivity and cognitive function in elderly
- **4-Hydroxyproline**: Key collagen component; converts to glycine (meeting ~60% of glycine requirement); benefits for skin, bone, joint health

## Methodology

Comprehensive narrative review. The author and team conducted original analytical work measuring taurine, creatine, carnosine, anserine, and 4-hydroxyproline content in beef cuts from three US commercial packing plants (Table 1). The review synthesizes evidence from animal models and human clinical trials across multiple decades. Table 2 compiles human supplementation trials with specific dosages, durations, subject populations, and outcomes.

---

## Limitations

1. **Funding bias**: Supported by National Cattlemen's Beef Association; the paper frames beef consumption very positively throughout
2. **Narrative review**: Not a systematic review or meta-analysis; potential for selective citation
3. **Dose-response data limited**: Most human taurine trials use different doses and durations without standardized protocols
4. **Aging-specific evidence thin**: While the paper discusses aging-related dysfunction (sarcopenia, neurodegeneration), most direct taurine-aging evidence comes from animal models rather than human trials
5. **No discussion of potential risks of high red meat consumption**: The paper argues against concerns about red meat but does not engage deeply with the mechanistic literature on red meat and disease risk

---

## Claims to Verify

1. **"70-kg person has ~70 g taurine"** (HuxTable 1992) -- cited from a 1992 source; worth confirming this is still the accepted estimate
2. **"3 g/day creatine for 28 days saturates muscle creatine at 142 mmol/kg dry matter"** -- attributed to Hultman et al. 1996 and Hickner et al. 2010; well-established finding
3. **"Consumption of 30 g dry beef can fully meet daily physiological needs"** for taurine and carnosine -- based on authors' own analytical data (Wu et al. 2016), which showed high nutrient content in beef
4. **"Cysteinesulfinate decarboxylase activity is about three orders of magnitude lower"** in humans vs. rats -- attributed to Sturman and Hayes 1980

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Dietary sources of taurine)**: Excellent source. Provides quantitative taurine content in beef cuts and confirms absence from all plant foods tested. Details absorption mechanisms (TauT, GAT2/3, PAT1 transporters) and enterohepatic circulation.
- **Q3 (Physiological roles relevant to aging)**: Strong coverage of anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, cardiovascular, neurological, retinal, and metabolic functions. Links to aging through sarcopenia, neurodegeneration, metabolic syndrome discussion. However, direct aging evidence is mostly indirect (animal models, disease states).
- **Q6 (Recommended intake and safety)**: Provides estimated physiological requirements (75 mg/day taurine for 70-kg adult), safety data from clinical trials (0.4-12 g/day without side effects), and specific dosing protocols from human studies.
