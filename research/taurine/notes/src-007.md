# src-007: Taurine as a potential anti-ageing therapy

**Citation:** Sheikh A, Iqbal M. Ann Med Surg (Lond). 2023;85(7):3759-3760. doi:10.1097/MS9.0000000000000826
**Type:** Short communication / narrative review
**Quality:** Peer-reviewed, open access (PMC10328712)

## Core Findings

This short communication argues that taurine supplementation may be a viable anti-ageing strategy primarily through its antioxidant properties, specifically its ability to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS), boost superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, and preserve mitochondrial function. The authors position taurine as a low-cost, low-risk, widely available dietary intervention compared to expensive anti-ageing therapies, but emphasize that the evidence base remains thin and more rigorous trials are needed.

## Key Arguments and Mechanisms

1. **ROS and ageing:** Ageing is driven by accumulation of cellular and molecular damage, largely from free radicals and ROS. Excessive ROS from mitochondrial dysfunction and antioxidant system degeneration causes cell dysfunction, apoptosis, necrosis, and senescent cell proliferation.

2. **Taurine's antioxidant mechanisms:**
   - Neutralizes hypochlorous acid (a hazardous oxidant from neutrophils during systemic inflammation)
   - Hypotaurine (precursor) scavenges hydroxyl radicals and blocks iron ion autoxidation, reducing lipid peroxidation
   - Reduces ROS generation and boosts antioxidant enzyme activity (especially SOD)
   - Inhibits proinflammatory oxidant synthesis (e.g., superoxide anion)
   - Preserves mitochondrial function (citing Jong et al. 2021)

3. **Protein homeostasis:** Taurine acts as a chemical chaperone promoting proper protein folding and membrane trafficking. Its deficiency in skeletal muscle increases ER stress and activates genes involved in protein synthesis/metabolism/folding, leading to damaged protein accumulation and cellular ageing (citing Ito et al. 2014 on tissue depletion accelerating skeletal muscle senescence and early death in mice).

4. **Other proposed benefits:** Taurine may reduce risk of diabetes, high blood pressure, and cardiovascular disease.

## Primary Evidence Cited

- **Abud et al. 2022 (Nutrition journal):** A double-blind RCT with 24 female volunteers aged 55-70. Taurine group took 3 x 500 mg daily (1.5 g/day) for 16 weeks vs. placebo (cornstarch). Key result: ~20% increase in SOD levels in taurine group vs. 3.5% decline in control group.
- **Ito et al. 2014:** Tissue depletion of taurine accelerates skeletal muscle senescence and leads to early death in mice.
- **Jong et al. 2021:** Taurine's role in mitochondria health beyond antioxidant function.
- **Various mechanistic studies** on hypotaurine, lipid peroxidation, Nrf2/HO-1 pathway activation.

## Caveats and Limitations

- The authors explicitly state: "there are not many studies that demonstrate how it can delay ageing, particularly in humans"
- The key RCT (Abud et al.) had only 24 participants -- very small sample
- The authors call for larger taurine dosage, more participants, and longer trial periods for stronger evidence
- No discussion of dosing safety thresholds, side effects, or contraindications
- This is a narrative short communication, not a systematic review or meta-analysis
- No primary data or original experiments presented

## Comparison with Singh 2023

This paper does NOT cite or discuss Singh et al. 2023 (the high-profile Science paper on taurine deficiency as a driver of ageing). The paper was accepted May 1, 2023, which is before Singh 2023 was published (June 2023), so this is expected. The mechanisms discussed (ROS scavenging, mitochondrial preservation, SOD enhancement) are broadly complementary to but narrower than the Singh 2023 framework, which additionally covers DNA repair, stem cell function, epigenetic changes, and lifespan extension across multiple species.

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Taurine and ageing mechanisms):** Provides moderate evidence for ROS/mitochondrial mechanisms and SOD enhancement, but limited to the antioxidant frame.
- **Q3 (Human clinical evidence):** Cites one small RCT (n=24, 16 weeks, 1.5 g/day) showing 20% SOD increase. Useful as supporting evidence but limited in scope.
- **Q4 (Dietary sources and safety):** Briefly mentions dietary sources (red meat, organ meats, chicken, turkey, eggs, shellfish, fizzy drinks) and characterizes taurine as low-risk and inexpensive. No quantitative dosing guidance or safety data.

## Claims to Verify

- **"almost 20% rise in SOD levels"** in the Abud et al. 2022 RCT: This is a key quantitative claim but drawn from a secondary citation. The original paper should be checked to confirm the exact percentage and statistical significance.
- **"3.5% decline in the control group"**: Same concern -- verify from the original Abud et al. paper.
- **n=24 participants in the RCT**: Small for a clinical trial; verify this is the actual enrollment vs. completers.
