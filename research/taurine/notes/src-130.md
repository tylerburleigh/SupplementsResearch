# src-130: The Dose Response of Taurine on Aerobic and Strength Exercises: A Systematic Review

**Citation:** Chen et al. (2021), Frontiers in Physiology, 12:700352. DOI: 10.3389/fphys.2021.700352
**Type:** Systematic review (PRISMA-guided, PROSPERO CRD42021225243)
**Quality:** Peer-reviewed, open-access. 24 citations. Full text available.

---

## Core Findings

This systematic review of 10 controlled human studies systematically analyzed taurine dose-response for both aerobic and strength exercise. The key finding is that effective taurine dose depends heavily on exercise type: endurance exercise benefits from higher doses (~1 g five times daily or ~6 g acutely for metabolic effects), while strength exercise requires much lower doses (0.05 g/day). Several studies found no performance benefit at various doses, indicating that dose, timing, and exercise type critically moderate outcomes.

## Dose-Response Evidence by Study

### Aerobic / Endurance Exercise (9 of 10 studies)

| Study | Dose | Timing | N | Population | Key Result |
|-------|------|--------|---|------------|------------|
| De Carvalho 2018 | 6 g acute | 120 min pre-exercise | 9 | Male swimmers (19.78 +/- 3.38 yr) | No performance improvement; increased plasma glycerol; no lactate reduction |
| Carvalho 2020 | 6 g or 3 g acute | Pre-exercise | 17 | Male subjects (24.8 +/- 4.07 yr) | 6 g increased lipid oxidation; 3 g increased glycerol; no difference between doses for lipid oxidation |
| Galan 2018 | 3 g/day | Post-exercise for 8 weeks | 9 | Male triathletes (30 +/- 4.6 yr) | Increased CK; no significant change in IL-6, TNF-alpha; taurine levels increased |
| Kowsari 2018 | 1 g x5/day | Before and after exercise | 20 | Male squash players (23 +/- 1.41 yr) | **Significant decrease in blood lactate**; reduced neuromuscular fatigue; improved choice reaction time |
| Balshaw 2013 | 1 g acute | Pre-exercise | 8 | Male runners (19.9 +/- 1.2 yr) | **Improved 3-km running performance**; no change in lactate, heart rate, or RPE |
| Ward 2016 | 1 g acute | 2 hours pre-exercise | 11 | Male cyclists (34.6 +/- 11.5 yr) | No performance advantage in 4-km time trial; increased lactate; altered blood gas parameters |
| Milioni 2016 | 6 g acute | 90 min pre-exercise | 17 | Male trainees (25 +/- 6 yr) | No improvement in high-intensity running; no effect on lactate or VO2 |
| Rutherford 2010 | 1.66 g acute | 1 hour pre-exercise | 11 | Male athletes (27.2 +/- 1.5 yr) | No time trial improvement; **16% increase in fat oxidation** vs placebo |
| Zhang 2004 | 2 g x3/day | 7 days (post first exercise) | 11 | Male college students (18-20 yr) | **Decreased DNA damage**; increased exercise time and VO2 |

### Strength Exercise (1 of 10 studies)

| Study | Dose | Timing | N | Population | Key Result |
|-------|------|--------|---|------------|------------|
| da Silva 2014 | 0.05 g/day | 14 days pre + 21 days during eccentric exercise | 21 | Male volunteers (21 +/- 6 yr) | **Decreased CK, LDH, muscular fatigue**; increased isometric strength; increased SOD, CAT, GPx (enzymatic antioxidants); no effect on TNF-alpha/inflammation |

## Effective Dose Ranges Summary

- **Low dose (0.05 g/day):** Effective for strength exercise -- reduces muscle damage markers (CK, LDH), oxidative stress, and fatigue; increases enzymatic antioxidants
- **Moderate dose (1-1.66 g acute):** Mixed results -- 1 g improved 3-km run (Balshaw) but not 4-km cycling (Ward); 1.66 g increased fat oxidation by 16% (Rutherford); 1 g x5/day reduced lactate (Kowsari)
- **Higher dose (3 g):** Increased glycerol (Carvalho 2020) but no impact on lipid oxidation; 3 g/day for 8 weeks increased CK without clear benefit (Galan 2018)
- **High dose (6 g acute):** No effect on lactate (De Carvalho 2018; Milioni 2016); increased glycerol and lipid metabolism markers; did not improve performance in swimming or running

## Duration of Supplementation

- **Acute (single dose pre-exercise):** Most common design (6 studies); effects appear within 60-120 minutes
- **Short-term (7 days):** Zhang 2004 used 6 g/day for 7 days; found decreased DNA damage
- **Longer-term (8 weeks):** Galan 2018 used 3 g/day post-exercise; limited clear benefits
- **Prolonged (14-35 days):** da Silva 2014 used 0.05 g/day for up to 35 days for strength exercise benefits

## Null / Negative Findings

- 6 g acute taurine had **no effect on blood lactate** in high-intensity exercise (Milioni 2016; De Carvalho 2018)
- 1 g acute taurine showed **no performance benefit** in 4-km cycling time trial (Ward 2016) and even increased lactate
- 6 g acute taurine did not improve high-intensity running performance (Milioni 2016)
- Several studies found **no significant effect on VO2max** (Ward 2016; Milioni 2016; Rutherford 2010)
- No effect on inflammatory markers (TNF-alpha, IL-6) at various doses (da Silva 2014; Galan 2018)

## Populations Studied

- All 10 studies were in **males only**
- Populations ranged from college students to trained athletes (swimmers, runners, cyclists, triathletes, squash players)
- Sample sizes small: range 8-21 participants (total across all studies ~134 participants)
- No studies in females, older adults, or clinical populations

## Safety Note

- The review cites that taurine intake of 3,000-10,000 mg/day is considered safe for humans (Shao & Hathcock, 2008)

## Methodology

- PRISMA-guided systematic review
- Searched PubMed, Medline, Web of Science, Google Scholar
- 1,046 initial articles screened; 10 included
- Risk of bias: at least 5 of 10 studies had risk of bias; 4 had high blinding risk; 5 had high randomization risk
- Cochrane risk of bias tool used

## Limitations

- Only 10 studies included; all male; small sample sizes (8-21)
- High risk of bias in several studies (blinding, randomization)
- Heterogeneous exercise protocols and outcome measures
- No meta-analysis performed (only systematic narrative review)
- Did not evaluate direct DNA damage or oxidative damage markers in some studies
- Authors note excess taurine as antioxidant may interfere with mitochondrial respiration and exercise-induced adaptation

## Relevance to Research Questions

This review directly addresses dose-response relationships for exercise performance. It provides a structured table of 10 studies with specific doses, exercise types, and outcomes. Key takeaway: the relationship is non-linear and exercise-type-dependent. Lower doses (0.05 g) work for strength; higher and more frequent doses (1 g x5/day) work for endurance lactate reduction; very high acute doses (6 g) show metabolic effects (glycerol, lipid oxidation) but no direct performance improvement. Multiple null findings at various doses temper expectations.

## Claims to Verify

- "1.66 g of acute taurine administered before exercise increased fat oxidation by up to 16%" -- this comes from Rutherford et al. 2010; the original paper should be checked for the exact percentage
- Sample sizes are small (8-21); the total of ~134 across all studies is modest for a systematic review
- The assertion that 0.05 g/day is effective for strength exercise comes from a single study (da Silva 2014, n=21)
