# src-168: Taurine, Energy Drinks, and Neuroendocrine Effects

**Authors:** Jonathan J. Caine, MD; Thomas D. Geracioti, MD
**Venue:** Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine, December 2016, 83(12):895-904
**DOI:** 10.3949/ccjm.83a.15050
**Type:** Review article (narrative)
**Quality note:** Metadata flagged as "degraded/paywall" but full text was accessible and substantive.

## Core Findings

1. **Little is known about taurine's neuroendocrine effects in humans.** Most clinical studies involve taurine combined with caffeine and other energy drink ingredients, making it impossible to isolate taurine's independent effects on cognition or energy metabolism.

2. **Taurine is an inhibitory neurotransmitter and neuromodulator**, structurally analogous to GABA. It acts as an agonist at both GABA and glycine receptors (with higher affinity for glycine receptors), though with substantially lower affinity than the endogenous ligands.

3. **Energy drinks constitute a major source of taurine supplementation** (~750 mg per 8-oz serving on average), but no double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of taurine alone in healthy adults has measured cognitive or energy parameters as clinical outcomes. The strongest clinical rationale for taurine supplementation remains taurine deficiency.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Energy Drink Context and Doses
- Average taurine concentration in 49 nonalcoholic energy drinks: 3,180 mg/L (~750 mg per 8-oz serving)
- Energy drinks may contain up to 1,000 mg taurine per 8-oz serving
- Typical American diet provides 123-178 mg taurine/day; one energy drink can increase this 6-16x
- Vegetarian diet: ~17 mg/day; vegan diet: essentially 0 mg/day
- US energy drink sales exceeded $12 billion in 2012

### Cognitive Function Evidence (Human)
- **Kammerer et al (2014):** Double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT; 1 g taurine given 45 min before physical fitness and cognitive testing in young adult soldiers. **No effect** on cardiorespiratory fitness, concentration, or immediate memory. Also no effect from 80 mg caffeine.
- **Rutherford et al (2010):** 1,660 mg oral taurine 1 hour before endurance cycling. No effect on fluid intake, heart rate, subjective exertion, or time-trial performance. Small (16%) increase in total fat oxidation during 90-min exercise.
- **Zhang et al (2004):** 30-second increase in cycling energy capacity after 7 days of 6 g oral taurine, but study was neither blinded nor placebo-controlled.
- **Giles et al (2012):** Referenced for differential cognitive effects of energy drink ingredients (caffeine, taurine, glucose).
- Authors note: "we are not aware of any double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial of taurine alone in healthy patients that measured energy parameters as clinical outcomes"

### Neuroendocrine Effects
- **Sympathetic nervous system:** 6 g/day for 7 days decreased epinephrine and blood pressure in borderline hypertensive patients but not normotensive subjects; 6 g/day for 3 weeks decreased norepinephrine in healthy men.
- **Growth hormone and prolactin:** Rat data show dose-dependent effects (intraventricular injection). In epileptic patients, 375-8,000 mg/day for 4-6 months stimulated GH secretion. However, a single 75 mg/kg oral dose did not trigger acute GH or prolactin increases in humans.
- **Cortisol:** Human salivary cortisol response to 2,000 mg oral taurine was assessed but methods and data were "not adequate to draw any conclusions."
- **Sex hormones:** Limited rodent data only; human effects unknown.

### Neuroprotective and Cognitive Mechanisms (Preclinical)
- Taurine levels increase in hippocampus after experimentally induced gliosis
- Neuroprotective against glutamate excitotoxicity
- Data in Alzheimer, Huntington, and brain ischemia models show taurine inhibits neuronal apoptosis
- Improvement in memory and cognitive performance linked to taurine supplementation in old mice, but similar results not found in adult mice with normal taurine levels
- Taurine proposed as preventive treatment for Alzheimer dementia (stabilizes protein conformations)

### Safety
- No established upper safe intake level
- Highest tested dose in humans: 10 g/day for 6 months without serious adverse effects
- Multiple studies used 1-6 g/day for 1 week to 1 year without major issues
- Suggested observed safe level: 3 g/day (Shao & Hathcock, 2008)
- No observed adverse effect level established
- Taurine inhibits cytochrome P450 2E1, potentially interacting with metabolism of anesthetics, analgesics, antidepressants, antibacterials, and antiepileptics

### Age-Related Decline
- Healthy elderly (61-81) have up to 49% decrease in plasma taurine vs. younger adults (27-57)
- Due primarily to age-dependent depletion of CSD activity in the liver
- Correction of late-life taurine decline "could be beneficial for cognitive performance, energy metabolism, sexual function, and vision, but clinical studies remain to be performed"

## Methodology

Narrative review covering taurine structure, synthesis, distribution, metabolism, mechanisms, effects, safety, and therapeutic targets. Comprehensive literature search through 2016 with 158 references. Not a systematic review; no formal quality assessment of included studies.

## Limitations

- Narrative review without systematic methodology
- Most human cognitive data comes from studies combining taurine with other ingredients (confounded)
- Few clinical trials of isolated taurine in healthy humans
- Neuroendocrine data largely from animal studies
- Published 2016; does not cover more recent taurine supplementation trials

## Relevance to Research Questions

**Q1 (Cognitive endpoints):** Provides important negative/null evidence -- the only double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of isolated taurine on cognition (Kammerer et al, 1 g dose) found no effect. Preclinical data suggest neuroprotective potential, but human evidence for cognitive enhancement in healthy adults is absent. Highlights the confounding of caffeine in energy drink studies.

**Q5 (Negative evidence / concerns from energy drink context):** Directly addresses the energy drink confound -- most studies cannot separate taurine from caffeine effects. Notes that energy drink companies advertise cognitive/physical benefits from taurine but "there are few human studies that examine this contention in the absence of confounding factors such as caffeine or glucose." Also raises P450 2E1 interaction concern.

## Claims to Verify

- "average concentration of taurine was 3,180 mg/L, or approximately 750 mg per 8-oz serving" (refs 23, 24) -- plausible but worth confirming
- "the largest dosage of taurine tested in humans appears to be 10 g/day for 6 months" (ref 139) -- single study, verify
- "49% decrease in plasma taurine concentration" in elderly (ref 124) -- verify actual age ranges and sample size
- Kammerer et al (2014) found no effect from either taurine (1 g) or caffeine (80 mg) -- unusually low caffeine dose; verify study design
