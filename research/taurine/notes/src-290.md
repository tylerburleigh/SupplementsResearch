# src-290: Marcangeli et al. 2025 - "Experimental Evidence Against Taurine Deficiency as a Driver of Aging in Humans"

**Venue:** Aging Cell (2025), 24(10):e70191
**Authors:** Marcangeli V, Cefis M, Hammad R, Granet J, Leduc-Gaudet JP, Gaudreau P, Aubertin-Leheudre M, Belanger M, Robitaille R, Morais JA, Gouspillou G
**Type:** Peer-reviewed original research (open access)
**Quality Assessment:** Full text available; high-quality cross-sectional study

---

## Core Findings (2-3 sentences)

In a cohort of 137 men aged 20-93, circulating (serum) taurine levels showed **no association** with age, muscle mass, strength, power, physical performance, body composition, insulin sensitivity, inflammatory markers, or any measured aspect of mitochondrial function (respiration, ROS production, calcium retention capacity). These findings directly challenge the central claim from Singh et al. (2023) that taurine deficiency is a primary driver of aging in humans, and they also challenge taurine's utility as a biomarker of aging or functional decline.

## Specific Claims from Singh 2023 Challenged

1. **Taurine declines with age in humans.** Marcangeli et al. found NO correlation between serum taurine and age across the 20-93 year range. They note that Singh et al.'s human correlation appeared to be "mainly driven by individuals aged 20 and under" and that "participants over the age of 65 were lacking" in the Singh et al. human dataset.

2. **Taurine supplementation improves muscle strength/function.** No association between taurine levels and handgrip strength, knee extension strength, lower limb power, or muscle cross-sectional area was found. No association with functional tests (6-min walk, Timed Up and Go, 30s sit-to-stand, step test) was observed either.

3. **Taurine improves glucose metabolism.** No association between serum taurine and HOMA-IR, QUICKI, or fructosamine levels.

4. **Taurine affects mitochondrial function.** No association between serum taurine and mitochondrial respiration (state III, ADP-stimulated), H2O2 emission (ROS proxy), or mitochondrial calcium retention capacity (mCRC).

5. **Exercise increases circulating taurine.** No difference in taurine levels between active (92.40 +/- 27.04 uM) and inactive (96.93 +/- 25.16 uM) participants (p = 0.338), contradicting Singh et al.'s finding that acute exercise elevates taurine.

6. **Taurine levels are within expected range.** Serum taurine concentrations were "within the range of what was reported by Singh et al. in humans," ruling out measurement artifact.

## Key Evidence / Data Points

- **Sample:** N = 137 men, ages 20-93, stratified into 49 inactive and 88 active
- **Physical performance tests:** 6-min walk, Timed Up and Go, 30s sit-to-stand, step test -- ALL showed no correlation with taurine
- **Step test anomaly:** The ONE significant finding was a **negative** correlation between taurine and step test performance (the opposite direction of what Singh's hypothesis would predict)
- **Muscle assessments:** DXA (body composition), pQCT (thigh muscle cross-sectional area), handgrip dynamometry, isometric knee extension, Nottingham Leg Extensor Power rig -- ALL null
- **Metabolic markers:** HOMA-IR, QUICKI, fructosamine, CRP -- ALL null
- **Mitochondrial function:** Measured in permeabilized vastus lateralis myofibers (muscle biopsies) -- state III respiration, H2O2 emission, calcium retention capacity -- ALL null
- **Taurine measurement:** Isotope dilution LC-ESI-MS/MS with taurine-d4 internal standard; intra-assay CV 0.0-5.2%, inter-assay CV 7-18%
- **Statistics:** Simple linear regressions with Pearson r coefficients; p < 0.05 threshold

## Methodology

- **Study design:** Secondary analysis of data from Cefis et al. (2025) published in Cell Reports Medicine
- **Cohort:** Deeply phenotyped cross-sectional sample of men across the adult lifespan
- **Physical activity classification:** Combined accelerometry (SenseWearMini) with self-reported PASE questionnaire; active = 150+ min/week moderate-vigorous PA OR 10,000+ steps/day OR MET >= 1.6
- **Inclusion criteria:** Men aged 20-100, BMI 18-35
- **Exclusion criteria:** Uncontrolled metabolic disease, neurodegenerative disease, >2 cigarettes/day, >2 alcohol drinks/day, anticoagulants, pacemaker/metal implant
- **Muscle biopsies:** Vastus lateralis via Bergstrom needle; permeabilized fiber preparation for mitochondrial function
- **Ethics:** Approved by UQAM Ethics Committee (certificate 2020-2703)

## Limitations Acknowledged

1. **Men only** -- results may not generalize to women
2. **Cross-sectional design** -- cannot establish causation or track within-person changes over time
3. **Serum taurine only** -- did not measure intracellular/tissue taurine levels (noting that Galloway et al. 2008 found no increase in muscle taurine after 7 days of supplementation in humans, unlike the ~40% increase seen in rodents after 2 weeks per Goodman et al. 2009)
4. **Does not rule out supplementation benefits** -- authors explicitly state the study "does not rule out potential positive health impacts of taurine supplementation in older adults, especially in individuals with low circulating taurine levels and individuals with chronic diseases"
5. **Species differences** -- authors highlight that taurine metabolism may differ between mice and humans, and the human taurine supplementation literature shows mixed results (unlike clear benefits in rodent models)

## Proposed Alternative Explanations

1. **Species-specific taurine metabolism:** Taurine may be a driver of aging in mice and worms but not in humans, given known differences (e.g., supplementation increases muscle taurine in rodents but not humans per Galloway 2008)
2. **Correlation vs. causation:** Singh et al.'s human data showing taurine-disease associations may reflect reverse causation (disease lowers taurine) rather than taurine deficiency causing disease
3. **Age-range artifact in Singh et al.:** The apparent decline in Singh's human data was driven by comparing children (age <20) to adults, not by a progressive decline across adulthood
4. **Corroborating evidence:** Authors cite Fernandez et al. 2025 (published in Science while their manuscript was under review) which also challenged taurine as an aging biomarker in mice, non-human primates, and humans

## Claims to Verify

- **N = 137:** Stated in abstract and confirmed in methods (49 inactive + 88 active = 137). Appears consistent.
- **Taurine concentrations (active: 92.40 +/- 27.04 uM, inactive: 96.93 +/- 25.16 uM):** Reported in results section, p = 0.338. Seems consistent with human serum taurine literature.
- **Inter-assay CV of 18% for low-concentration standard:** This is notably high for LC-MS/MS and could introduce measurement noise, potentially attenuating correlations toward null. Worth noting as a methodological concern, though the intra-assay CVs were much better (0-5.2%).

## Relevance to Research Questions

This is a **critical counter-evidence paper** that directly challenges the Singh et al. 2023 hypothesis that taurine deficiency drives aging in humans. It provides comprehensive null results across a wide battery of aging-relevant outcomes in a well-characterized human cohort spanning the full adult lifespan. Its findings are corroborated by Fernandez et al. 2025 (Science), further strengthening the counter-evidence.
