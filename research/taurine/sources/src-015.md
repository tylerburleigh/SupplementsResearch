The Nutrient Taurine Shown to Slow Aging in Animal Studies
Boosting intake of taurine had anti-aging benefits in research involving mice and monkeys, but the impact on humans isn’t clear.
Keith Homan/Shutterstock
By
Lisa Rapaport
Fact-Checked
Published on June 13, 2023
Taurine, a nutrient found naturally in fish and meat and added as a supplement to many popular energy drinks, appears to help promote healthy aging — at least in mice and monkeys.
Low taurine levels are associated with accelerated aging in many animal species, according to a new
study published in
Science
. And taurine supplements may help some animals live longer, healthier lives, the researchers note.
While these findings are encouraging enough to start testing the effects of taurine supplements in people, it will be several years before any results from human trials are completed, says senior study author
Vijay Yadav, PhD
, an assistant professor of genetics and development at the Columbia University Vagelos College of Physicians and Surgeons in New York City.
“Until then we do not recommend supplementation,” Dr. Yadav says.
Taurine Supplements Increased the Lifespan of Middle-Aged Mice by 10 to 12 Percent
To examine the relationship between taurine and aging, researchers first looked at blood levels of this nutrient in mice, monkeys, and people. They found taurine levels decline with age for animals and humans. In fact, 60-year-old people had only about one-third as much taurine in their blood as 5-year-old kids.
Then, scientists tested the effects of daily taurine supplements in middle-aged mice. They found taurine increased the average lifespan by 12 percent for female mice and 10 percent for males. This translated into about three to four more months of life for the mice, which is roughly the same as about seven or eight years of life for humans, according to the study.
For older mice, a year of daily taurine supplements also appeared to limit age-related weight gain in females, increase bone health, improve muscle strength and endurance, promote immune health, and reduce behaviors that indicate depression and anxiety in rodents.
Middle-aged monkeys saw similar benefits. When they got daily taurine supplements for six months, these animals had less weight gain, stronger bones, a healthier immune system, and lower blood sugar levels, the study found.
These results raise the possibility that daily taurine supplements could one day help prevent or reverse some age-related health problems in people, says
Charles Mobbs, PhD
, a professor of neuroscience, endocrinology, and geriatrics at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai in New York City.
“However, as always, we cannot recommend that people immediately begin consuming such supplements until careful clinical trials have established the safety and efficacy of supplementation,” says Dr. Mobbs, who wasn’t involved in the new study.
More Research on Taurine’s Effect on Human Aging is Needed
More research is also needed to better understand exactly how taurine might help slow down natural aging processes, says
Toren Finkel, MD, PhD
, director of the Aging Institute at the University of Pittsburgh Medical Center in Pennsylvania.
“It’s very hard to extrapolate from mice to humans when it comes to aging, as our metabolism, the diseases we die from, and our lifespans are so different,” says Dr. Finkel, who wasn’t involved in the new study.
Based on the amount of taurine in supplements given to mice in the study, people would need to take about 6,000 milligrams of taurine daily to get an equivalent amount of this nutrient for their body size, Finkel notes.
“That’s at the upper limit of what is thought to be safe, but the long-term consequences of such high doses are not clear,” Finkel says. “Taurine is high in foods like fish, so absent a controlled randomized trial, I would argue that if you want to follow the tenets of this study the safest thing to do at this time is to eat more fish.”
