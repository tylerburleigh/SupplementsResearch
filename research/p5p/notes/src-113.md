# Source src-113: Metabolite Supplementation Reverses IBD Epithelial Dysfunction

**Citation:** Abbas-Egbariya H, et al. "Supplementation with endogenous healthy gut metabolites reverses the disruptions of in vitro and ex vivo epithelial functions induced by fecal content from IBD patients." *Gut Microbes* 17(1):2597568, 2025. DOI: 10.1080/19490976.2025.2597568. PMC12710890.

**Venue:** Gut Microbes (peer-reviewed, open access)
**Quality Assessment:** Full paper text available with methods, results, and discussion.

---

## Core Findings

This study developed a patient-based in vitro model to identify endogenous gut metabolites that can reverse IBD-associated epithelial dysfunction. Using pooled fecal samples from CD patients, UC patients, and healthy controls cultured with Caco-2 cells and patient-derived colonoids, the authors identified five prioritized metabolites -- including **pyridoxal (the active form of vitamin B6/P5P)** -- whose supplementation into IBD fecal content improved epithelial barrier integrity (TEER), reduced proinflammatory CXCL1 signaling, and increased cellular ATP levels. Vitamin B6 metabolism was significantly enriched in healthy controls vs. IBD, and subnormal pyridoxal levels have been reported in 29% of IBD patients.

---

## Key Evidence / Data Points

1. **Pyridoxal as one of five "good metabolites" (G-MBs):** Pyridoxal, azelate, fructose-6-phosphate, galactose 1-phosphate, and ribose 5-phosphate were prioritized through a multi-step pipeline: (a) Spearman correlation of metabolite levels with epithelial ATP/TEER outcomes (FDR < 0.25), (b) in vivo cross-validation using the SOURCE CD multi-omics cohort, and (c) commercial availability. All five were supplemented together as a mixture.

2. **Concentrations tested:** G-MBs low: pyridoxal 0.1 uM, azelate 1 uM, fructose-6-phosphate 1 uM, galactose 1-phosphate 0.1 uM, ribose 5-phosphate 0.002 uM. G-MBs high: pyridoxal 10 uM, azelate 10 uM, fructose-6-phosphate 10 uM, galactose 1-phosphate 1 uM, ribose 5-phosphate 1 uM.

3. **TEER improvement:** Supplementation of G-MBs into UC pool #3 and CD pool #2 fecal content significantly improved epithelial integrity (higher TEER) compared to IBD pools alone. Similar increases were noted with UC pool #1 and CD pool #1.

4. **CXCL1 reduction:** Supplementation significantly reduced CXCL1 secretion from epithelial cells with UC pool #3 and CD pool #2.

5. **ATP levels in colonoids:** G-MBs improved CD pool #2 ATP levels. UC pool #3 ATP levels increased only at higher concentrations. Significant ATP increases also noted with UC pool #1 and CD pool #1.

6. **Vitamin B6 pathway depletion in IBD:** Pathway analysis showed vitamin B6 metabolism significantly associated with control (non-IBD) samples (FDR <= 0.1). Literature reports subnormal pyridoxal in 29% of IBD patients (Vagianos et al., 2007).

7. **Pyridoxal mechanism:** As the active form of vitamin B6, pyridoxal serves as a coenzyme for glutathione synthesis, reducing oxidative stress in epithelial cells.

8. **Prior animal evidence:** Vitamin B6 supplementation in IL10 knockout mouse models improved molecular and histological markers of colonic inflammation (Selhub et al., 2013).

---

## Methodology

- **Study design:** Patient-based in vitro/ex vivo functional model
- **Samples:** Pooled fecal material from 20 CD, 20 UC, and 20 healthy controls across 3 independent pools (pool #1: 10/group; pools #2-3: 5/group). Samples processed to separate supernatant (metabolites) from heat-killed bacteria.
- **Epithelial models:** Caco-2 cell monolayers and rectal biopsy-derived colonoids from 5 non-IBD controls.
- **Readouts:** TEER (barrier integrity), CXCL1 secretion and mRNA (inflammation), cellular ATP (metabolic function).
- **Metabolomics:** LC-MS with 549-standards library; Spearman correlation with FDR < 0.25.
- **Cross-validation:** SOURCE CD cohort multi-omics data (terminal ileum transcriptomics + metabolomics).
- **Statistics:** One-way ANOVA with Sidak correction, paired t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests.

---

## Limitations

1. **Not a standalone P5P/pyridoxal study:** Pyridoxal was always tested as part of a 5-metabolite mixture (G-MBs), so individual contributions of pyridoxal cannot be isolated.
2. **Small sample sizes:** 20 patients per group across 3 pools; 5 colonoid lines.
3. **In vitro only:** No animal models or human clinical trials. The system uses heat-killed bacteria, eliminating dynamic host-microbe interactions.
4. **No dose-response for individual metabolites:** Only "low" and "high" concentrations of the full mixture were tested.
5. **Short-term exposure:** Colonoids exposed for 5 hours; Caco-2 cells for 24 hours. Long-term effects unknown.
6. **Non-IBD colonoids only:** The epithelial cells were from healthy donors, not IBD patients, so effects on diseased epithelium specifically were not assessed.

## Relevance to P5P Research Questions

- **Q3 (Mechanisms of action):** Directly relevant. Pyridoxal was identified as an epithelial-protective metabolite. Proposed mechanism: coenzyme for glutathione synthesis, reducing oxidative stress. Improves barrier integrity and cellular energy (ATP).
- **Q4 (Deficiency/insufficiency):** Strongly relevant. Vitamin B6 metabolism pathway was significantly depleted in IBD fecal samples. 29% of IBD patients reported to have subnormal pyridoxal. Dietary lower intake of B6 may contribute.
- **Q6 (Disease-specific evidence - Crohn's):** Relevant. CD fecal pools showed significant disruption of epithelial functions. G-MB supplementation (including pyridoxal) reversed CD-specific epithelial dysfunction. Prior IL10 knockout mouse data supports B6 anti-inflammatory effects in colitis.
- **Q1, Q2, Q5, Q7:** Not directly addressed. No bioavailability data, no specific dosing recommendations for supplementation, no healthspan endpoints, no safety data specific to supplementation.
