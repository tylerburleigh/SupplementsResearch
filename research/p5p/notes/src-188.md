# src-188: Vitamin B-6-Induced Neuropathy: Exploring the Mechanisms of Pyridoxine Toxicity

**Citation:** Hadtstein F, Vrolijk M. *Adv Nutr.* 2021;12(5):1911-1929. doi:10.1093/advances/nmab033. PMID: 33912895. PMC8483950.

**Source type:** Peer-reviewed review article (Open Access)
**Quality flag:** Metadata labeled "degraded" due to paywall_page heuristic, but full text is substantive and complete (113K chars, 1323 sentences). The content is authoritative and fully extractable.

## Core Findings

1. **Pyridoxine (PN) toxicity causes sensory peripheral neuropathy through PDXK inhibition**, leading to disrupted GABA neurotransmission in dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons, excitotoxicity, and axonal degeneration. This is proposed as the most plausible mechanism over competing theories (aldehyde toxicity, quinone methide formation, PLP-dependent enzyme inhibition).

2. **Dose-response is complex and shows large interindividual variation.** EFSA set a UL of 25 mg/d; the US set 100 mg/d. Case reports describe neuropathy at doses as low as 1.4 mg/d (Lareb database), while controlled studies found 200-500 mg/d tolerated for years in specific patient populations.

3. **PLP plasma concentrations plateau and do not increase with higher PN doses** (regulated by protein-binding availability), while PN, PL, and 4-PA concentrations rise proportionally. This suggests the toxic agent is PN itself, not PLP.

## Key Evidence / Data Points

### Dosing thresholds
- **EFSA UL:** 25 mg/d; **US UL:** 100 mg/d (line ~110)
- **Controlled dose-response study (5 healthy men):** 56.9 mg/kg/d produced symptoms in 1.5 months; 12 mg/kg/d in 7 months (line ~224-238)
- **Long-term tolerability:** 200-500 mg/d for up to 24 years in homocystinuria patients (n=17) and 8 months-6 years in hyperoxaluria patients (n=22) without neurological effects (line ~241-254)
- **Lareb case reports (1991-2017):** 90 reports of neuropathy with doses 1.4-100 mg/tablet; in 30 cases symptoms improved after discontinuation, in 29 they did not (line ~255-265)
- **Low-dose case reports:** 2 patients with neuropathy at 24 and 40 mg/d (de Kruijk & Notermans 2005); 1 patient at 30 mg/d (line ~255-265)

### Pharmacokinetics
- PLP plateaus at 300-400 nmol/L regardless of PN dose (line ~417-424)
- PN concentrations become non-normally distributed with high interindividual variation
- In some individuals, daily PN may extend PN half-life, with plasma PN reaching 1023 nmol/L by day 7 (Vrolijk et al.) (line ~652-654)
- Fasting samples (Speitling et al.) showed PN does not accumulate at steady state, while 4-PA, PLP, and PL reach steady-state elevations (line ~648-650)

### Mechanism: PDXK Inhibition
- PN has higher affinity for PDXK than other substrates; acts as competitive inhibitor of PL-to-PLP conversion (line ~814-820)
- Critical PL:PN ratio for PDXK inhibition: PLP formation favored when PL:PN > 7.5; PDXK deficiency-equivalent inhibition at PL:PN ~5.5 (line ~838-846)
- PN is relatively impermeable to BBB (log P = -1.10), confining PDXK inhibition to periphery (line ~820-832)
- Genetic PDXK mutations cause axonal sensory neuropathy with PLP deficiency (7.8-10.8 nmol/L vs reference >30 nmol/L) (line ~776-782)

### Tissue-specific effects (rat data)
- At 1400 mg/kg/d PN for 6 weeks: PLP decreased in gastrocnemius (low PNPO), increased in RBCs, unchanged in brain, liver, kidney (line ~853-865, Table 2)
- Gastrocnemius PL:PN ratio dropped to 1.11 (vs 0.44 at normal dose), supporting PDXK inhibition in tissues with low PNPO activity
- Brain maintained high PL:PN (19.05 even at high dose), protecting against PDXK inhibition

### GABA mechanism
- PDXK directly interacts with apo-GAD (glutamate decarboxylase), facilitating PLP transfer to GAD65 (line ~1204-1206)
- GAD65 exists primarily as apo-enzyme, making it vulnerable to PDXK inhibition (line ~1207-1211)
- Disrupted GABAergic presynaptic inhibition in DRG neurons may lead to excitotoxicity and Wallerian-type degeneration (line ~1233-1252)
- Chicory extract (GABAA upregulator) ameliorated PN-induced neuropathy in rats; picrotoxin (GABAA antagonist) exacerbated it (line ~1260-1262)

## Methodology

- Comprehensive narrative review synthesizing evidence from case reports, controlled human studies, animal toxicology, cell culture experiments, and pharmacokinetic studies
- Evaluates four competing mechanistic hypotheses and argues for PDXK inhibition as most plausible
- No new primary data; systematic comparison of existing evidence

## Limitations

- No primary experimental data; relies on synthesis of heterogeneous evidence
- Case report data at low doses has major limitations (unverified intake, no causality established)
- Animal-to-human extrapolation is uncertain (animals less sensitive; different fiber vulnerability patterns)
- The GABA-mediated excitotoxicity mechanism is proposed but not directly tested in DRG neurons
- Tissue-specific enzyme expression data for sensory neurons is sparse
- No specific data on PLP (P-5-P) supplementation vs PN supplementation toxicity profiles

## Relevance to Research Questions

**Q2 (Efficacy/dosing for supplementation):** Highly relevant. Establishes PN toxicity thresholds and the critical distinction between PN (the supplemented form) and PLP. PLP plasma levels plateau regardless of PN dose, suggesting diminishing returns and potential toxicity at higher PN doses. The EFSA UL of 25 mg/d vs US UL of 100 mg/d discrepancy is important for dosing recommendations.

**Q7 (Safety/toxicity):** Central source for safety assessment. Provides the most detailed mechanistic account of PN-induced neuropathy available. Documents that neuropathy risk exists even below regulatory ULs in sensitive individuals. Key insight: PN (not PLP) is the toxic agent; P-5-P supplementation may avoid the PDXK inhibition pathway entirely since PLP bypasses PDXK. However, the paper does not directly compare PN vs PLP supplementation safety.

**Q1 (Healthspan mechanisms), Q3-Q6 (Specific conditions):** Tangentially relevant. PLP's role in 56+ enzymes and the GABA pathway connect to broad metabolic health. Tissue-specific PLP depletion via PDXK inhibition could theoretically affect any PLP-dependent process, including immune function and inflammation relevant to Crohn's disease, but this is not directly explored.

## Claims to Verify

1. **"Prevalence in 2 cohorts of neuropathy patients between 2.5% and 12.4%"** (line ~100) - cited to refs 8 and 11; should verify these are peer-reviewed epidemiological studies with defined methodologies, not convenience samples.

2. **"56.9 mg/kg/d produced symptoms in 1.5 months"** (line ~228-238) - this is from Berger et al. 1992 (ref 10), a study of 5 healthy men. For a 70 kg person, 56.9 mg/kg = ~4 g/d, which is well above any supplementation dose. The relevance to typical supplementation levels (25-100 mg/d) is limited.

3. **"90 reports...doses ranging from 1.4 to 100 mg/tablet"** (Lareb data, line ~259-265) - dose refers to tablet strength, not necessarily daily intake. Multiple tablets or additional dietary sources could inflate actual exposure. The 1.4 mg lower bound seems surprisingly low and warrants scrutiny.
