Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) are liver-associated enzymes commonly used as biomarkers of hepatic injury or disease. Serum or plasma ALT and AST concentrations are typically measured on automated clinical chemistry analyzers by the determination of enzymatic activity. Pyridoxal-5'-phosphate (P5P), the active form of vitamin B6, is a catalytic cofactor required for these enzymatic reactions. In 2002, the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry and Laboratory Medicine (IFCC) published reference measurement procedures for both ALT and AST that included P5P supplementation of the assay reagents.

,
2
However, many major clinical assay manufacturers continue to provide ALT and AST reagents both with and without P5P supplementation. When the reagent is not supplemented with P5P, the extent of the reaction relies on the patient’s endogenous vitamin B6 within the blood sample. Therefore, the use of reagents without P5P supplementation may underestimate ALT and AST concentrations in patients with vitamin B6 deficiency and may fail to identify patients with significant acute or chronic liver injury.
3
,
4

In 2017, the American College of Gastroenterology and the North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition released clinical guidelines that included recommendations for specific upper reference limits for ALT.

,
6
These recommendations sparked controversy over concerns that ALT assays are not harmonized and that the universal application of ALT cutoffs may lead to overdiagnosis and unnecessary further testing.

recently emphasized 3 key issues that continue to complicate the ability to use common clinical cutoffs for ALT: (1) lack of standardization in ALT methods, particularly due to the commercial availability of both P5P-supplemented and nonsupplemented reagents; (2) lack of traceability to the IFCC reference measurement procedure; and (3) inappropriate criteria for the selection of reference populations.

The extent of variation in ALT and AST methods used across the United States and internationally is not well understood, particularly as it pertains to the use of P5P-supplemented reagents. Therefore, the College of American Pathologists (CAP) Clinical Chemistry Committee included supplemental questions within a proficiency testing mailing to assess participating laboratories’ awareness and adoption of ALT and AST reagents that are supplemented with P5P.

METHODS

In May 2023, the CAP distributed 4 optional supplemental questions to clinical laboratories that participated in the General Chemistry and Therapeutic Drugs proficiency testing program (C-B 2023) to assess awareness and adoption of P5P supplementation in ALT and AST assay reagents (Supplemental Table, see supplemental digital content at
https://meridian.allenpress.com/aplm
in the May 2025 table of contents). It was assumed that most laboratories that perform either ALT or AST testing would perform both (eg, as part of a comprehensive metabolic panel and/or hepatic function panel) and would take the same reagent supplementation approach to both tests; therefore, 1 set of supplemental questions applied for both tests. Laboratory institution types were extracted from the CAP’s demographics database, and missing types for domestic laboratories were imputed with internet search results. Logistic regression models were used to test for reagent usage differences between institution locations and institution types. A Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple pairwise comparisons. All analyses were performed with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina), and a significance level of .05 was used.

Plans and Barriers to Switch to P5P-Supplemented Reagents

Of 2611 respondents indicating whether they intend to switch to P5P-supplemented reagents in the future, only 141 (5.4%) responded “yes”; of those, 100 (3.8%) further responded “within the next year,” and 41 (1.6%) responded “later than 1 year.” Most respondents either answered “no” (920; 35.2%) or “unsure” (1550; 59.4%). The most common barrier cited was that the laboratory’s assay manufacturer does not provide supplemented reagents (1297 of 2140; 60.6%) (
Table 3
). Other barriers included staffing limitations (12.4%; 265), the cost or difficulty in managing supplemented reagents (8.6%; 183), and that the patient population would not benefit from P5P-supplemented reagents (6.4%; 136). Four hundred sixty-four laboratories (21.7%) that responded to the barriers question indicated “other” barriers. Free-text comments provided with some of those responses indicated that some laboratories considered P5P adoption as a higher-level decision (eg, medical director or corporate/network health system) (78 responses) or they were awaiting the installation of new chemistry instrumentation in order to adopt P5P-supplemented reagents (63 responses).

Comparison of Laboratory Responses With Assay Availability by Manufacturer

Owing to the high rate of the response “Our assay manufacturer does not provide it” as a barrier to use of P5P-supplemented reagents, we stratified the supplemental question responses by the laboratories’ reported reagent codes for ALT on the C-B 2023 survey (

Specifically, we compared the rate of this response (“Our assay manufacturer does not provide it”) to the actual availability of P5P-supplemented ALT reagents for each method code group that had more than 10 responses to the questionnaire. P5P reagent availability was adjudicated by the CAP Clinical Chemistry Committee’s review of ALT/AST package inserts as well as direct communication with company representatives. We found that P5P-supplemented ALT/AST reagents are indeed not available from some major manufacturers for customers in the United States, despite being available in other regions for the same systems. However, other major manufacturers, for example, Roche and Siemens, do offer P5P-supplemented reagents across all major platforms in the United States, despite 49% (241 of 496) of Roche-method respondents and 50% (133 of 266) of Siemens-method respondents indicating “Our assay manufacturer does not provide it” as a barrier to adoption.

DISCUSSION

The supplemental questions demonstrated that only 38% of participating laboratories use ALT/AST reagents supplemented with P5P, despite long-standing IFCC recommendations to do so. The most common barrier cited for using P5P-supplemented reagents was that the laboratory’s reagent manufacturer does not provide them. However, our review of package inserts for several of the major clinical chemistry manufacturers in the United States indicated that reagent versions with P5P supplementation are available for some, but not all, major platforms reported as in use by survey participants. Therefore, the results may highlight a lack of awareness of the availability of different reagent options rather than intentional decisions by laboratories to use nonsupplemented reagents. It is not always clear whether ALT and AST assays are supplemented with P5P, and the availability of P5P-supplemented reagents depends on both the reagent manufacturer and the analyzer platform used. Thus, laboratories should check their reagent package inserts and consult with their manufacturer. Further, our analysis demonstrates that there is a need for ALT and AST assay manufacturers to (1) provide P5P-supplemented reagents (in cases where they are not already available); (2) clearly label reagent products, package inserts, and proficiency testing method codes with P5P status; and (3) consider phasing out nonsupplemented reagent products such that laboratories can effectively harmonize to the IFCC-recommended assays.

Beyond reagent availability, other cited barriers included staffing limitations, the cost or difficulty in managing supplemented reagents (eg, some free-text responses indicated shortened onboard stability; some manufacturers require the laboratory to perform extra reagent preparation steps), and the belief that the patient population would not benefit or that the benefit was not clear. The anticipated patient benefits are dependent on the prevalence and severity of vitamin B6 deficiency in the population, which may be as high as 10% in the United States, with risk factors including malabsorption, drug interactions, and alcoholic hepatitis.
10
Other free-text responses revealed that some laboratories were simply unaware of the option of P5P-supplemented reagents, had not been asked to use supplemented reagents (eg, by providers), or that they were part of a broader laboratory network or health system that had not aligned to this approach.

Our results provide only current P5P supplementation status for survey respondents and thus we are not able to conclude if adoption of P5P-supplemented reagents has increased over time in response to the IFCC recommendations. However, independent of the supplemental questions, our committee’s comparison of ALT peer groups for Roche, which is the only manufacturer that has consistently provided ALT/AST survey reagent codes distinguishing “P5P” in the name, suggests that the percentage of laboratories using Roche instruments that specify P5P method codes has increased from 7% in 2013 to 19% in 2023. Our current supplemental question data indicate that some Roche users not using “P5P” method codes responded that they are using P5P-supplemented reagents, so these measurements may represent an underestimate.

Laboratories should ensure that they are reporting ALT and AST results with reference intervals that were established with their method. If a laboratory transitions to P5P-supplemented reagents, the reference intervals may need to be reestablished or reverified to align. Hypothetically, reference intervals established from methods using P5P-supplemented reagents may be slightly higher, though this would depend on the composition of the reference population and the inclusion of any patients with vitamin B6 deficiency. Our review of ALT and AST package inserts found that this was not always the case for reference intervals provided by manufacturers. In the absence of harmonized ALT and AST assays, laboratories should partner with their clinician stakeholders to carefully determine reference intervals that are appropriate for their specific method and patient population, rather than rely on proposed universal cutoffs that do not take method variation into consideration. When establishing or verifying reference intervals, laboratories should consider the exclusion of patients with obesity or regular alcohol consumption.
11

Limitations of this study include the distribution of the supplemental questions only to laboratories that participated in this proficiency testing event, and the question design that combined ALT and AST, given that it is possible that some laboratories may not take the same approach for both tests. Further, it was not feasible to confirm availability of P5P-supplemented reagents for ALT and AST assays from all manufacturers, instruments, and countries that participated in the C-B 2023 program.

CONCLUSIONS

Overall, the results highlight the degree of variation of ALT and AST method approaches and a general lack of awareness of the existence and benefits of P5P-supplemented ALT and AST reagents. Both laboratories and assay manufacturers have roles in improving awareness and adoption of P5P-supplemented reagents. Clinical laboratories are encouraged to (1) review ALT and AST reagent options with their assay manufacturer; (2) ensure that they are using reference intervals aligned to their specific reagent and patient population; and (3) confirm that they are using the appropriate ALT and AST reagent codes when submitting proficiency testing results.
