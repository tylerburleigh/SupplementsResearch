- **Authors:** Day, Christopher R.; Kempson, Stephen A.
- **Year:** 2016
- **Venue:** Biochimica et Biophysica Acta (BBA) - General Subjects, 1860(6), 1098-1106
- **DOI:** 10.1016/j.bbagen.2016.02.001
- **Citations:** 202
- **Study Type:** Narrative review (comprehensive, mechanistic focus)
- **Quality:** ok - full author manuscript with substantive content

## Core Findings

This review comprehensively examines betaine's dual roles as an osmoprotectant (kidney) and methyl group donor (liver), with a focus on therapeutic potential for NAFLD and ALD. Animal models consistently demonstrate that dietary betaine supplementation can prevent and reverse hepatic steatosis through multiple mechanisms (AMPK activation, normalization of lipid metabolism genes, ER stress relief, mitochondrial preservation). However, the sole human RCT in NASH (55 patients, 12 months) was underpowered and inconclusive, showing no significant improvement. The authors identify potential species differences in BHMT kinetics (human Km potentially 30-fold higher than rat/pig) as a possible translational barrier and call for larger, longer, rigorously controlled human trials.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Dietary Intake and Pharmacokinetics
- Average US dietary betaine intake: 100-300 mg/day, rarely exceeds 400-500 mg/day (line 67-68)
- Plasma betaine: 20-70 micromol/L, not protein-bound (line 63-64)
- Highest tissue concentrations in liver, kidney, and testes
- Endogenous synthesis from choline occurs in liver and kidney
- Betaine concentration in rat liver tissue water: 0.4-4.1 mM (line 519-520)
- Oral betaine safe dose range: 3-30 g/day (line 560)
- LD50 >10 g/kg body weight in rats (line 949)

### Methylation / One-Carbon Metabolism
- Betaine donates a methyl group to homocysteine via BHMT, producing methionine and dimethylglycine (DMG)
- BHMT and methionine synthase contribute equally to homocysteine remethylation in liver (line 212)
- BHMT represents up to 2% of total soluble liver protein (line 482-483)
- DMG is a strong non-competitive inhibitor of BHMT (line 204)
- BHMT is a zinc metalloenzyme; homocysteine must bind first before betaine can bind (line 203-204)
- BHMT-2 has 80% identity to BHMT but cannot use betaine as methyl donor (line 207-208)

### BHMT Kinetics and Species Differences
- Rat/pig BHMT Km for betaine: averages ~0.064 mM (variable but generally low) (line 505-506)
- Human BHMT Km for betaine: ranges from 0.023 mM to 2.47 mM across studies (line 509, Table 3)
- Some human Km values exceed 2.0 mM, more than 30-fold higher than rat/pig averages
- Variability exists even within the same research group (line 509-510)
- No direct comparison under identical laboratory conditions available (line 511-512)
- Authors conclude evidence remains equivocal; cannot definitively ascribe human trial failure to BHMT kinetics (line 529-531)

### Animal Model Evidence (NAFLD)
- Mice fed high-fat diet + betaine: increased SAM, prevented betaine deficiency, insulin resistance, and steatosis (line 372-374)
- Betaine administered after NAFLD development reversed insulin resistance and steatosis (line 374-375)
- Mechanisms: normalization of insulin signaling, gluconeogenesis, glycogen synthesis pathways (line 375-376)
- Betaine restored adipose tissue function and insulin sensitivity, partly via ER stress relief (line 377-379)
- AMPK activation in liver associated with attenuation of steatosis (line 380-381)
- Down-regulation of lipogenic genes and up-regulation of fatty acid oxidation genes via genomic methylation (line 389-393)
- BHMT knockout mice: 8-fold plasma homocysteine increase, 21-fold liver betaine increase, 43% SAM decrease, ~75% methylation potential reduction, fatty liver by 5 weeks (line 483-486)

### Animal Model Evidence (ALD)
- Ethanol inhibits methionine synthase (folate cycle), impairing homocysteine remethylation (line 421-423)
- Betaine provides alternative remethylation pathway via BHMT (line 424-425)
- Betaine normalized homocysteine in alcohol-fed mice: decreased fatty liver, inflammation, ER stress (line 425-428)
- Co-feeding betaine with ethanol preserved mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation proteins in rats (line 433-440)
- Betaine may increase alcohol elimination rate via epinephrine-mediated increase in NAD+/NADH ratio (line 445-449)

### Human Clinical Evidence
- **NASH trial (Abdelmalek et al., 2009):** 55 patients, 12 months, betaine did not improve hepatic steatosis or normalize liver enzymes. Only 35 completed the study - underpowered and inconclusive (line 470-476)
- **Hyperhomocysteinemia:** Betaine at >=6 g/day effective for severe hyperhomocysteinemia (homocystinuria); rapid sustained homocysteine reduction in mild cases (line 226-231)
- **Body composition:** Betaine supplementation did not affect body weight or body composition in human subjects (Schwab et al., 2002) (line 238-239)
- Inability to verify liver betaine accumulation from oral supplements in humans (line 532-533)

### Transport and Bioavailability
- BGT1 (betaine-GABA transporter) is much more abundant in liver than kidney (line 297-298)
- Additional betaine uptake pathways in hepatocytes: SNAT (amino acid) and OCTN2 (carnitine) transporters (line 304-306)
- Beta-alanine is a naturally occurring BGT1 inhibitor; may impede betaine uptake in disease states (line 538-539)
- Kidney reabsorbs filtered betaine nearly completely; SIT1 transporter in proximal tubule, BGT1 in medulla (line 282-287)

## Methodology Notes

- Narrative review synthesizing data from multiple animal and human studies
- No systematic search strategy described; authors selected key studies illustrative of betaine's roles
- Provides original Table 3 comparing BHMT kinetic constants across species from multiple published sources
- Covers chemistry, physiology (kidney osmoregulation, liver methylation), and therapeutic applications
- Heavy emphasis on mechanistic data from rodent models; limited human data discussed

## Limitations

1. Narrative review without systematic methodology - potential selection bias
2. Animal model data predominantly from mice and rats with different dietary induction protocols; cross-study comparisons are complicated
3. Rats may be more resistant to some aspects of ethanol-induced liver injury than mice (line 553-554)
4. Only one human NASH trial discussed (Abdelmalek et al.); authors note it was underpowered
5. BHMT kinetic data across species come from different laboratories using different methods - no direct head-to-head comparison
6. Betaine and homocysteine concentrations in human liver are largely unknown (line 524-525)

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (Dietary Intake):** Directly reports average US intake (100-300 mg/day) and food sources (whole grains, spinach, shrimp, beets). Discusses endogenous synthesis from choline.
- **Q2 (Clinical Endpoints):** Covers the key NASH RCT (negative but underpowered), hyperhomocysteinemia treatment (positive), and body composition (no effect). Strong animal evidence for liver outcomes but limited human confirmation.
- **Q3 (Methylation Cofactors/MTHFR):** Detailed mechanistic coverage of BHMT pathway, one-carbon metabolism, SAM generation, and the interplay between folate-dependent and betaine-dependent remethylation. Discusses DMG as BHMT inhibitor.
- **Q4 (Tradeoffs vs Alternatives):** Discusses species differences in BHMT kinetics as a potential explanation for why betaine works in rodents but not humans. Notes methionine synthase (folate pathway) as the alternative remethylation route.
- **Q5 (Adversarial/Contradicting Evidence):** Highlights the disappointing human NASH trial result, the equivocal BHMT kinetics data, and the inability to confirm liver betaine accumulation from supplements in humans.
- **Q6 (Dose-Response/Safety):** Reports safe oral dose range (3-30 g/day), LD50 (>10 g/kg in rats), effective dose for hyperhomocysteinemia (>=6 g/day). Notes betaine is inexpensive and well-tolerated.
- **Q7 (Animal/Mechanistic Longevity):** Extensive mechanistic data from rodent NAFLD and ALD models showing betaine's effects on AMPK activation, gene methylation, ER stress, mitochondrial preservation, and insulin sensitivity. BHMT knockout data showing dramatic metabolic consequences.

## Claims to Verify

1. **"BHMT represents up to 2.0% of total soluble protein" in liver (line 482-483)** - cited to Pajares & Perez-Sala 2006 (ref 24). This is a high figure worth cross-referencing with the primary source.
2. **Human BHMT Km values ranging from 0.023 to 2.47 mM (Table 3)** - this 100-fold range is remarkable. The source notes variability even within the same research group (line 509-510), suggesting methodological factors may account for much of the spread.
3. **Average dietary betaine intake 100-300 mg/day (line 67)** - cited generically without a specific primary reference in this section; should be verified against the Craig 2004 reference (ref 2).
