# src-051: Effect of Homocysteine-Lowering Nutrients on Blood Lipids

## Metadata
- **Title**: Effect of Homocysteine-Lowering Nutrients on Blood Lipids: Results from Four Randomised, Placebo-Controlled Studies in Healthy Humans
- **Authors**: Olthof MR, van Vliet T, Verhoef P, Zock PL, Katan MB
- **Year**: 2005
- **Venue**: PLoS Medicine, Volume 2(5), e135
- **DOI**: 10.1371/journal.pmed.0020135
- **Citations**: 86
- **Quality**: ok (full text available, 63K characters, 658 sentences)
- **Type**: Pooled analysis of four RCTs (betaine: 3 studies; folic acid: 2 studies; phosphatidylcholine: 1 study)

## Core Findings (2-3 sentences)
Betaine supplementation at 6 g/d for 6 weeks significantly increased LDL cholesterol by 0.36 mmol/L (11%) and triacylglycerol by 0.14 mmol/L (13%) in healthy humans, while also lowering fasting homocysteine by 12-20%. These adverse lipid effects may offset the cardiovascular benefit expected from homocysteine lowering. Folic acid (0.8 mg/d) lowered homocysteine without affecting lipids, making it the preferred agent for homocysteine reduction.

## Key Evidence and Data Points

### Betaine and LDL Cholesterol (primary finding)
- **6 g/d betaine, 6 weeks, combined across Studies 1-3 (n=151)**:
  - Total cholesterol: +0.42 mmol/L (8%), 95% CI: 0.30-0.55
  - LDL cholesterol: +0.36 mmol/L (11%), 95% CI: 0.25-0.46
  - Triacylglycerol: +0.14 mmol/L (13%), 95% CI: 0.04-0.23
  - Total/HDL ratio: +0.23 (6%), 95% CI: 0.14-0.32
  - HDL cholesterol: no change (0.01 mmol/L, 95% CI: -0.03 to 0.04)

### Dose-Response
- 1.5 g/d and 3 g/d betaine raised LDL cholesterol but **not statistically significant**
- Effect on LDL cholesterol was evident **after 2 weeks** (Studies 1 and 2)
- Study was not originally powered for lipid outcomes; dose-response relationship not definitively established

### Homocysteine Lowering by Betaine (from prior publications)
- 6 g/d: 12-20% reduction in fasting homocysteine
- 3 g/d: 15% reduction
- 1.5 g/d: 12% reduction
- Post-methionine-load homocysteine: 20-40% reduction at 6 g/d

### Folic Acid (0.8 mg/d, 6 weeks, Studies 1 and 3, n=75)
- No effect on any lipid parameter
- Lowered homocysteine by 20-21%

### Phosphatidylcholine (~2.6 g/d choline, 2 weeks, Study 4, n=26 men)
- Triacylglycerol: +0.14 mmol/L (8%), p<0.05
- No effect on total, LDL, or HDL cholesterol
- Authors note this could be a chance finding; results inconclusive

## Methodology Notes

### Study Designs
- **Study 1**: Parallel, 6 wk, n=36 (ages 30-50), 3 arms (6g betaine / 0.8mg folic acid / placebo), 12/group
- **Study 2**: Parallel, 6 wk, n=76 (ages 30-50), 4 arms (6g / 3g / 1.5g betaine / placebo), 19/group
- **Study 3**: Cross-over, 6 wk per period, 6-wk washout, n=39 (ages 50-70), 3 treatments (6g betaine / 0.8mg folic acid / placebo)
- **Study 4**: Cross-over, 2 wk per period, 2-wk washout, n=26 men (ages 50-71), 2 treatments (phosphatidylcholine ~2.6g choline / placebo oil)

### Analysis
- Blood lipid measurements were **post-hoc** for Studies 1 and 2 (originally powered for homocysteine)
- Pre-planned for Studies 3 and 4 but not powered for lipid endpoints
- Combined effect calculated using weighted mean method (Curtin et al., 2002)
- All studies placebo-controlled, double-blind
- LDL cholesterol calculated via Friedewald formula
- Participants selected for higher-than-average homocysteine (8.4-23.1 umol/L ranges)

### Limitations
1. **Not originally powered for lipid outcomes** -- the combined analysis was done post-hoc
2. Participants had elevated homocysteine (selected from top of distribution), limiting generalizability to normohomocysteinemic populations
3. Short intervention duration (2-6 weeks) -- unknown whether lipid effects persist, plateau, or normalize with chronic use
4. Different blood matrices across studies (EDTA plasma, heparin plasma, serum) -- authors addressed this by computing within-matrix changes only
5. No direct assessment of clinical endpoints (CVD events); risk estimates based on epidemiological modeling

## Proposed Mechanism
Betaine and phosphatidylcholine increase blood lipids through increased VLDL secretion from the liver. The proposed pathway:
1. Betaine increases S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe) via enhanced remethylation of homocysteine to methionine
2. SAMe serves as methyl donor for phosphatidylethanolamine -> phosphatidylcholine conversion (PEMT pathway)
3. Increased phosphatidylcholine availability promotes VLDL assembly and secretion
4. More VLDL export -> higher circulating lipids

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q1 (dietary intake)**: Notes dietary betaine intake is 0.5-2 g/d from wheat products, beets, spinach. Supplemental doses of 1.5-3 g/d overlap with high-end dietary intake.
- **Q2 (clinical endpoints)**: No direct clinical endpoints. Estimates CVD risk tradeoff: ~10% increase from LDL rise vs. ~10-15% decrease from homocysteine lowering. Net effect may be neutral or slightly harmful.
- **Q3 (methylation cofactors/MTHFR)**: Directly addresses the betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase (BHMT) pathway. Shows betaine lowers homocysteine 12-20% at various doses. Does not specifically address MTHFR polymorphisms but mentions related meta-analysis (Klerk et al.).
- **Q4 (tradeoffs vs alternatives)**: **Strongly relevant.** Directly compares betaine vs. folic acid for homocysteine lowering. Concludes folic acid is preferred because it lacks adverse lipid effects. Phosphatidylcholine is intermediate (raises TAG only, not LDL).
- **Q5 (adversarial/contradicting evidence)**: **Strongly relevant.** This is a key adversarial finding: betaine supplementation raises LDL cholesterol and triacylglycerol, potentially negating cardiovascular benefits. This directly contradicts the narrative that TMG is purely beneficial for cardiovascular health.
- **Q6 (dose-response/safety)**: Addresses dose-response (1.5, 3, 6 g/d). Lower doses showed non-significant trends toward LDL increase. 6 g/d is established as the therapeutic dose for homocystinuria. Time course shows effect within 2 weeks.
- **Q7 (animal/mechanistic longevity)**: Proposes VLDL-mediated mechanism but does not directly study longevity or animal models. Cites supporting animal studies (Hayes et al. rat studies, Matthews et al. pig studies).

## Claims to Verify
- The combined weighted mean effect is stated as a fixed-effects meta-analytic approach (Curtin et al. 2002). The heterogeneity across studies appears moderate -- Study 1 showed 0.63 mmol/L LDL increase while Study 2 showed 0.28 mmol/L (overlapping CIs but clinically different magnitudes). The weighting method should be confirmed as appropriate for heterogeneous study designs (parallel vs. cross-over).
- The statement that "6 g/d is the lowest therapeutic dose used to lower plasma homocysteine in hyperhomocysteinemic patients" (line 352) references Brenton et al. 1966, which is a very old citation and may not reflect current practice.
- The CVD risk estimates (10% from LDL, 6% from TAG, 10-15% reduction from homocysteine) are modeled from epidemiological data, not directly measured -- these are approximations that depend on the validity of the underlying risk models.
