# Source src-047: Effect of Folic Acid and Betaine Supplementation on FMD

## Metadata

- **Title:** Effect of Folic Acid and Betaine Supplementation on Flow-Mediated Dilation: A Randomized, Controlled Study in Healthy Volunteers
- **Authors:** Olthof MR, Bots ML, Katan MB, Verhoef P
- **Year:** 2006
- **Venue:** PLoS Clinical Trials
- **DOI:** 10.1371/journal.pctr.0010010
- **Citations:** 22
- **Quality:** ok (substantive full-text content available)

## Study Design

- **Type:** Randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind, crossover trial
- **Registration:** ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00102843
- **Participants:** 39 healthy men and women, aged 50-70 years (of 40 enrolled; 1 withdrew for relocation). Selected from 87 eligible participants for the 40 with highest plasma homocysteine (range 10.2-21.7 umol/L at screening).
- **Interventions:** Each participant received all three treatments in random order for 6 weeks each with 6-week washout between:
  - 6 g/d betaine (BUFA B.V.)
  - 0.8 mg/d folic acid + 6 g lactose
  - 6 g/d lactose (placebo)
- **Supplements** dissolved in water, taken twice daily (after breakfast and evening meal). Quinine (2 mg) added to all supplements to mask taste differences.
- **Primary outcomes:** Flow-mediated dilation (FMD) of the brachial artery and plasma homocysteine concentrations. Duplicate FMD measurements on days 41 and 43 of each period.
- **Power calculation:** 30 participants needed to detect absolute difference of 2 FMD% (power=0.8, alpha=0.05). 40 enrolled to allow for attrition.
- **Statistical analysis:** Linear mixed effects models (SPSS 12.0), Tukey's pairwise comparisons, treatment-by-period interaction to check carryover.

## Core Findings

1. **Betaine lowered homocysteine by 12%** (-1.2 umol/L; 95% CI: -1.6, -0.8) relative to placebo (p<0.001).
2. **Folic acid lowered homocysteine by 20%** (-2.0 umol/L; 95% CI: -2.3, -1.6) relative to placebo (p<0.001).
3. **Neither supplement improved FMD.** Mean FMD after placebo was 2.8 +/- 1.8 FMD%. Differences vs. placebo: betaine -0.4 FMD% (95% CI: -1.2, 0.4); folic acid -0.1 FMD% (95% CI: -0.9, 0.7).
4. **Betaine increased LDL cholesterol ~11% and triacylglycerol ~13%** (reported in companion publication Olthof et al. 2005, PLoS Med).
5. **No serious adverse events.** 112 non-serious adverse events in 31 participants, mostly headache/migraine (59) and common cold/influenza (29).

## Methodology Notes

- Crossover design (each participant served as own control) with 6-week washout -- appropriate for reducing inter-individual variability in FMD.
- Duplicate FMD measurements on two separate days per treatment period to reduce within-participant variability (CV ~65%).
- All images read in duplicate by blinded reader (reading CV 22%).
- Standardized meals provided on days preceding FMD measurements to control for dietary confounders.
- No nitroglycerine (NTG) test or Doppler measurements performed -- acknowledged as a limitation.
- Good compliance verified by >2-fold increase in serum folate with folic acid treatment.

## Limitations

1. Surrogate outcome (FMD), not clinical cardiovascular events -- short 6-week treatment periods.
2. No NTG (endothelium-independent vasodilation) test performed.
3. No Doppler flow measurements after cuff release.
4. Small sample (n=39 completers) -- though authors argue power was sufficient given narrow CIs.
5. Healthy population only -- results may not generalize to patients with cardiovascular disease.
6. The adverse lipid effects of betaine (increased LDL and triglycerides) could theoretically have counteracted any benefit from homocysteine lowering, though folic acid also showed no FMD improvement.

## Relevance to TMG/Betaine Research

- **Direct RCT evidence for betaine (TMG) at 6 g/d dose** -- shows effective homocysteine lowering but no improvement in a validated cardiovascular surrogate marker.
- **Negative/null result for cardiovascular benefit** of betaine supplementation in healthy adults.
- **Safety signal:** betaine increased LDL and triglycerides (~11-13%), which is a potential concern.
- **Comparison with folic acid:** both agents lowered homocysteine via different pathways (betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase vs. folate-dependent remethylation), and neither improved FMD, arguing against homocysteine as a causal factor for vascular dysfunction in healthy people.
- Comprehensive review table (Table 3) of 17 studies on folic acid and FMD in healthy and patient populations -- useful contextual reference.

## Relevance by Research Question

- **Q2 (clinical endpoints):** Null result for betaine on FMD (cardiovascular surrogate). Moderate relevance -- FMD is a surrogate, not a hard endpoint.
- **Q3 (methylation cofactors/MTHFR):** Shows betaine effectively lowers homocysteine by 12% at 6 g/d, confirming its role as a methylation cofactor.
- **Q5 (adversarial/contradicting evidence):** Strong -- this is a well-designed RCT showing no cardiovascular benefit and potential lipid adverse effects from betaine.
- **Q6 (dose-response/safety):** Provides data on 6 g/d dose, reports adverse lipid changes, and notes no serious adverse events.

## Claims to Verify

- LDL increase of ~11% and triacylglycerol increase of ~13% with betaine are from a companion paper (ref 39, Olthof et al. 2005 PLoS Med), not directly measured in this study's results section. Should be cross-referenced with that primary source.
- The assertion that the study was "sufficiently powered" based on narrow CIs is an arguable post-hoc justification. The actual FMD effect sizes observed were far smaller than the 2 FMD% the study was designed to detect.
