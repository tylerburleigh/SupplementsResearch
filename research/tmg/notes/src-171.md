# src-171: Homocysteine, B vitamins, and cardiovascular disease: a Mendelian randomization study

## Metadata
- **Title:** Homocysteine, B vitamins, and cardiovascular disease: a Mendelian randomization study
- **Authors:** Shuai Yuan, Amy M. Mason, Paul Carter, Stephen Burgess, Susanna C. Larsson
- **Year:** 2021
- **Venue:** BMC Medicine, 19:97
- **DOI:** 10.1186/s12916-021-01977-8
- **Study Type:** Mendelian randomization (two-sample MR using GWAS summary statistics)
- **Quality Assessment:** Full text available, high-quality peer-reviewed MR study. Open access (CC-BY).

## Core Findings (2-3 sentences)

This Mendelian randomization study found suggestive (but not Bonferroni-significant) evidence that higher genetically predicted homocysteine increases stroke risk (OR 1.11 per SD, p=0.008), with the strongest effect for subarachnoid hemorrhage (OR 1.26, p=0.013). Higher genetically predicted folate was suggestively protective against coronary artery disease and stroke, while vitamin B6 was suggestively protective against ischemic stroke. Vitamin B12 showed no association with any cardiovascular outcome.

## Key Evidence/Data Points

1. **tHcy and stroke:** Per 1-SD increase in genetically predicted tHcy: any stroke OR 1.11 (95% CI 1.03-1.21, p=0.008), subarachnoid hemorrhage OR 1.26 (95% CI 1.05-1.51, p=0.013), ischemic stroke OR 1.11 (95% CI 1.03-1.21, p=0.011).
2. **Folate and CVD:** Higher genetically predicted folate: CAD OR 0.88 (95% CI 0.78-1.00, p=0.049), any stroke OR 0.86 (95% CI 0.76-0.97, p=0.012).
3. **Vitamin B6:** Ischemic stroke OR 0.88 (95% CI 0.81-0.97, p=0.009), but peripheral artery disease OR 1.30 (95% CI 1.09-1.54, p=0.004) -- conflicting direction.
4. **Vitamin B12:** No association with any of 12 CVD outcomes studied.
5. **Multiple testing correction:** Bonferroni threshold was p<0.001 (4 exposures x 12 outcomes). None of the suggestive associations survived this threshold, meaning all findings are nominally significant only.
6. **Homocysteine-lowering efficacy:** Folic acid reduces blood homocysteine by ~25%; B12 adds an additional ~7% reduction.

## Methodology Notes

- **Design:** Two-sample Mendelian randomization using summary-level GWAS data
- **Instrumental variables:** 14 SNPs for tHcy, 2 for folate, 1 for B6, 14 for B12 (genome-wide significance p<5x10^-8, LD clumped r2<0.01)
- **Variance explained:** SNPs explained 6.0% of tHcy variance, 1.0% for folate, 1.3% for B6, 6.0% for B12
- **Outcome data:** 12 CVD endpoints from CARDIoGRAMplusC4D, MEGASTROKE, HERMES, ISGC, UK Biobank, and FinnGen
- **Main analysis:** Multiplicative random-effects inverse-variance-weighted model
- **Sensitivity analyses:** Weighted median, MR-Egger, MR-PRESSO; also sensitivity analysis excluding 4 pleiotropic SNPs (associated with blood lipids)
- **Population:** Primarily European ancestry (to minimize population structure bias)
- **Key limitation:** Only 1 SNP for vitamin B6, derived from a GWAS of n=1,864 -- small discovery sample

## Relevance to TMG/Betaine Supplementation

**Direct relevance:** The paper's Figure 1 explicitly describes the homocysteine remethylation pathway, noting that homocysteine is reconverted to methionine "by receiving a methyl group from 5-methyltetrahydrofolate, the active form of folate, or betaine in the remethylation pathway." This places betaine (TMG) directly in the metabolic pathway under study.

**Indirect relevance:** The study establishes that genetically elevated homocysteine increases stroke risk, and that folate (which lowers homocysteine via the same remethylation pathway betaine operates in) is suggestively protective against CVD. This provides a mechanistic rationale for TMG supplementation: if lowering homocysteine reduces stroke risk, and betaine is an alternative homocysteine-lowering agent, TMG supplementation may also reduce stroke risk through the same pathway.

**Key caveat:** The study does not directly examine betaine/TMG. The genetic instruments relate to the folate-dependent remethylation pathway, not the betaine-dependent one. Additionally, RCTs of B-vitamin homocysteine-lowering therapy have generally failed to show CVD benefit, suggesting that simply lowering homocysteine may not be sufficient -- the MR signal may reflect pleiotropic genetic effects.

## Limitations

1. All associations are "suggestive" -- none survive Bonferroni correction (p<0.001 threshold)
2. European ancestry only -- limited generalizability
3. Possibility of horizontal pleiotropy (some tHcy SNPs also affect blood pressure, HDL cholesterol)
4. Vitamin B6 instrument derived from very small GWAS (n=1,864) with no positive control validation
5. MR studies assume linear effects and may not capture non-linear relationships
6. Possible canalization (developmental compensation for genetic variants) could bias results
7. Bonferroni correction may be overly conservative, inflating type 2 error

## Claims to Verify

- The tHcy-associated SNPs explained 6.0% of variance (line 114). This is a round number; verify against the cited source (van Meurs et al. 2013).
- Vitamin B6 GWAS had only n=1,864 (line 101). This is unusually small for a GWAS and warrants noting as a fragile instrument.
- Folic acid reduces homocysteine by 25%, B12 adds 7% (line 267-268). These are cited from the Homocysteine Lowering Trialists' Collaboration meta-analysis (BMJ 1998). The numbers are well-established in the literature but worth cross-referencing.
