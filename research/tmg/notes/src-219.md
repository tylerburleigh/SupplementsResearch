# src-219: Homocysteine and Cardiovascular Disease: Evidence on Causality from a Meta-Analysis

## Bibliography
- **Title:** Homocysteine and cardiovascular disease: evidence on causality from a meta-analysis
- **Authors:** David S Wald, Malcolm Law, Joan K Morris
- **Year:** 2002
- **Venue:** BMJ (British Medical Journal), Volume 325, Issue 7374, pages 1202-1208
- **DOI:** 10.1136/bmj.325.7374.1202
- **Citations:** 1,459
- **Quality:** ok (full text extracted via raw text fallback from PDF)

## Study Type
Systematic review and meta-analysis combining two distinct study designs:
1. **72 genetic (MTHFR) studies** -- a Mendelian randomization approach using the C677T polymorphism as an instrumental variable (16,849 cases total)
2. **20 prospective cohort studies** of serum homocysteine and disease events (3,820 participants, 3,820 events across disease types)

Three disease endpoints: ischaemic heart disease (IHD), deep vein thrombosis with/without pulmonary embolism (DVT), and stroke.

## Core Findings

This paper provides what was, at the time of publication, the strongest evidence that elevated serum homocysteine is a **causal** risk factor for cardiovascular disease. The key innovation is combining MTHFR genetic studies (which approximate a randomized experiment) with prospective observational studies -- two designs with different potential biases that converged on quantitatively similar effect estimates.

**Primary effect sizes (odds ratio per 5 umol/l increase in homocysteine):**
- IHD: 1.42 (95% CI 1.11-1.84) in MTHFR studies; 1.32 (1.19-1.45) in prospective studies; combined 1.33 (1.22-1.46)
- DVT: 1.60 (1.15-2.22) in MTHFR studies only (no prospective studies available)
- Stroke: 1.65 (0.66-4.13) in MTHFR studies; 1.59 (1.29-1.96) in prospective studies; combined 1.59 (1.30-1.95)

**Risk reduction projections from lowering homocysteine by 3 umol/l** (achievable with ~0.8 mg/day folic acid):
- IHD: 16% reduction (11-20%)
- DVT: 25% reduction (8-38%)
- Stroke: 24% reduction (15-33%)

## Methodology Notes

- **Mendelian randomization logic:** The MTHFR C677T polymorphism creates a natural experiment. About 10% of people are TT homozygotes, 43% CT heterozygotes, 47% CC wild-type. TT homozygotes have ~2.7 umol/l higher homocysteine than CC (~20% elevation). Because genotype is randomly assigned at conception, TT and CC groups should not differ systematically in other CVD risk factors. Data confirmed no differences in cholesterol, blood pressure, smoking, or BMI between TT and CC groups.
- **Regression dilution correction:** Prospective studies used single homocysteine measurements; the authors corrected for within-person variability using a published correction factor of 1.33 (based on 5-year average interval between blood collection and events).
- **Random effects model** used for all summary odds ratios to allow for heterogeneity across studies.
- **Heterogeneity** was significant in MTHFR studies for IHD and stroke, which the authors attribute to variation in dietary folate intake across populations. This is consistent with gene-environment interaction, not a flaw in the meta-analysis.
- **Publication bias:** Regression asymmetry test was non-significant for both IHD (P=0.55) and DVT (P=0.43) studies.

## Limitations

1. **Mendelian randomization caveats:** The authors acknowledge the possibility of genetic linkage -- a gene near MTHFR controlling an unknown CVD risk factor -- but note no such linkage is known.
2. **Stroke MTHFR data sparse:** Only 7 MTHFR studies for stroke (1,217 cases), yielding a very wide confidence interval (0.66-4.13 for the 5 umol/l increase).
3. **No prospective DVT data:** The DVT analysis relies entirely on MTHFR genetic studies.
4. **Ecological rather than individual-level:** The MTHFR analysis compares genotype frequencies in cases vs controls rather than tracking individuals over time.
5. **Post-2002 context:** This paper predates the large B-vitamin intervention trials (HOPE-2, NORVIT, VITATOPS) that largely failed to confirm CVD benefit from homocysteine lowering. The causal inference from this meta-analysis has been challenged by subsequent RCT evidence, suggesting the association may be partly confounded or that homocysteine is a marker rather than a modifiable causal pathway. This is a critical caveat for any synthesis.

## Relevance to TMG/Betaine Research

**Direct relevance:** This paper establishes the causal framework linking homocysteine to CVD. Since betaine (TMG) is a primary methyl donor in the alternative homocysteine remethylation pathway (via betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase, BHMT), any evidence that lowering homocysteine reduces CVD risk directly supports the rationale for TMG supplementation.

**Indirect relevance:** The paper focuses on folic acid as the homocysteine-lowering agent (via the folate-dependent remethylation pathway through MTHFR/MTR). It does not discuss betaine or the BHMT pathway. However, the quantitative relationship it establishes -- homocysteine reduction of 3 umol/l yields clinically meaningful risk reduction -- provides a benchmark against which TMG's homocysteine-lowering effects can be evaluated.

**Critical caveat:** Subsequent large RCTs of B-vitamin supplementation (post-2002) have generally failed to demonstrate CVD benefit despite successful homocysteine lowering. This undermines the causal claim and, by extension, the rationale for any homocysteine-lowering intervention including TMG. Any synthesis must address this tension.

## Claims to Verify

- The combined IHD odds ratio of 1.33 (1.22-1.46) is derived by combining the MTHFR estimate of 1.42 and prospective estimate of 1.32. The method of combination is not explicitly described in the text but appears to be a fixed-effect average. This is consistent across the paper (Table 2, lines 1342-1365).
- The small RCT cited (Schnyder et al., NEJM 2001) with 13/102 vs 23/94 events (P=0.04) is a secondary citation, not a primary finding. Verify this claim against the original trial.

## Relevance by Research Question

- **Q1 (Dietary intake):** Not directly addressed. The paper discusses folate intake modulating MTHFR effects but not dietary betaine.
- **Q2 (Clinical endpoints):** **Strongly relevant.** Provides the primary quantitative framework linking homocysteine to IHD, DVT, and stroke risk.
- **Q3 (Methylation cofactors/MTHFR):** **Strongly relevant.** Central to the paper's Mendelian randomization approach. Detailed genotype-stratified homocysteine data provided for 33 studies (Table 1).
- **Q4 (Tradeoffs vs alternatives):** Moderately relevant. Folic acid is the comparator intervention (0.8 mg/day for 3 umol/l reduction), providing a benchmark for TMG.
- **Q5 (Adversarial/contradicting evidence):** **Strongly relevant.** This paper represents the strongest pro-causality evidence, but post-2002 RCT failures create a critical adversarial tension that must be addressed.
- **Q6 (Dose-response/safety):** Moderately relevant. Establishes a dose-response relationship (5 umol/l increment) but for homocysteine itself, not for TMG dose. No safety data.
- **Q7 (Animal/mechanistic longevity):** Not addressed. Human epidemiological study only.
