Visual Abstract
. Vitamin D Deficiency Increases Mortality Risk in the UK Biobank.
Low vitamin D levels have been associated with increased mortality, but randomized trials in participants with severe vitamin D deficiencies are lacking. This study used nonlinear Mendelian randomization to assess genetic evidence for the role of low vitamin D status in mortality.
Abstract
Background:
Low vitamin D status is associated with increased mortality, but randomized trials on severely deficient participants are lacking.
Objective:
To assess genetic evidence for the causal role of low vitamin D status in mortality.
Design:
Nonlinear Mendelian randomization analyses.
Setting:
UK Biobank, a large-scale, prospective cohort from England, Scotland, and Wales with participants recruited between March 2006 and July 2010.
Participants:
307 601 unrelated UK Biobank participants of White European ancestry (aged 37 to 73 years at recruitment) with available measurements of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-(OH)D) and genetic data.
Measurements:
Genetically predicted 25-(OH)D was estimated using 35 confirmed variants of 25-(OH)D. All-cause and cause-specific mortality (cardiovascular disease [CVD], cancer, and respiratory) were recorded up to June 2020.
Results:
There were 18 700 deaths during the 14 years of follow-up. The association of genetically predicted 25-(OH)D with all-cause mortality was L-shaped (
P
for nonlinearity < 0.001), and risk for death decreased steeply with increasing concentrations until 50 nmol/L. Evidence for an association was also seen in analyses of mortality from cancer, CVD, and respiratory diseases (
P
≤ 0.033 for all outcomes). Odds of all-cause mortality in the genetic analysis were estimated to increase by 25% (odds ratio, 1.25 [95% CI, 1.16 to 1.35]) for participants with a measured 25-(OH)D concentration of 25 nmol/L compared with 50 nmol/L.
Limitations:
Analyses were restricted to a White European population. A genetic approach is best suited to providing proof of principle on causality, whereas the strength of the association is approximate.
Conclusion:
Our study supports a causal relationship between vitamin D deficiency and mortality. Additional research needs to identify strategies that meet the National Academy of Medicine's guideline of greater than 50 nmol/L and that reduce the premature risk for death associated with low vitamin D levels.

Comment on "Vitamin D Deficiency Increases Mortality Risk in the UK Biobank"

Vitamin D deficiency is known to be one indicator of untreated sleep apnea, which has many noninfectious life-threatening comorbid consequences. It would have been helpful if this paper provided a summary of the cause of death in this subset of the UK biobank so that the potential link with sleep apnea could start to be examined.

Editor's Note

The Editors of Annals were alerted by a reader questioning the validity of the statistical methods used in an article we published on Oct 25, 2022 by Sutherland and colleagues, “Vitamin D Deficiency Increases Mortality Risk in the UK Biobank: A Nonlinear Mendelian Randomization Study”.(1) Namely, authors used the residual method which was a commonly used method in Mendelian randomization studies at the time of publication; however, this method makes strong assumptions about the data that may not be met in practice.(2) A newer method, the doubly-ranked method, was published after the publication of the Annals study and is believed to be a superior and more valid approach to the residual method in some instances.(3) Authors of the Annals study (1) have now reanalyzed their data and present their findings in the following letter to the Editor, viewable at:
https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/ANNALS-24-02796
.

Authors' Response to Editor Note

Editors requested that we address a reader’s concern regarding the approach (1) used to analyze our data in our recent study published in Annals, “Vitamin D Deficiency Increases Mortality Risk in the UK Biobank: A Nonlinear Mendelian Randomization Study” (2). Using both linear and the residual method for non-linear Mendelian randomization [MR] (1) to explore the association between genetically predicted higher 25(OH)D and mortality in the UK Biobank data, our original study reported a linear association for respiratory disease related mortality, and non-linear associations for all-cause, cardiovascular disease and cancer mortality (2).

Since the publication of our study in Annals, others have published findings suggesting the residual method is unreliable if the genetic influences on the instrumented exposure vary in the population (3,4). In this instance, a newer method (published after our Annals paper), the doubly-ranked non-linear MR approach (4), might be more appropriate since it has been shown to be less sensitive to the violation of the constant genetic effect assumption compared to the residual method. As there is now evidence that the genetic effects on 25(OH)D concentrations can vary in the population in a way that would not have been detected when stratification was based on the residuals (the approach we used to verify whether our data met the non-heterogeneity assumption) (3), this raises a concern about bias in our initial findings through a violation of the constant genetic effect assumption.

We have now conducted a re-analysis using the doubly-ranked non-linear MR method. Defining the population, instrument, outcomes, and covariate adjustment as previously described, we re-ran the non-linear MR analyses using the doubly-ranked stratification method. Figure 1 (viewable with published letter,
here
) confirms heterogeneity in the genetic association with 25(OH)D across the strata which justifies the use of the doubly-ranked method(3,4). Figure 2 (viewable with published letter,
here
) shows that this re-analysis no longer provides evidence for a non-linear association between 25(OH)D and all-cause, CVD or cancer mortality, and we confirmed that this finding is not sensitive to the choice of the number of strata. This re-analysis does not affect the observed inverse linear association between 25(OH)D and respiratory disease mortality, which is supported by four of the five methods used (p<0.05 for IVW MR, weighted median MR, weighted mode MR and MR Presso; p=0.17 for MR Eggr).

We have also performed a power analysis to explore our ability to discount the association observed in our original analyses using the residual method (2). Our original analyses on all-cause mortality suggested that the non-linearity was stemming from a 21% mortality reduction (OR 0.79, 95%CI 0.70 to 0.89) per 1 SD higher 25(OH)D, occurring in the <25 nmol/L category. Using the doubly-ranked method, our power to detect this associating in strata where >90% of the participants have levels < 25nmol/l would be 43% (power under 95% CIs 14% to 77%). Therefore, while the analyses using the doubly-ranked method did not provide evidence for an association of 25(OH)D with all cause, cancer or CVD mortality, the data is not sufficient to discount an effect that would be restricted to the correction of severe deficiency.

In summary, re-analysis using the doubly-ranked method shows that for all-cause, CVD or cancer mortality, genetic evidence for an association with 25(OH)D was not observed in either linear or non-linear analyses. While a beneficial effect arising from correction of severe vitamin D deficiency is biologically plausible and compatible with some evidence from clinical trials (5), MR studies conducted to date are underpowered and have failed to provide robust evidence for underlying causality with respect to mortality risk.
