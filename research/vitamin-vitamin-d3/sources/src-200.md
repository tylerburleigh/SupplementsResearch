Abstract Meta-analysis is a vital component in clinical decision making, but previous work found binary event meta-analytic10
results can be fragile, affected by only a small number of patients in speciﬁc trials. Meta-analyses can also miss literature, and a11
method for estimating how much additional unseen data would ﬂip results would be a useful tool. This works establishes a12
complementary and generalisable deﬁnition of meta-analytic fragility, based on Ellipse of Insigniﬁcance (EOI) and Region of13
Attainable Redaction (ROAR) methods originally developed for dichotomous outcome trials. This method does not require14
trial-speciﬁc alterations to estimate fragility and yields a general method to estimate robustness of a meta-analysis to data15
redaction or addition of hypothetical trial outcomes. This method is applied to 3 meta-analyses with conﬂicting ﬁndings on the16
association of vitamin D supplementation and cancer mortality. A full meta-analysis of all trials cited in the 3 meta-analyses17
yielded no association between vitamin D supplementation and cancer mortality. Using the method outlined here, it was18
determined that meta-analytic fragility was high in all cases, with recoding of just 5 patients in the full cohort of 133,262 patients19
was enough to cross the signiﬁcance threshold. Small amounts of redacted or non-included data also had substantial impact on20
each meta-analysis, with addition of just 3 hypothetical patients to an ostensibly signiﬁcant meta-analyses (N = 38,538) enough21
to yield a null result. This method for analytical fragility is complementary to previous investigations that suggested22
meta-analyses are frequently fragile. It further shows that merely increasing the sample size is not an assurance against fragility.23
Caution should be advised when interpreting the results of meta-analyses and conﬂicting results may stem from inherent24
fragility and should be carefully employed.25

Introduction27
Meta-analyses are critical for synthesising medical evidence and providing robust estimates of treatment effects ( Murad et al.,28
2014; Dechartres et al., 2013; Nordmann et al., 2012). However, there are concerns that meta-analyses are frequently improperly29
conducted, and in recent years there has been methodological concerns over the rigour of an increasing number of these publi-30
cations (Lawrence et al., 2021 ; Siemens et al., 2021; Hameed et al., 2020), particularly when constituent Randomized Controlled31
Trials (RCTs) have data integrity issues which meta-analysis cannot solve (Wilkinson et al., 2024, 2025). There is an additional32
consideration that deserves mention in dichotomous outcome or binary outcome trials, which is the issue of fragility. In RCTs,33
many trial results are fragile, with recoding of a small number of events to non-events or vice versa in either the experimental or34
control arm often suﬃcient to convert a seemingly statistically signiﬁcant result to a null, and vice versa ( Grimes, 2022; Tignanelli35
and Napolitano, 2019 ; Baer et al., 2021a ,b).36
37
While this issue has generated much recent discussion in RCTs, this has not received much consideration for meta-analyses,38
which are often thought of as more robust to such manipulations. This however may not be the case. Previously, Atal et al (Atal39

fragility useful but not generalisable, and less intuitive to interpret than a typical RCT fragility metric.47
48
In this work, we establish a generalizable meta-analytic fragility metric, based upon Ellipse of Insigniﬁcance (EOI) analysis for49
dichotomous outcome trials (Grimes, 2022). EOI is a reﬁned and analytical fragility metric that simultaneously ﬁnds the degree50
of fragility in both control and experimental arms of dichotomous outcome trials, deriving its name from the geometrical real-51
ity relating to chi squared tests that for any seemingly signiﬁcant result mapped on a plane where one axis corresponds to the52
experimental arm and the perpendicular the control, there is an associated ellipse inside which all results are null. EOI analysis an-53
alytically ﬁnds and resolves the minimum displacement (Fewest Experimental/Control Knowingly Uncoded Participants (FECKUP)54
vector) from this region, reporting fragility as proportion of group sizes in context. As it is analytical and based upon chi square55
testing, it handles samples of all size, reporting a readily interpreted contextualised measure.56
57
Intimately related to this is Region of Attainable Redaction (ROAR) analysis ( Grimes, 2024b), a methodology for ascertaining po-58
tential inﬂuence of redacted data on reported results. There are many reasons why relevant data might be missing from an59
analysis, ranging from simple absent data, missed studies, inappropriate cut-offs and even deliberate cherry-picking (Grimes and60
Heathers, 2021). This is highly relevant to meta-analysis, and it would be useful to have a method to quantify the potential impact61
of redaction on reported results and gives an additional estimate of how robust reported results may be when new data is ac-62
crued. Accordingly in this work, we will extend these methodologies to handle meta-analysis, contrast them to Atal et al’s metric,63
and apply them to conﬂicting Vitamin D trials to illustrate their epidemiological utility. This package of meta-analytic fragility tools64
we name EOIMETA for brevity.65

Application to vitamin D studies94
Results of vitamin D supplementation and cancer mortality have been markedly inconsistent. In recent years, several meta-95
analyses of published trials yielded markedly conﬂicting results. A review and meta-analysis by Zhang et al (Zhang et al., 2019 )96
and correction (Zhang et al., 2020 ) of 5 RCTs involving 39,168 patients found a signiﬁcant association between vitamin D supple-97
mentation and reduced cancer mortality, reporting a 15% decrease in the risk of cancer death with vitamin D supplementation.98
Similarly, a meta-analysis by Gou et al ( Guo et al., 2023 ) of 11 RCTs involving 112,165 patients found a signiﬁcant reduction in99
relative risk of cancer death with supplementation of 12%. By contrast, a subsequent study of 6 RCTS involving 61,853 patients100
(Zhang et al., 2022 ) found no signiﬁcant reduction in cancer death risk with vitamin D supplementation. All 3 meta-analyses in-101
volved combinations drawn from just 12 trials.102
103
To investigate this apparent case of meta-analytic fragility, we extracted data from all 12 studies included in the 3 meta-analyses.104
A small inconsistency between all 3 meta-analysis was found in relation to one RCT (Scragg et al., 2018 ); In Zhang et al, the rate105
of cancer deaths in the supplementation and control group were reported as 44/2558 and 45/2550 respectively, versus being106
reported as 28/2558 versus 30/2558 in Gou et al and 30/2550 versus 30/2550 in Zheng et al. Disagreement between these ﬁgures107
appears to stem from cancers detected in both groups prior to the randomisation process. Accordingly, the adjusted ITT ﬁgure108
used in this work is 30/2544 versus 30/2535 respectively for Scragg et al’s work. A further nuance is that for another small RCT109
(Ammann et al., 2017), direct data was not given on the number of cases. This however can be estimated from minimal assump-110
tion through the hazard ratio. For all included studies, relative risk and 95% conﬁdence intervals were calculated explicitly except111
for Lehouck et al (Lehouck et al., 2012) where zero recorded deaths in the supplementation sample required conﬁdence intervals112
to be estimated by bootstrap methods.Initially, we ran fragility analysis on all of these meta-analysis, with the new metric outlined113
here as well as Atal et al’s algorithm. After this, we combined data and performed a meta-analysis on all 12 studies to ascertain114
potential effects of vitamin D and cancer mortality. A full fragility and robustness analysis was then performed on this, and results115
discussed to elucidate ﬁndings.116

Discussion149
The meta-analytic fragility tool presented here, EOIMETA, is generalisable and ﬂexible, complementing Atal et al’s study and pa-150
tient speciﬁc method. The nuance between both approaches is that in Atal et al’s algorithm identiﬁes a speciﬁc combination of151
patients from speciﬁc studies that may be moved to alter results, whereas the current work establishes a method for pooling152
meta-analysis on ascertaining their group fragility and the potential impact of missing data. In this respect, they answer comple-153
mentary and subtlety different questions speciﬁc to meta-analyses.154
155
In RCT literature, a frequent objection to the use of fragility analysis methods is that the mere existence of a small FI might156
be an artefact of trial design. For clinical trials especially, a well-designed trial should be structured as to minimize patient ex-157
posure to unknown harms, striving to ensure that just enough participate to allow the detection of clinically relevant effects. He158
argument that RCTs at least might be fragile ‘by design’ is however convincingly countered by other authors, who ﬁnd no evidence159
of p-value distributions clustering around signiﬁcance thresholds after a sample size calculation and ﬁnd additional that fragility160
in well-designed studies is not always low. More recent robust methods for fragility analysis like EOI and for redaction like ROAR161
also have application far beyond ostensibly fragile-by-design RCTs, but for cohort studies, preclinical work, and even ecological162
studies. The fragile-by-design argument is also a weaker on in the context of meta-analysis, where the marshalling of many stud-163
ies to increase effect precision measurements should strengthen the evidence but results here and previously suggest that even164
meta-analysis of large groups of patients can be profoundly fragile.165
166
There are a few subtle issues and limitations to consider additionally; Atal et al’s meta-analytic fragility metric in principle iden-167
tiﬁes the precise combination of inter-study and patient recoding required to ﬂip a result, a “worse-case” scenario, identifying168
the most fragile point in a meta-analyses. This usually results in the Atal et al fragility metric being typically smaller than the169
complement EOI fragility metric outlined here, but this is not always the case. Because Atal et al’s method is a greedy algorithm,170
it is time-consuming to run on large collections of studies and can sometimes miss optimal and smaller solutions. Secondly, the171
adjustment of the events and non-events in the experimental group due to weighed inverse variance methods in the EOI fragility172
method subtly alters group composition, resulting in a smaller EOI fragility than a crude pooled estimate. What is evident from the173
examples shown in this work is that even in large cohorts with relatively low heterogeneity, the impact of recoding only miniscule174
numbers of patients can drastically alter interpretation; in the 12-study meta-analysis, recoding a mere 5 patients in 133,262 was175
suﬃcient to alter the result from a null to a positive ﬁnding.176
177
While the method outlined here is analytic and rapid, it has some limitations that must be considered. Like Atal’s method, it178
applies only to meta-analyses of trials with dichotomous outcomes and is not suitable for continuous outcomes. It is also ap-179
propriate only in contexts where internal confounding is low, and it may not be reliable in the presence of high between-study180
heterogeneity. Accordingly, careful application is required. At its most essential, this implementation reﬂects the meta-analytic181
relative risk by generating a synthetic pooled 2 ×2 table that preserves the total sample size in each arm. This can be interpreted182
as the best single-study approximation of all studies under ﬁxed-effect assumptions. The resulting meta-analytic fragility metric183
is useful for modeling perturbations in results, such as the addition of patients (who were previously redacted) or the recoding184
of events and non-events. The advantage of this approach lies in its tractability, offering a simpler alternative to working with185
multiple studies individually and a deterministic and analytic resultant fragility metric. However, it is important to emphasize that186
it does not correspond to true patient-level pooling or standardisation.187
188
The use of vitamin D meta-analyses in this work was chosen as illustrative rather than speciﬁc, but it is worth noting that there189
are methodological concerns with much Vitamin D research ( Grimes et al., 2024). This goes beyond the scope of the current190
work, but serves as an example of the reality that meta-analysis is only as strong as its underlying data, and the conclusions191
drawn from them must always be seen in context. While meta-analysis is a powerful method for reﬁning effect size estimates, it192
cannot overcome poorly conducted research or bad data ( Jané et al., 2025 ), nor it is intrinsically robust. These limitations should193
be kept in mind when considering meta-analytic results, and scientists should consider them doubly when opting to perform a194
meta-analysis. Mass produced and unreliable meta-analyses are a recognised and growing problem ( Ioannidis, 2016) , and we195
need be mindful not to add to increasingly issue of research waste and unreliable research ( Glasziou and Chalmers, 2018; Grimes,196
2024a).197
