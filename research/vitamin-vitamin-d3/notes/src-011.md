# Source src-011: Borsche et al. (2021) - COVID-19 Mortality Risk and Vitamin D3 Status

**Citation:** Borsche L, Glauner B, von Mendel J. (2021). "COVID-19 Mortality Risk Correlates Inversely with Vitamin D3 Status, and a Mortality Rate Close to Zero Could Theoretically Be Achieved at 50 ng/mL 25(OH)D3: Results of a Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis." *Nutrients*, 13(10):3596. doi: 10.3390/nu13103596. PMCID: PMC8541492.

**Quality assessment:** Substantive full-text paper (systematic review + meta-analysis). Peer-reviewed in MDPI Nutrients. Quality is adequate. The paper is an advocacy-leaning review with strong conclusions drawn from observational/correlational data.

---

## Core Findings

1. A meta-analysis combining one population study (19 European countries, ~449 million people) and seven clinical studies (1,601 hospitalized COVID-19 patients) found a significant negative correlation between vitamin D3 blood levels and COVID-19 mortality: r(32) = -0.3989, p = 0.0194.

2. OLS regression of the combined data intersected the zero-mortality axis at approximately 50.7 ng/mL 25(OH)D3, which the authors propose as a potential optimal blood level.

3. The authors argue that current official thresholds (WHO: 30 ng/mL; D-A-CH: 20 ng/mL) are far too low for optimal immune function and were set historically only to prevent rickets.

## Key Evidence and Data

### Study Design and Sample
- Systematic literature search of PubMed and c19vitamind.com registry (Nov 2019 - Mar 2021)
- Started with 563 + 66 records; after screening, 8 eligible studies remained (PRISMA flowchart)
- Population study: Ahmad et al., 19 European countries, ~449M population
- Clinical studies: 7 studies totaling 1,601 patients with D3 measured pre-infection or within 1 day of hospital admission
- Median (IQR) vitamin D across all cohorts: 23.2 ng/mL (17.4-26.8)

### Correlation Results
| Dataset | Pearson r | p-value |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Population study (Ahmad, 19 countries) | r(17) = -0.4154 | p = 0.0770 |
| Hospital studies (corrected) | r(13) = -0.4886 | p = 0.0646 |
| Combined | r(32) = -0.3989 | **p = 0.0194** |

### Regression Results (Combined)
- Intercept: 140.29
- Coefficient: -2.7654
- R-squared: 0.159 (Adj. R-squared: 0.133)
- Zero-mortality threshold: ~50.7 ng/mL

### Correction Applied to Clinical Data
- Clinical mortality rates were corrected for age, sex, and diabetes using an Economist ML model
- Population study CMRs were NOT corrected (corrections were attempted but not used to avoid hidden variables)

## Methodology

- **Design:** Systematic review with meta-analysis using correlation and linear regression (not a traditional effect-size meta-analysis)
- **Data sources:** PubMed + c19vitamind.com registry
- **Analysis:** Pearson and Spearman correlations, OLS linear regression using Python (scipy.stats, statsmodels)
- **Inclusion:** Studies reporting D3 blood levels pre-infection or on day of hospital admission, with mortality data by D3 cohort
- **Exclusions:** 4 meta-studies, 1 retracted study, 3 studies with skewed/nonrepresentative patient characteristics, 4 studies with missing cohort-level data

### Methodological Limitations
1. R-squared values are very low (0.159 combined), meaning vitamin D alone explains only ~16% of mortality variance
2. Each dataset individually showed only marginally significant correlations (p = 0.077 and p = 0.065); significance only achieved in combined analysis
3. Clinical data corrected using a third-party ML model (The Economist's COVID mortality risk estimator) rather than individual patient data
4. Population study used country-level aggregate D3 data (ecological fallacy risk)
5. Heterogeneous D3 measurement timing across studies
6. Very few data points exist above 35 ng/mL, making the 50 ng/mL threshold an extrapolation
7. No randomized controlled trials included -- all data observational
8. The regression is linear; the true relationship may be nonlinear

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q2 (Immune function):** Provides mechanistic discussion of vitamin D3's role in innate and adaptive immunity, ACE2/RAS modulation, cathelicidin production, and cytokine storm suppression. These are review-level claims supported by cited literature.
- **Q3 (Optimal levels and doses):** Directly addresses optimal blood levels, proposing ~50 ng/mL (125 nmol/L) as optimal for immune protection. Argues that 200-800 IU/day is insufficient and 4,000-10,000 IU/day is needed. Discusses historical reasons for low official thresholds. Notes major discrepancy between WHO (30 ng/mL), D-A-CH (20 ng/mL), and the authors' recommended 40-60 ng/mL.
- **Q4 (Baseline deficiency):** The correlation between D3 levels and mortality is central to the paper. Lower baseline D3 was associated with higher COVID-19 mortality.
- **Q6 (Expert disagreement):** Discusses ongoing debate about causality (whether low D3 causes poor outcomes or is merely a biomarker). Notes that official recommendations remain at historically low thresholds.
- **Q7 (Evidence challenging popular narrative):** The authors themselves acknowledge the absence of placebo-controlled, double-blinded studies. The low R-squared value and marginal individual-dataset significance qualify the strength of their own conclusions.

## Claims to Verify

1. **"4000 to 10,000 units needed to generate optimal blood level"** (line ~308): This is stated without direct citation to a dose-response study; it appears to be derived from multiple cited sources. The specific dose range should be cross-checked against primary dose-response literature.
2. **"200 mcg/mL vitamin K2"** (line ~308): Units appear inconsistent (mcg/mL vs. daily dose). This may be a typo -- likely means 200 mcg/day.
3. **"Hunter-gatherer levels of 110-125 nmol/L"** (line ~1099): Single citation [174]; worth verifying against the primary source.
4. **"Vitamin D influences more than 2500 genes"** (line ~204): Single citation [39]; this is a commonly cited figure but exact number varies by source.

## Notes for Synthesis

- This is a COVID-19-specific study, not a general vitamin D supplementation paper. Its findings on optimal D3 levels and immune function are relevant but should be contextualized within the broader literature.
- The authors are independent researchers (not affiliated with medical institutions), which is neither positive nor negative but worth noting.
- The paper is more advocacy-oriented than typical systematic reviews, with strong policy recommendations and philosophical statements.
- The c19vitamind.com registry used as a search source is a curated collection that may have selection bias toward positive findings.
