# src-128: ViDA Trial - Vitamin D3 and Cardiovascular Disease

**Citation:** Scragg R, Stewart AW, Waayer D, et al. "Effect of Monthly High-Dose Vitamin D Supplementation on Cardiovascular Disease in the Vitamin D Assessment Study: A Randomized Clinical Trial." JAMA Cardiol. 2017;2(6):608-616. doi:10.1001/jamacardio.2017.0175

**Source:** PMC5815022 | PMID: 28384800

**Quality assessment:** Full-text peer-reviewed RCT. Substantive content with complete methods, results, and discussion. Metadata had empty fields but content matches the expected paper.

## Core Findings

The Vitamin D Assessment (ViDA) trial found that monthly high-dose vitamin D3 supplementation (initial 200,000 IU bolus, then 100,000 IU/month for median 3.3 years) did not prevent cardiovascular disease in a general population of 5,108 adults aged 50-84 years in Auckland, New Zealand. The primary outcome of CVD occurred in 11.8% of the vitamin D group vs 11.5% of the placebo group (adjusted HR 1.02, 95% CI 0.87-1.20). No secondary CVD outcomes showed significant benefit.

## Key Evidence

### Primary outcome (CVD composite, ICD-10 I10-I82)
- Vitamin D: 303/2558 (11.8%) vs Placebo: 293/2550 (11.5%)
- HR 1.02 (95% CI 0.87-1.20), P = .81

### Deficiency subgroup (deseasonalized 25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL)
- Vitamin D: 80/612 (13.1%) vs Placebo: 88/658 (13.4%)
- HR 1.00 (95% CI 0.74-1.35), P = .99
- **No benefit even in deficient participants**

### Secondary outcomes (all null)
| Outcome | HR (95% CI) | P |
|---------|-------------|---|
| Myocardial infarction | 0.90 (0.54-1.50) | .68 |
| Angina | 1.43 (0.90-2.26) | .13 |
| Heart failure | 1.19 (0.84-1.68) | .34 |
| Stroke | 0.95 (0.55-1.62) | .84 |
| Hypertension | 0.84 (0.53-1.33) | .45 |
| Arrhythmias | 0.93 (0.62-1.39) | .71 |
| Venous thrombosis | 0.74 (0.34-1.61) | .45 |

### 25(OH)D levels confirming dose adequacy
- Treatment group achieved mean 25(OH)D > 50 ng/mL at follow-up
- Placebo group remained at ~26 ng/mL
- Difference: >20 ng/mL sustained throughout the trial
- Baseline mean: 25.3 ng/mL (observed), 26.5 ng/mL (deseasonalized)
- 24.9% were vitamin D deficient at baseline (deseasonalized 25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL)

## Methodology

- **Design:** Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
- **Population:** 5,108 community-dwelling adults aged 50-84, Auckland, NZ
- **Recruitment:** 47,905 invited from family practices; 5,110 randomized; 5,108 analyzed (2 withdrew consent)
- **Demographics:** Mean age 65.9; 58.1% male; 83.3% European/other ethnicity; mean BMI 28.5
- **Intervention:** Vitamin D3 200,000 IU initial bolus, then 100,000 IU/month (oral soft-gel capsules, Tishcon Corporation)
- **Duration:** Median 3.3 years (range 2.5-4.2)
- **Adherence:** 83.9% overall (84.9% vitamin D, 82.9% placebo by self-report); confirmed by blood levels in 10% random sample
- **Retention:** 87% actively participating at final follow-up
- **Outcomes tracked via:** National Health Index linking to hospital discharges, mortality records, and prescription data (New Zealand Ministry of Health)
- **Analysis:** ITT, Cox proportional hazards with robust sandwich variance, adjusted for age/sex/ethnicity
- **Power:** 75% power to detect 20% reduction (reduced from planned 80% due to lower-than-expected event rate); equivalent to 80% power for 21% reduction

## Limitations

1. **Monthly bolus dosing** -- Authors acknowledge this may be less effective than daily/weekly dosing; vitamin D has short half-life in circulation and may only be present for days after each monthly dose. This is a key methodological caveat.
2. **Underpowered for subgroup and secondary outcomes** -- 596 events observed vs 890 expected; limited power for deficiency subgroup and individual CVD endpoints.
3. **Short follow-up** -- Median 3.3 years; cannot exclude longer-term effects.
4. **Outcome adjudication** -- Outcomes were not independently adjudicated; relied on hospital discharge coding (ICD-10).
5. **Generalizability** -- Auckland, NZ population; predominantly European ethnicity.
6. **No emigration tracking** -- Could not identify participants who left the country.

## Relevance to Research Questions

- **Q2 (CVD evidence):** Directly relevant -- large RCT showing no CVD benefit from vitamin D3 supplementation. One of the key negative trials in this domain.
- **Q4 (Baseline deficiency):** Highly relevant -- prespecified subgroup analysis in 1,270 deficient participants showed no benefit (HR 1.00). This is important because it addresses whether deficiency modifies the treatment effect (it does not).
- **Q6 (Expert disagreement):** Relevant -- the authors caution against closing the topic entirely, citing the dosing frequency issue and observational epidemiology on sun exposure and CVD.
- **Q7 (Evidence challenging supplementation narrative):** Highly relevant -- this is a landmark negative trial. Despite achieving high 25(OH)D levels and good adherence, no CVD benefit was observed. The authors suggest observational associations may be confounded by lifestyle factors or that 25(OH)D may be a surrogate for sun exposure.

## Claims to Verify

- **N=5,108 analyzed:** Confirmed. 5,110 randomized; 2 withdrew consent (both placebo), leaving 5,108 for ITT analysis. This is internally consistent across the text.
- **24.9% baseline deficiency:** Confirmed. 1,270/5,108 = 24.9% with deseasonalized 25(OH)D < 20 ng/mL. Vitamin D group: 612/2,558 (23.9%); Placebo: 658/2,550 (25.8%).
- **83.9% adherence:** This is self-reported; biochemical confirmation only in a 10% random sample (438/515 = 85.0% participation rate). Self-reported adherence may overestimate actual intake, but the biochemical data (20+ ng/mL difference) supports adequate dosing.
