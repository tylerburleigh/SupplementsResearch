# Source src-004: Nicotine Treatment of Mild Cognitive Impairment

**Citation:** Newhouse P, Kellar K, Aisen P, et al. "Nicotine treatment of mild cognitive impairment: A 6-month double-blind pilot clinical trial." *Neurology*. 2012;78(2):91-101. doi:10.1212/WNL.0b013e31823efcbb. PMCID: PMC3466669.

## Study Design

- **Type:** Double-blind, parallel-group, placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial (Class I evidence)
- **Duration:** 6 months double-blind (followed by optional 6-month open-label, reported separately)
- **Sites:** 3 academic medical centers (U Vermont, UCSD, Duke)
- **N:** 74 randomized (39 nicotine, 35 placebo); 67 completed (34 nicotine, 33 placebo)
- **Registered:** Clinicaltrials.gov NCT00091468

## Population

- Nonsmoking subjects (never-smokers and former smokers) aged 55+ with **amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI)**
- Mean age: ~76 years; 45 male, 29 female
- MMSE 24-30; CDR 0.5 with memory box score 0.5 or 1.0
- Excluded: significant medical/neurologic disease, psychiatric illness, substance abuse, current tobacco/nicotine use, cognitive-enhancing medications
- APOE genotype: 30/70 carried at least one epsilon-4 allele

## Doses Used

- Transdermal nicotine (Nicotrol, Pfizer): started at 5 mg/day, titrated to **15 mg/day** by day 21
- 73/74 subjects received the full 15 mg/day target dose
- Delivered over 16 hours via patch
- **Note:** 15 mg/day exceeds the <5 mg/day threshold in the research brief. This is a moderate-to-high dose. Findings are relevant but not directly generalizable to low-dose protocols.

## Key Findings

### Cognitive Effects (Primary and Secondary)

1. **Sustained attention (CPT):** Significant nicotine-induced improvement in hit RT standard error over interstimulus interval (F(1,57)=4.89, p=0.031 ITT; F(1,54)=14.96, p=0.0003 completers). Effect size = 0.78 (Cohen d) at week 26.
2. **Memory:** Significant improvement in delayed word recall accuracy (F(1,70)=5.92, p=0.018). Reduced information loss between immediate and delayed paragraph recall (F(1,60)=4.42, p=0.04).
3. **Attention/psychomotor speed:** Improved choice reaction time accuracy (F(1,66)=4.44, p=0.04). Trends for speed of memory (p=0.06), RT variability (p=0.07), continuity of attention (p=0.09).
4. **No significant effect on clinical global impression** (CGIC, p=0.13), though numerically more nicotine subjects rated as improved (23.5% vs 9.1%).
5. **APOE4 interaction:** Double APOE4 carriers showed significantly greater cognitive benefit from nicotine on power of attention (p=0.019) and digit vigilance speed (p=0.01).

### Safety and Side Effects

1. **Cardiovascular:** Significant reduction in systolic blood pressure (F(1,71)=9.01, p=0.004). Nicotine group: -4 mm Hg change vs placebo +9.6 mm Hg. No effect on diastolic BP, pulse, or temperature.
2. **Weight:** Modest weight loss (~2.5 kg by day 182) in nicotine group. No clinically low BMI developed.
3. **Adverse events:** More total AEs with nicotine (82 vs 52, p<0.05), primarily GI and neurologic, mostly mild. 4 nicotine subjects discontinued for AEs vs 0 placebo (p=0.05).
4. **Dependence/withdrawal:** No withdrawal symptoms reported. No subjects continued nicotine use post-study. No evidence for abuse liability in this nonsmoking population.

### Neuroprotection Discussion

- Authors cite evidence that nicotine may be neuroprotective and may have a role in amyloid processing (via alpha-7 nAChR/PI3K pathway).
- Counter-evidence noted: nicotine has been shown to exacerbate tau pathology in a rodent model (Deng et al., 2010).
- Suggests longer treatment periods (>1 year) may be needed to detect disease-modifying effects.

## Limitations

1. Relatively small sample (n=74) -- powered only for CPT cognitive measure, not clinical global ratings or progression outcomes.
2. Population was MCI patients, not healthy non-smokers -- cognitive enhancement may be specific to impaired populations (authors note nicotine "does not generally improve performance unless subjects are impaired").
3. **Dose (15 mg/day) is above the <5 mg/day threshold** of interest in the research brief.
4. Only nonsmokers tested; generalizability to former smokers or active smokers unknown.
5. No data on disease progression (study too short).
6. No active dose-finding; titration was for side effects only, not efficacy.

## Relevance to Brief Questions

- **Q1 (Cognitive effects):** STRONG relevance. Demonstrates significant improvements in attention (CPT), memory (delayed recall), and psychomotor speed in nonsmokers. However, population is MCI (impaired), not healthy. Dose is 15 mg/day, not low-dose. The principle that nicotinic stimulation improves cognition in nonsmokers is well-supported.
- **Q2 (Cardiovascular/metabolic):** MODERATE relevance. Shows reduced systolic BP and modest weight loss at 15 mg/day. Suggests cardiovascular safety over 6 months. But dose is above brief threshold.
- **Q3 (Longevity/neurodegeneration):** MODERATE relevance. Discusses neuroprotective potential of nicotine via alpha-7 nAChR pathway and potential role in amyloid processing. Notes counter-evidence on tau pathology. APOE4 finding suggests possible benefit for genetically at-risk individuals.
- **Q5 (Dependence/withdrawal):** STRONG relevance. No withdrawal symptoms, no continued nicotine use, no abuse liability in 39 nonsmokers over 6 months at 15 mg/day. If no dependence at 15 mg/day, lower doses should carry even less risk.
- **Q6 (Evidence against):** MODERATE relevance. Notes more AEs with nicotine, 4 discontinuations, potential tau pathology exacerbation from animal data. Weight loss may be concerning in some populations.

## Claims to Verify

- **Sample size (n=74):** Consistent across abstract, results, and methods sections. 100 screened, 74 randomized. Verified.
- **Effect size 0.78:** Stated in results section. Reported as Cohen d at week 26. No independent verification possible from raw data.
- **Weight loss ~2.5 kg:** Results section states -1.3 kg at day 91. Discussion states "approximately 2.5 kg by day 182." The day-182 figure is not explicitly in the results table, raising minor concern about precision.
- **No withdrawal symptoms:** Stated categorically in discussion. No systematic withdrawal assessment scale mentioned -- relies on subject/informant reports. Could underestimate mild withdrawal.
