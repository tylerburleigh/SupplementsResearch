# src-019: Rapid Effect of Nicotine Intake on Neuroplasticity in Non-Smoking Humans

**Citation:** Grundey J, Thirugnanasambandam N, Kaminsky K, et al. (2012). *Frontiers in Pharmacology*, 3:186. doi:10.3389/fphar.2012.00186

**Quality note:** Metadata flagged as "degraded" (paywall_page reason), but the full text is an open-access Frontiers article with complete methods, results, and discussion. Treated as authoritative.

## Study Design

- **Type:** Randomized, placebo-controlled, single-blind crossover experiment
- **N:** 48 healthy non-smokers (12 per protocol group; 50-58% female per group)
- **Population:** Caucasian, age 24-27 (group means), no neurological/psychiatric disease, no medication
- **Intervention:** Nicotine nasal spray (1 mg cumulative dose, Nicorette) vs. placebo spray
- **Plasma level achieved:** 8-9 ng/ml (comparable to 15 mg/16h nicotine patch)
- **Plasticity protocols:** tDCS (anodal/cathodal) and PAS (PAS-10 inhibitory / PAS-25 excitatory)
- **Outcome:** Motor-evoked potentials (MEPs) via TMS at 0-120 min, plus next morning/evening for nicotine sessions
- **Cross-referenced study:** Thirugnanasambandam et al. (2011) used nicotine PATCH (15 mg/16h) with same protocols

## Key Findings

1. **Nicotine spray abolished focal facilitatory plasticity (PAS-25):** In placebo, PAS-25 enhanced MEPs for up to 90 min. Under nicotine spray, this enhancement was completely eliminated.

2. **Nicotine spray abolished focal inhibitory plasticity (PAS-10):** PAS-10-induced excitability diminution was completely prevented by nicotine spray.

3. **Nicotine spray abolished non-focal facilitatory plasticity (anodal tDCS):** Anodal tDCS-induced excitability enhancement was eliminated under nicotine.

4. **Nicotine spray delayed and weakened non-focal inhibitory plasticity (cathodal tDCS):** Cathodal tDCS effects were delayed (starting at 15 min vs. immediately) and shortened (lasting only 5 min), with a second brief window at 90-120 min.

5. **No direct effect on cortical excitability:** Nicotine spray alone did not change baseline motor cortex excitability.

6. **Critical pharmacokinetic difference:** The spray (rapid onset, peak at 5-10 min) produced fundamentally different effects than nicotine patch (slow onset, hours), despite achieving similar 8-9 ng/ml plasma levels. Patch focused and enhanced facilitatory plasticity; spray abolished it. Authors attribute this to rapid nicotinic receptor adaptation (desensitization, upregulation) occurring during prolonged patch exposure but not with acute spray.

7. **Heterogeneous cognitive literature acknowledged:** The paper reviews conflicting evidence on nicotine and cognition in non-smokers, noting some studies show improvement, others no effect, and some show decrements.

## Doses Used

- **Nicotine nasal spray:** 1 mg cumulative dose (10 mg/ml solution)
- **Plasma level:** 8-9 ng/ml (peak at 5-10 min)
- **Comparison reference:** Nicotine patch 15 mg/16h achieves similar plasma levels
- **Important:** Only a single dose was tested; dose-response effects not explored

## Population

- 48 healthy non-smokers
- Age range: 24-27 years (group means)
- Mixed gender (~50% female per group)
- Excluded: chronic/acute medical conditions, neurological/psychiatric history, medication use, pregnancy

## Relevance to Brief Questions

### Q1: Cognitive effects in healthy non-smokers
**Directly relevant but indirect measure.** The study does not test cognitive performance directly. It measures neuroplasticity (LTP/LTD-like mechanisms) which are the physiological substrate of learning and memory. The key finding is that acute nicotine disrupts the formation of both facilitatory and inhibitory plasticity in non-smokers. The authors note that connecting these neuroplasticity changes to cognition is "not easily drawn" given the heterogeneous cognitive literature. The mechanism proposed is calcium overshoot: nicotine increases intracellular calcium via nicotinic receptors, potentially exceeding the optimal range for LTP/LTD induction.

### Q2: Cardiovascular/metabolic health effects at <5 mg/day
**Not addressed.** No cardiovascular or metabolic measures were taken.

### Q3: Longevity pathways
**Not addressed.**

### Q4: Intermittent vs daily comparison
**Highly relevant.** The central finding is that pharmacokinetic profile matters critically: rapid-onset (spray, mimicking cigarette smoking) produces opposite effects to slow-onset (patch, mimicking sustained exposure). This has direct implications for intermittent vs. continuous dosing regimens. The authors argue that adaptive receptor changes (desensitization, upregulation) occurring during prolonged patch exposure reshape the neuroplastic response.

### Q5: Dependence/withdrawal risk
**Minimally addressed.** Side effects noted: 96% of non-smokers reported sneezing/coughing after nicotine spray (rapidly subsiding). The authors note that nicotine spray has "fast and similar effects on craving and withdrawal symptoms" as patch. Dizziness and throat irritation also mentioned.

### Q6: Evidence against low-dose nicotine
**Relevant as cautionary evidence.** Acute 1 mg nicotine abolished multiple forms of neuroplasticity in non-smokers. If neuroplasticity is the basis of learning and memory, acute nicotine could impair rather than enhance cognitive function. However, the authors emphasize this is a physiological measure, not a cognitive test.

### Q7: Expert disagreement
**Tangentially relevant.** The paper acknowledges heterogeneity in cognitive nicotine effects and proposes that differences in pharmacokinetics (delivery method, onset speed, duration) may explain conflicting results across studies.

## Claims to Verify

- **N = 48 subjects:** Stated in methods (line 123-124). Table 1 confirms 12 per group. However, results section mentions "44 of 46 subjects" for side effects (line 203), suggesting 2 subjects may have been excluded from side-effect reporting. Minor discrepancy worth noting.
- **Plasma level 8-9 ng/ml:** Not directly measured in this study. Cited from Tonnesen et al. (1991) and Pomerleau et al. (1992). Authors acknowledge this as a limitation (no blood levels obtained).
- **1 mg dose:** Clearly stated and consistent throughout the paper.

## Limitations

1. Single-blind design (experimenter knew condition)
2. No blood nicotine verification
3. Only single dose tested (no dose-response)
4. No direct cognitive testing (only neuroplasticity measures)
5. No next-day follow-up in placebo group
6. Proposed calcium-mediated mechanism is hypothetical
