1 INTRODUCTION
The National Alzheimer’s Project Act, signed into law in January 2011,
calls for an aggressive and coordinated national plan to accelerate
research on Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and Alzheimer’s disease-related
dementias (ADRD), and to provide better clinical care and services for
people living with dementia and their families. The first national plan
was created in 2012. The plan establishes five ambitious goals to both
prevent future cases of AD/ADRD and to better meet the needs of the
millions of American families currently facing dementia. The National
Institutes of Health (NIH) leads the research effort on the first goal,
to prevent and effectively treat AD/ADRD by 2025. T o help achieve
this goal, triennial AD, ADRD, and dementia care summits are held to
assess and refine research priorities in the national plan. Vascular con-
tributions to cognitive impairment and dementia (VCID), a term used
to describe the broad field of research considering the role of a range
of vascular risk and vascular lesions on cognition and used broadly
by the American Heart Association, 1 the Alzheimer’s Association, 2
and the NIH, 3 is defined as an ADRD, and thus research priorities
for VCID are set at the ADRD summits. The last ADRD summit held
prior to this workshop was the ADRD Summit 2016 (see T able 1,s u m -
mary of the AD and ADRD summits). T o evaluate the state of the
field in VCID research, refine and identify new priorities for VCID,
and foster collaborations, the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Insti-
tute (NHLBI) and the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and
Stroke (NINDS) convened a working group (WG) May 7 to 8, 2018. 4
The WG brought together a group of interdisciplinary experts and pro-
gram staff from NIH to build upon recommendations from the VCID
research milestones defined by ADRD Summit 2016. 5 In this report,
we provide a summary of the WG presentations, discussion, and its rec-
ommendations for future research. This report reflects the attendees’
perspectives on current understanding on VCID research, potential
mechanisms contributing to VCID, and research gaps as well as future
research opportunities, and is not meant to be a comprehensive review.
We include a limitation section at the end of the report to acknowl-
edge several important research areas that were not discussed in the
WG or, as a result, this report. Finally, for the purposes of this report,
we use the term VCID throughout to refer to “vascular contributions
to cognitive impairment and dementia,” as this has been the preferred
use of this term in recent years. This is distinct from specific clinical
syndromes, such as vascular cognitive impairment, and our use of the
term VCID referring to contributions rather than purely vascular syn-
dromes is felt to better represent the great deal of overlapping patholo-
gies seen, with a range of clinical and radiographic manifestations. In
addition, the term VCID allows inclusion of a broad range of vascular
mechanisms and phenotypes, as is appropriate for this heterogeneous
group of disorders. These range from monogenetic disorders leading to
a pure small vessel disease phenotype to cerebral amyloid angiopathy
to mixed pathologies, with many forms between. This report is focused
on common pathways and mechanisms that occur in VCID, as well as
research priorities for this heterogeneous group of disorders.
Dementia is a constellation of neurological and vascular disorders
that typically occur during aging and are characterized by cognitive
impairment severe enough to interfere with one’s daily function. While
AD is the most common dementia diagnosis, AD as a clinical syn-
drome displays multiple pathologies in more than half of clinically
diagnosed individuals. Emerging evidence suggests that most clinical
AD cases, especially those in persons over the age of 80, have both
classic AD pathology with plaques and tangles as well as vascular
pathology.6 VCID is defined as the neurovascular unit being confronted
with and failing to cope with biological insults, often as people age,
due to systemic and cerebral vascular disease, proteinopathy includ-
ing Alzheimer’s biology, metabolic disease, and/or immune response,
resulting in cognitive decline. 2 VCID research overlays multiple clin-
ical diagnoses, including cerebro- and cardiovascular disease, stroke,
AD and other types of dementia, some of which involve neither AD nor
stroke.3 Chronic systemic comorbidities and risk factors are also asso-
ciated with deleterious cognitive outcomes. These comorbidities and

2 CURRENT STATE OF KNOWLEDGE
The WG meeting included review of the existing state of knowledge
in the field of VCID, with a focus on epidemiologic and basic science
evidence supporting vascular contributions to dementias of aging. This
report represents the knowledge reviewed and synthesized by the
working group members, and thus is not a fully comprehensive review
covering all aspects of VCID. Furthermore, the format of the WG did
not incorporate public comment, and thus we acknowledge that this
document is a reflection of the material covered rather than a repre-
sentation of a broader interpretation of the existing literature.
2.1 Epidemiology of VCID
There is a large and growing body of epidemiological evidence suggest-
ing an association between cardio- and cerebrovascular risk factors
and disease; and cognitive impairment and dementia diagnoses. 17-19
While some risk factors may directly contribute to dementia, others
may have a more indirect impact, possibly through affecting risks for
cardio- and cerebrovascular diseases (either clinically apparent or qui-
escent). As the ability to diagnose “subclinical” cardio- or cerebrovascu-
lar diseases improves, the understanding of potential mechanisms link-
ing vascular risk factors and markers and dementia expands. However,
epidemiologic data clearly suggest that the potential impact of vascular
risk factors on dementia may not be entirely through easily measurable
vascular disease (such as stroke or small vessel disease or silent brain
infarcts), or at least as limited by current technology. Microvascular
dysfunction, measured using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as
well as biomarkers of microvascular disease, only accounted for 16.2%
of the effect of aortic stiffness on cognitive function, 20 white matter
hyperintensities (WMH) only partially mediated the effect of hyperten-
sion on cognition,21 and mediation analyses of other vascular risk fac-
tors have not found a clear imaging feature explaining the relationship
between risk factor and cognition. 22 As technology and biomarkers
of downstream vascular changes of these risk factors improves, more
will be understood about the full range of mechanisms at play relating
to these risk factors. In addition, it is also plausible that a vascular risk

Another important use of observational data is in evaluating trends
in populations over time. 17 The incidence of dementia in high-income
countries has declined for the past decades, which has occurred in par-
allel to the improvement of cardiovascular health, and it has been pos-
tulated that better prevention and management of vascular diseases
and risk factors might partially contribute to this decline.37,38 This does
not, however, mean a reduction in dementia prevalence, given the esti-
mated longer life span of individuals with dementia, and thus it has
been hypothesized that there will be a doubling of individuals with
ADRDs by 2060, compared to 2014.39
Despite the concerning rising prevalence of dementia, the reduction
in incidence may be informative and ultimately, if continued, would
lead to reductions in prevalence. Thus, a better understanding of
what factors contribute to the decline could provide insight into
possible prevention and intervention strategies. The SPRINT-MIND
(Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial-Memory and Cognition
in Decreased Hypertension) trial, completed after this workshop,
showed that intensive blood pressure control lowered risk for mild
cognitive impairment (MCI)—although this was not the study’s primary
outcome; no significant effect was found for dementia, the primary
outcome—strengthening the connection between vascular health and
brain health and providing momentum to examine whether targeting
other vascular risk factors or multiple risk factors could prevent
VCID.40 As further evidence that risk factors such as hypertension
do not act entirely through measurable brain lesions or brain dis-
ease, in the SPRINT-MIND MRI sub-substudy, white matter lesions
(WML), interpreted as small vessel disease, progressed less rapidly in
individuals treated with more aggressive blood pressure control with
statistically significant but relatively small effect size. At the follow-up
(median intervention period of 3.4 years and median follow-up time
of 3.97 years), the intensive treatment group had less increase in
WML compared to the standard treatment group yet the difference
between the two groups was small, with a mean between-group
difference for change in WML volume of −0.54 cm 3 (95% CI, −0.87
to −0.20 cm 3).40,41 The FINGER (Finnish Geriatric Intervention
Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability) trial demon-
strated that a multimodal intervention, which included both lifestyle
interventions as well as vascular risk factor control, across multiple
risk factors, was promising in reducing 2-year cognitive decline, 42
further demonstrating the potential for prevention by focusing on
vascular contributions. Further studies modeled after FINGER are
under way across the world, and will provide important information
on prevention of dementia through vascular risk. 43 As most of the
vascular risk factors are modifiable, preventable, and treatable, it is
attractive to target vascular contributors of dementia as potential
prevention and new treatment options. It is possible, however, that
not all preventive therapies should be applied equally to all individuals,
and intensive blood pressure control may be ideal in most but not
all adults, with remaining questions about ideal treatment targets
in the oldest old or individuals with especially high cerebrovascular
risk.
2.2 VCID pathophysiology
As stated above, vascular pathology frequently accompanies AD
pathology in clinical AD patients based on human autopsy findings,
especially in patients of older age. 44 Vascular disease and vascular tis-
sue injury, such as intracranial atherosclerosis, arteriolosclerosis, silent
infarcts, microinfarcts, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, and microbleeds,
have been shown to correlate with dementia symptoms, with less AD
pathology observed for an equivalent level of cognitive impairment
if vascular pathology is also present. 6 However, the mechanisms by
which these vascular pathologies may contribute to dementia are
not well understood. Potential mechanisms linking vascular function
to cognitive function and potential effects of vascular disorders on
cognitive impairment have been summarized in recent reviews. 3,45,46
Cerebrovascular abnormality is thought to occur early in the AD con-
tinuum, as depicted in the revised Jack model of AD biomarkers. 45,47

3 POTENTIAL MECHANISMS
3.1 Endothelial dysfunction
The cerebrovascular endothelium plays critically important roles in
regulation of CBF, BBB, blood coagulation, immune surveillance, and
trophic support of neuronal and glial cells. Historically, the concept
of “endothelial dysfunction” was articulated in the 1980s, shortly
after discovery of endothelium derived relaxing factor/nitric oxide
(NO).57-60 Loss of NO is considered to be a central mechanism
of endothelial dysfunction. 61 Indeed, major cardiovascular risk fac-
tors including hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, and aging
impair the production and/or biological activity of endothelial NO.
Relevant to this report, existing literature supports the notion that
endothelial dysfunction may contribute to initiation and development
of cognitive impairment through a variety of mechanisms. A study using
cultured human brain microvascular endothelial cells suggested that
inhibition of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) causes signifi-
cant upregulation of amyloid precursor protein (APP) as well as expres-
sion and activity of β-site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme
1.62 Further analysis of signaling mechanisms demonstrated that the
effects of NO on expression and processing of APP are mediated by
activation of soluble guanylate cyclase and increased production of
cyclic guanosine monophosphate (GMP). 62,63 In vivo validation and
extension of the results obtained in cultured human cerebrovascular
endothelium was performed in eNOS-null mice (Nos3 −/−). Consistent
with in vitro findings, elevated expression of APP , beta-secretase 1
(BACE1), and increased production of A β peptides were detected in
the brain parenchyma of eNOS-null mice. 62,64 Moreover, it was found
that endothelial NO deficiency instigates activation of microglia and
impairment of glycolytic function of astrocytes.65,68 Behavioral studies
revealed that eNOS-null mice suffer from cognitive impairment. 65,66
The importance of intact eNOS function in preservation of cognitive
function was also demonstrated in an experimental model of endothe-
lial dysfunction induced by a high salt diet. 69,70 While the exact mech-
anisms responsible for cognitive impairment induced by the loss of
endothelial NO are complex and incompletely understood, studies
from several groups indicate that besides aberrant processing of APP ,
inactivation of eNOS (by gene deletion or treatment with high salt diet)
also promotes phosphorylation of tau protein in neuronal cells. 67,70,71
Importantly, in mice exposed to a high salt diet, NO precursor, L-
arginine, suppressed phosphorylation of tau and prevented cognitive
dysfunction. In contrast, NO deficiency reduced neuronal calpain nitro-
sylation that in turn caused activation of cyclin-dependent kinase 5
and tau phosphorylation.71 These observations suggested that reduc-
tion in CBF (hypoperfusion) does not explain detrimental effects of
eNOS inactivation on phosphorylation of tau and cognitive function
and that endothelium-derived NO may diffuse into brain parenchyma
and exert direct effects on neuronal cells.70 Additionally, partial eNOS-
deficiency in aged (eNOS+/−) mice first leads to elevation of A β40 in
cerebral blood vessels rather than in the brain parenchyma,72 suggest-
ing that in this model, cerebrovascular alterations occur independently

3.2 Pericyte dysfunction
Pericytes are contractile cells located between brain capillary endothe-
lial cells (ECs), astrocytes, and neurons. 76 Their unique position sug-
gests that pericytes may regulate key neurovascular functions of the
brain, which has been supported by recent findings. Pericyte loss
has been shown to reduce CBF and brain capillary perfusion, and
diminish global and individual capillary responses to neuronal stimuli,
which results in neurovascular uncoupling and reduced oxygen sup-
ply to the brain and metabolic stress. 77 Furthermore, pericyte loss
also has been demonstrated to cause BBB breakdown and accumula-
tion of blood-derived toxin proteins in the brain. 78 Rapid loss of peri-
cytes could also lead to loss of pericyte-derived neurotrophic growth
factors pleiotrophin (PTN) and acute circulatory collapse. Notably,
it has been reported that intracerebroventricular PTN infusion pre-
vented neuron loss in pericyte-ablated mice despite persistent circu-
latory changes; thus, suggesting that restoration of pericyte function
might be a potential therapeutic option. 79 Not surprisingly, disrupted
white-matter microcirculation occurs before neuronal loss in pericyte-
deficient mice, suggesting that pericytes play an important role in reg-
ulating white-matter structure and function. A recent study showed
that A β oligomers can constrict capillaries both in humans and mice
specifically at pericyte locations, but not in arterioles and venules, 80
highlighting the crucial role pericytes play in NVU and pericytes as an
important mediator of A β oligomer-induced vascular damage. Inter-
estingly, BBB breakdown and elevated CSF levels of pericyte injury
marker sPDGFRβ (soluble platelet derived growth factor receptor- β)
predicted cognitive decline in apolipoprotein E (APOE)4 (ε3/ε4o r ε4/ε4)
carriers independently of AD Aβ and tau pathology in a recent study.81
Consistent with data from the mouse, 82 this study found that APOE4
(ε3/ε4o r ε4/ε4) carriers have elevated CSF levels of proinflammatory
cyclophilin A (CypA) and matrix metalloprotease-9 (MMP9) suggest-
ing activation of this BBB-degrading pathway by APOE4 gene, and
that pericytes derived from APOE (ε4/ε4) human induced pluripotent
stem cells had higher levels of CypA and secreted MMP9 than those
derived from APOE (ε3/ε3), implicating that pericyte function may be
affected by APOE genotype.81 In addition to pericytes, there is evi-
dence that brain perivascular macrophages also affect BBB permeabil-
ity and other aspects of neurovascular regulation.83,84
3.3 Hypertension
Several lines of investigation are converging to suggest blood
pressure (BP) control may prevent dementia and associated
neuropathology.85,86 Indeed hypertension and diabetes are rec-
ognized as the strongest modifiable risk factors for AD/ADRD. 87 A
growing volume of observational studies show associations between
vascular risk factors (eg, hypertension and arterial stiffness) and evi-
dence of AD pathology (eg, Aβ and tau).88,89 It is important to note that
measured BP is a complex phenotype bolstered by autonomic factors,
peripheral resistance, and structural changes in the vasculature. While
associative findings do not prove causality, there is direct evidence
in animals and humans indicating that better cardiovascular health
(including lower BP) is advantageous for brain health. 90-92 Results
from the SPRINT MIND clinical trial demonstrated that intensive BP
control (systolic BP target of ≤120 mm Hg vs 140 mm Hg) may reduce
the risk of MCI, which is often a prodromal phase of dementia. 40
Finally, specific medications used to control hypertension have been
hypothesized to have beneficial cognitive effects, beyond an effect of
reducing cognitive decline purely by reducing blood pressure. This may
be particularly true for drugs active on the renin-angiotensin system,
such as angiotensin II receptor blockers and angiotensin converting
enzyme (ACE) inhibitors. 93 These drugs may have additional effects
beyond those acting on blood pressure, including anti-inflammatory
effects and possible direct impacts on A β degradation.94,95 Recent
meta-analyses, however, do not show a clear antihypertensive drug
class effect for dementia prevention.96,97
Recent experiments in mice have emphasized that immune modi-
fication by ACE can greatly affect the pathology observed in models
of AD. Specifically, experiments with ACE 10/10 mice, a strain that
overexpresses ACE in monocytes and macrophages, showed that
such mice have a more effective immune response when challenged
with either tumors or models of infection. 98 These mice were crossed
with a mouse model of AD and compared to AD mice with wild type
expression of ACE.99 The animals overexpressing ACE in macrophages
had a marked and highly significant reduction in cortical amyloid
plaque area, reduced astrogliosis, reduced soluble A β levels, and
retention of cognitive function. These data emphasize that ACE-
mediated enhancement of the immune response may be effective
at reducing AD-associated neuroinflammation and A β pathology.
They also emphasize how important it is to understand the effects on
AD of all drugs used to treat hypertension and other cardiovascular
problems. Finally, these data suggest that it may be useful to consider
inflammation as not just the number of inflammatory cells or even the
types of cells comprising the infiltrate, but rather by some measure of
effectiveness of the inflammation in clearing the underlying biological
insult.

3.4 Atherosclerosis
An examination of temporal trends in the age-specific incidence of
dementia in the Framingham Heart Study and other cohorts showed
that the age-specific incidence of dementia has declined over the past
three decades. 100 The parallel improvement in several vascular risk
factors (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and smoking) raised the possi-
bility that this decline might be attributable to reduced vascular risk
factors; however, adjusting for vascular risk factors at midlife or after
age 65 did not substantially alter the trend. 101 Atherosclerotic car-
diovascular events such as stroke and myocardial infarcts, however,
did partially explain the temporal trend observed. This apparent con-
tradiction might be explained by considering subclinical and clinical
manifestations of atherosclerosis as markers of accumulated exposure
to vascular risk factors over a lifetime, or by considering the impact
of atherosclerosis on brain, cardiac, and other organ structure and
function.102
The extent of systemic atherosclerosis tends to be correlated across
various arterial beds and can be readily quantified. Cerebral or sys-
temic atherosclerosis has been related to MRI evidence of structural
brain injury and poorer cognition in many cohort studies of community-
living men and women, across various race and ethnic groups. 103-106
These effects seem to begin as early as the third and fourth decades
of life and the adverse impact of early atherosclerosis on brain struc-
ture and function seems even greater than the effect of atherosclerosis
later in life,107-111 emphasizing that VCID accrue throughout life; that
is, dementia in at least some instances is a life course disease.
Atherosclerosis may adversely impact brain structure and function
through multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. The atheroma can
directly alter the stiffness of the aorta and cerebral vasculature; arte-
rial stiffness assessed as carotid-femoral pulse wave velocity has been
associated with smaller brain volumes, increased volume of abnor-
mal white matter, and microstructural injury within apparently nor-
mal looking white matter. 107,112-114 It could also impair cerebral cir-
culation at rest or under stress even at subclinical degrees of carotid
stenosis.115 Coronary atherosclerosis and myocardial infarction, per-
haps through ischemic cardiomyopathy, can lower cardiac index and
hence cerebral perfusion 116,117 and has also been associated with
overt and covert cardioembolic brain infarctions, 118 either directly or
due to shared risk and atherosclerosis in extra- or intracranial vascular
beds paralleling coronary disease.
Atherosclerosis may also be viewed as a paradigm that offers valu-
able lessons for understanding VCID. Like atherosclerosis, VCID may
begin early in life. Adherence to the American Heart Association Life’s
Simple 7 optimal lifestyle characteristics reduces risks, not only of
atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease events, but also of cognitive
decline and dementia and like atherosclerosis many genetic and bio-
logical pathways, including inflammation, oxidative stress,s and mito-
chondrial dysfunction, seem to underlie most dementia. 119,120 In fact,
common genetic pathology has been shown to underlie both cardio-
vascular disease and dementia and both conditions share many cir-
culating biomarkers such as homocysteine, leptin, clusterin, YKL −40,
CD14, miRNA 132, BNP , ST2, GDF-15, troponin, VEGF, IGF1, CRP ,
IL6, TNF-alpha, and markers of the NO pathway. Thus, exploring com-
mon biological pathways underlying both atherosclerosis and cogni-
tive decline could improve understanding of VCID. It is less clear if
atherosclerosis, whether systemic or intracranial, is found in asso-
ciation with AD-specific neuropathologic changes. Although autopsy
studies have reported associations between circle of Willis atheroscle-
rosis and AD, 121 this association has not been found consistently in
studies using biomarkers of AD neuropathology in living subjects, with
no association between intracranial atherosclerosis and amyloid by
PET in nondemented participants from U.S. communities.122
Importantly, the heart is not a surrogate for the brain when studying
the contributions of atherosclerosis in VCID; therefore, careful stud-
ies to explore the specific dose-, age-, and gene-specific interactions in
the impact of atherosclerosis and vascular risk factors on the brain are
needed.123,124 For instance, theAPOE4 allele appears to potentiate the
adverse impact of a variety of vascular risk factors on the brain.125,126
3.5 Diabetes
Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a growing public health problem affecting hun-
dreds of millions of people globally. Economic forces driving lifestyle
changes in physical activity and diet are contributing to the global
growth of diabetes and related disease burden. Diabetes affects both
small and large blood vessels that can lead to multiple end organ dam-
age, including the nervous system. 127 In humans and non-humans,
T2D is associated with changes in BBB permeability 128,129 that are
thought to contribute to cognitive impairment. 52 Secondary inflam-
mation that occurs in diabetes is also thought to further contribute
to nervous system damage. T2D is associated with lower cortical vol-
umes on MRI,130 lower cognitive functioning,131 cognitive decline, and
ADRD;132,133 however, mixed findings have been reported and mecha-
nisms for observed neurocognitive dysfunction remain unclear.134 T2D
may be a more salient risk factor for non-AD pathology than for AD
pathology, at least based on evidence from post mortem studies.135,136
Common co-occurring factors such as obesity, diet, and physical activ-
ity also appear to be associated with neurocognitive dysfunction, which
further complicates the relationship between T2D and dementia. 86
Future research efforts should clarify the independent and interacting
risks and causes that lead to both T2D and dementia to explain, and
hopefully leverage, the link between these devastating disorders.

4.1 Animal models
Previous reviews have summarized existing VCID animal models and
remaining needs for animal models that recapitulate the human VCID
disease spectrum. 155,57 In this section, we will focus on models that
mimic chronic cerebral hypoperfusion (CCH) as an example of VCID
model. The increasing evidence from autopsy studies indicating that
AD is due to mixed vascular and degenerative pathologies substanti-
ates the need for adequate animal models that dissect the underlying
mechanisms and to evaluate novel intervention strategies. Several car-
diovascular risk factors (eg, hypertension, HHcy, and diabetes mellitus
[DM]) have been associated with AD development. 156,157 All of these
risk factors can lead to CCH, which has been repeatedly linked to AD
pathogenesis. T o establish causal and temporal relationships several
in vivo studies in mice have been conducted, which do indeed show
that hypertension, DM, and HHcy have a similar impact on AD-related
pathology and cognitive performance, which may be associated with
cerebrovascular dysfunction.158-160 It is noteworthy that the number
of mouse studies reviewing these risk factors in AD transgenic mice is
limited and causal data are still rare. Because cerebral hemodynamic
changes were not assessed in these animals, no data are available to
indicate whether the aggravating effect of the vascular risk factors
studies might be mediated by CCH. There is quite good evidence from
mouse studies that indicates a role for hypoxia, induced by reduced
cerebral flow, in AD neuropathology and reduced cognitive function.
It is thought that hypoxia particularly stimulates APP processing and
Aβ production in ECs and neighboring neurons, promoting CAA gener-
ation and vascular dysfunction.161
Cardiovascular risk factors and cerebral flow disturbances have an
accelerating and/or aggravating effect on AD brain pathology and cog-
nitive decline in combined mouse models of vascular disease and AD.
Based on current literature, we conclude that studies so far have not
yet succeeded in establishing whether CCH plays a causative or coin-
cidental role in AD disease initiation and/or progression. Underlying
molecular mechanisms have not been clarified nor have therapeutic
interventions been successful.
4.2 Engineered human tissues
How vascular risk factors affect dementia is not understood and dif-
ficult to model in animals, as mice differ from humans in many ways,
including Aβ clearance rate, APOE isoforms, lipoprotein profile, and the
immune system.162 It is nearly impossible to fully recapitulate the many
factors that contribute to AD in mice, and our reliance on mouse mod-
els may account for a significant proportion of the failure rate in trans-
lating animal study results into successful clinical trials. Given that clin-
ical trials targeting the central nervous system (CNS) inherently have
exceptionally high failure rates, it is imperative that alternative transla-
tional platforms with greater predictive value be developed and imple-
mented for drug development. Current innovations across the health
and engineering sectors are now yielding new platforms based upon
the field of tissue engineering, where individual cellular components
are combined together in 3-dimensional (3D) systems to generate func-
tional tissue. Several new platforms are now available, mostly focused
on the NVU that is composed of EC, smooth muscle cells (SMCs), peri-
cytes, astrocytes, and neurons. Recent advances using induced pluripo-
tent stem cells (iPSC)-derived neurons to form “brain in a dish” 3D cul-
tures promise to fill an important niche between humans and animal
models toward deciphering regulatory pathways and identifying thera-
peutic avenues for neurologic and psychiatric diseases.163,164 The most
advanced model of human CAA to date was created using scaffold-
directed 3D bioengineered vessels consisting of primary human ECs
and human SMCs, cultured either without primary human astrocytes
to mimic leptomeningeal arteries or with primary human astrocytes
to mimic penetrating arteries.165,166 The engineered vessel contains a
tight BBB that separates “brain” from “blood” circulating through the
vessel lumen. Importantly, Aβ originating from the “brain” side crosses
the engineered BBB into the “blood” side and both the rate of trans-
port and accumulation in the vessel wall can be quantified. Further,
the model recapitulates the well-established genetic effects of APOE
on Aβ transport and accumulation. Suitability of this model for use in
drug development was validated by investigating the beneficial effects
of high density lipoprotein (HDL), which showed that HDL isolated
from young healthy donors attenuated Aβ-mediated EC activation and
reduced CAA in the engineered vascular wall independent of APOE
isoform.165,166 These results show the promise of engineered human
tissues as an experimental platform to investigate VCID mechanisms.

should be considered in future studies because it can demonstrate the
dynamic temporal relationship between vascular health and cognitive
impairment.
4.4 Biomarkers for VCID
As the NI) developed research priorities for the ADRD (specified as
frontotemporal, Lewy body, vascular, and mixed dementia), one consis-
tent recommendation emerged across all dementia types: to identify
and validate biomarkers.87 The rationale is that biomarkers can bridge
the gap between preclinical models and human disease, providing
windows on individual pathophysiologic processes that ultimately lead
to clinical impairment. Biomarkers are also key to early phase clinical
trials of novel or existing treatment strategies, facilitating patient
selection and risk stratification and providing actionable information
on an intervention’s target engagement, mechanism of action, and
potential safety and efficacy.
NIH’s response for the VCID field was to create the MarkVCID
consortium (https://markvcid.partners.org/w).180 Comprised of seven
participating sites, a coordinating center, external advisory commit-
tee, and NIH leadership, MarkVCID’s charge is to validate a suite of
biomarkers for small vessel related VCID to the point of readiness for
multi-center clinical trials. This mission has dictated MarkVCID’s over-
all approach of focusing on biomarkers that are reproducible across
sites and linked closely enough to key biological processes underly-
ing cSVD to be useful for patient selection, risk stratification, or out-
come assessment in a trial. An important corollary is that different
VCID stages may require different biomarkers, such as markers of small
vessel dysfunction in at-risk asymptomatic patients versus markers of
white matter injury in symptomatic disease.
MarkVCID recently concluded its initial 2-year phase of harmoniz-
ing methods for clinical and cognitive assessment, neuroimaging, and
fluid handling across the sites and implementing consent procedures
to allow open sharing of samples and data. Each site has proposed can-
didate biomarkers that were reviewed by the coordinating center and
external advisory committee and ultimately evaluated by NIH leader-
ship for readiness and scientific priority. The remainder of MarkVCID’s
efforts will focus on independently validating the prioritized biomark-
ers (T able3) across multiple consortium sites; thus, firmly establishing
their availability for use in future interventional trials.
4.5 Pathology markers for VCID
Further human pathologic studies are vital to advance knowledge of
VCID. The mechanism of cognitive impairment from many vascular
pathologies remains unclear and there is currently no animal model
that mirrors the human condition. It is important to note that most
neuropathology sampling schemes in aging studies focus on regions
involved in neurodegenerative disease, yet vascular pathologies are
often microscopic and multifocal so the presence as well as extent of
vascular pathology is likely under-recognized in aging literature. For
instance, microscopy of the aged human brain often shows a limited
number of microinfarcts. Yet, just a few microinfarcts found at autopsy
may indicate hundreds if not thousands in the whole brain, 181 but
even then it remains unclear if this amount of tissue loss fully explains
cognitive impairment from microinfarcts. 182,183 Indeed, in the human
brain, there are both direct and indirect effects from vessel pathology
and vascular tissue injury. 184,185 Cellular and molecular mechanisms
downstream of these vessel diseases and associated with tissue injury
should be explored.186 These downstream processes may include vari-
ations in inflammatory processes, clearance of waste, and interactions
with other neurodegenerative processes. Vascular mechanisms may be
focused on white matter187 and/or the cortical regions of the brain,188
and regional mechanisms could also differ. Indeed, watershed regions
of the human brain appear especially important. 189 Linking vascular

studies

ally focused on cognitive testing and generalized cognitive phenotypes
with limited assessments of neuropathology and subclinical vascular
biomarkers. VCID can affect any and multiple cognitive domains and
is most commonly associated with impairment in executive function,
speed of processing, and attention. 195-197 Standard screening instru-
ments for global cognitive performance show strong discrimination
for VCID at mild and severe impairment relative to unimpaired
individuals,198 but more work is needed. Development of cognitive
batteries sensitive and specific to VCID declines may be needed to
untangle VCID and mixed pathologies from forms of dementia without
VCID. Longitudinal cognitive function remains the most clinically
meaningful endpoint in research studies; yet, there are inherent
limitations to relying solely on cognition. Cognition is proposed to be

phenomena that co-occur in most cases of dementia, it is essential that

4.7 Evaluation of life-course exposure and its
impact
Epidemiologic studies have contributed key insights into the associa-
tion between cardiovascular risk factors and risk of cognitive impair-
ment and dementia over the life course. Such studies have consis-
tently demonstrated that mid-life cardiovascular factors, 17,34,213,214
including diabetes,215-217 blood pressure/hypertension,31,32,36,218 and
obesity33,123,219-221 increase risk of late-life cognitive impairment and
dementia. In contrast, results for late-life cardiovascular factors have
been less clear with some studies finding weaker associations or in
the case of high blood pressure and obesity, converse associations in
which late-life cardiovascular factors were associated with lower risk
of adverse cognitive outcomes. 123,215,219-221 T h et r u er o l eo fl a t e - l i f e
risk factors on cognition can be hard to evaluate, given concerns about
reverse causation (in which changes in blood pressure and weight, for
example, may be secondary to cognitive impairment, for example, or
at least indicate concurrent disability which could contribute to risk
of dementia),221,222 rather than themselves causing cognitive decline.
Furthermore, survival bias reduces the likelihood that individuals with
markedly abnormal vascular risk factors in mid life survive to late life,
both limiting their ability to develop dementia, 223 and reducing the
likelihood of these risk factors remaining in the very elevated range in
older adulthood.
Evidence is now emerging that establishes the importance of car-
diovascular risk factors for cognitive outcomes even earlier in the life
course. In the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults
Study, cardiovascular factors beginning from early adulthood, 120
including systolic and diastolic blood pressure, glucose, subclinical
atherosclerotic calcification, and heart rate variability, were associ-
ated with cognitive outcomes at mid life. 120,224-229 In the Young Finns
Study, childhood cardiovascular factors have also been linked to mid-
life cognitive function even after adjusting for mid-life cardiovascular
exposures.230 Assessment of vascular risk factors over time also allows
for evaluation of a range of cardiovascular risk factor characteristics
including clinical as well as subclinical risk factors levels, 120,224 cumu-
lative exposure, 224,230 trajectories, and changes or variability over
time.226,228
Critical questions remain regarding the role of vascular risk factors
over the life course, particularly the mechanistic pathways by which
cardiovascular risk factors influence cognitive outcomes at each life
stage, the link between mid-life cognitive changes and the develop-
ment of late-life vascular cognitive impairment and dementia, and the
effects of treatment timing on prevention/delay of cognitive impair-
ment. In addition to traditional observational studies, researchers are
also leveraging the power of existing epidemiological studies and com-
bining data across cohorts and life course stages to address these cru-
cial questions.
4.8 Assessment of environmental and social
factors
In the accompanying sections, we have described the cerebrovascu-
lar and cardiovascular disease (CVD) risks, biomarkers, and pathologic
markers of VCID. In addition, it is important to recognize that our
environment, communities, and socioeconomic conditions impact our
vascular, neurologic health, and disease. 168 The known environmen-
tal and sociocultural factors associated with CVD and stroke can also
be assessed as contributors to VCID. Arguably, the constant and high
demands for blood supply of brain tissue makes it exquisitely sensi-
tive to acute and chronic blood flow disruptions that are known to
affect cognitive function and ADRD. 231 Environmental factors, such
as air pollution, are recognized risks shared by CVD and dementia. 232

accessible and facilitate lifespan studies of when and how vascular
health and disease impact cognitive health and diseases. In terms of
public health, these available data resources can guide study of optimal
adult lifespan time points for developing targeted disease prevention
and therapeutic interventions for reducing VCID.
4.9 Future clinical trials
It is increasingly clear that ADRD, like many biologic processes, is not
the result of a solitary factor but instead due to a complex, multi-step
combination of physiologic events. Incident ADRD is associated
with vascular, neurologic, metabolic, and inflammatory signals and
responses.44,234 However, clinical trials to date have typically tested
interventions based on the basic science observations focused on the
maintenance of neuronal cell function and prevention of neuronal
death as a pathway for reducing ADRD burden. More recently, the
emergence of physiologic evidence supporting the diverse pathways
involved in the process of ADRD onset has broadened the opportunity
for developing clinical trials focused on additional targets, including
vascular targets.235 In addition, the recent imaging results from clinical
trials such as ACCORD (Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in
Diabetes) and SPRINT-MIND indicate that interventions which reduce
CVD risk also reduce the rate of abnormal white matter disease
formation, providing imaging evidence for the biologic basis of an
intervention and an effective surrogate outcome for efficacy. 40,236
Although no significant difference in the primary outcome, dementia,
was found with more aggressive blood pressure control, SPRINT-
MIND made an additional important advance in the field by showing
the feasibility of including adjudicated MCI as a clinical trial outcome
and the need for composite outcomes (MCI and dementia) when
event rates are low, thus improving the power and sample size metrics
needed to test future interventions. These and other results highlight
the need for additional investment in understanding the basic mech-
anism for the efficacy of cardiovascular disease prevention therapies,
such as improved blood pressure control, on lowering the risk for MCI
and ADRD. Animal models from rodents to non-human primates are
critical to inform the epidemiologic and clinical trial observations in
humans as a foundation for the design of additional definitive clinical
trials focused on impacting vascular mechanisms in ADRD.
4.10 Limitations of the convened workgroup and
this report
This report summarized the topics that were discussed at the work-
shop and is not a comprehensive review of the state of the field. We
acknowledge that several important topics and contributors to VCID
were not discussed at the workshop and thus not included in this
report, but were included in the 2019 ADRD summit report. These
topics include, but are not limited to, the following: the role of psy-
chosocial factors, including stress, depression, and mental health; sleep
and sleep disorders; and importantly, social determinants of health and
their impact on cognitive outcomes as they relate to VCID. In addition,
several forms of VCID and possible mechanisms were not discussed
or were only discussed minimally at the WG, and thus not included in
this report. These include (among others): cerebral amyloid angiopa-
thy as a form of VCID, its subtypes, and its overlap with AD; the role of
perivascular and glymphatic drainage in VCID development or progres-
sion; and overlapping pathology with cardiac disease including the pre-
viously described finding of cardiomyocyte inclusions in some dement-
ing illnesses. Some of these topics represent important research priori-
ties and opportunities; we discuss below those opportunities that were
specifically discussed at the WG, but acknowledge the large number of
additional relevant areas important for future study.
